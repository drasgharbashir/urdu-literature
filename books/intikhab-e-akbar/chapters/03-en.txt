# 2. God knows who said it, on what day, with a sound mind

God knows who said it, on what day, with a sound mind,
that the East can see no escape from the West:

"The world is gone; then why should we cling to the faith now?
This bundle of religious questions feels a heavy load.

Religious restrictions are harmful; it is right to break them,
but these *maulvis* (Muslim scholars) stand in the way, and there is no help for them.

Sprinkle them with water by philosophical methods,
so that this ember of religion goes out and turns to ash.

Let the scissors of policy work by such intricate methods
that the root of religion is cut and the whole house falls down.

Let practice vanish altogether, let only the words remain,
and let the kettledrum of Western wisdom bring even those low.

Then your nation will prosper again in the turning of the heavens;
what wonder if the stream of good fortune flows again?"

National progress worked a doomsday in the Muslim's ear;
he began to say, "What a blessing, if it should come our way!"

"If that beauty of the West takes my heart in her hand,
for her drunken eye I would give away rosary and prayer-mat." [TN: Here and below Akbar weaves in lines of Hafiz, the 14th-century Persian poet, changing a few words. This couplet parodies the opening of Hafiz's famous ghazal: "If that Turk of Shiraz takes my heart in her hand, for her Indian mole I would give Samarkand and Bukhara." Akbar's Persian: "If that beauty of the West takes my heart in her hand, for her drunken eye I would give away rosary and prayer-mat."]

In short, the worshipper of the East folded up his prayer-mat and rose,
and with the strength that had come into his heart, he shouted a challenge.

Here writing, there a speech, here intrigue, there a ban:
whom he scolded, whom he rebuked, whom he won over, whom he struck!

When does the man of passion look to the results?
He thought, "I shall become the Darius of a new nation."

A two-day policy gave him strength from that side;
then on this side the kettledrum of victory and triumph began to sound.

[illegible]
those tresses that spread a drunken scent of ambergris everywhere.

To escape the snare of the outward senses became hard;
what were imagined houris beside the sight of fairies?

They broke, they fell, they slipped; then love came to them;
no strength stayed in their prayers, no patience in their hearts.

The pleasure-loving companions struck up the lute of delight,
and all played "dadra-dara" with the plectrum of desire.

Lost in the love of idols, stones had already fallen on their wits;
[illegible] the mercury of affectation rose over every heart.

What was the standing of the poor, the grieving, the helpless, the narrow-hearted?
Such a state had come that the nation was to be the loser.

The age paid no heed to Hali's prayers,
and the self-adorning friends did not fear Akbar's wit.

The silent cry of the people of dignity was this:
"O Guardian of the world's order, O Possessor of the Throne!

Alas for these bewitching, captivating mistresses, bane of the faith!
They have carried off patience from the heart as the Turks carry off the plunder-tray." [TN: The second line of this Persian couplet is Hafiz's; his first line laments "these saucy, sweet-working gypsy girls, who stir the city"; Akbar has made them "bewitching mistresses (misses), bane of the faith".]

Everyone was amazed: why had these conditions arisen?
It had not been the Sayyid's intention that the stream should run this way.

He was a great supporter of purdah, an upholder of obedience;
he wished the star of Islam to shine at its height.

When the dome of religion broke as easily as a bubble,
what came of the fortune and honour that should have come from that side?

We heard everything; but in the end, when we looked, what did we see?
The same bricks, the same stones, the same lime, the same mud.

Here we go on breaking the binding of the nation;
there the game is our rivals', every throw of theirs a winning one.

We have seen the results with our own eyes in this enlightened age:
the heavens have dashed the rebellious down on the dust of failure.

Does anyone anywhere teach contempt for religion?
When has the star of fortune shone after the light of the heart was put out?

There is much heedlessness and neglect of practice in the world, granted;
but belief is the root, and that should be held dear.

Goodwill does not hinge on giving up religion at all;
everyone has professed English loyalty from the heart.

It was not Sarah's intention that Ishmael should take pride in it;
let the manner not be hostile, that was the whole intention. [TN: The reading of this couplet is uncertain; the allusion appears to be to Sarah, Hagar and Ishmael.]

What will happen when we forget our own history?
For God's sake, if only you would take one look at this scene!

Prayer now without ablution, and on this side the mosque weeps;
here the heart has made the Quran into a mere section, read without desire.

The machines are running and no one else can have his way;
on this side are blunt butts, on that side a lightning-swift saw.

What does it mean, to despise one's own nation?
What magic has made the children self-regarding and self-adorning?

In one place ignorant children, in another powerless old men;
these flounder, and that poor fellow comes in for the speeches.

Why are these moral, these spiritual foundations breaking?
Why has the commanding self overcome the soul at peace? [TN: Two stages of the self in Islamic ethics, from the Quran: the nafs-e ammara, "the self that commands to evil", and the nafs-e mutmainna, "the soul at peace".]

Of what machine will they become parts, having lost their community?
Though, yes, some *banjara* (itinerant grain-carrier) may mix them in among his oxen.

If our rulers are zealous at worship in church,
why should we, the subjects, wander astray in the desert of irreligion?

Action is needed, no doubt; but why should we lose our light?
The age is turning; we are not planets, we are the fixed stars.

"He is the First, He is the Last": this is honey that nourishes the soul;
[illegible: the remainder of this line is uncertain]

Why is this life-giving image not fixed upon the heart,
that the boy may be a star of the highest heaven in spiritual progress?

Though the elders of the nation worry about this day and night,
these waves are weak, and on this side runs the current of heedlessness.

I was ready to set out these intricate arguments
when at that moment the honoured Hafiz cried out:

"Talk of the minstrel and wine, and seek less after the secret of time,
for no one has solved, and no one will solve, this riddle by philosophy."

# 3. If I keep to the old ways, Akbar

If I keep to the old ways, Akbar,
the Sayyid says plainly, "This colour is dingy."

If I adopt the new style,
my own nation raises a cry and a wail.

If you speak of moderation, that is neither here nor there;
everyone has stretched his feet out beyond the limit.

On this side the insistence that you may not even touch a lavender-water bottle;
on that side the craze: "Cupbearer, bring the flask of wine!"

On this side the register of policy and expediency is impure;
on that side is the post-bag carrying the revelation from Vilayat. [TN: Vilayat, "abroad", in Indian usage meant England. The word also means sainthood, which makes the "revelation" a pun.]

In short, the soul of Majnun suffers a twofold torment:
the affliction of Laila's company and of Laila's absence.

# 4. These rosaries and cries of "God is great", these praises and prayers

These rosaries and cries of "God is great", these praises and prayers
are all the pride of the hearts of God's servants.

These white soldiers of the regiment every Sunday
adorn the court of the church.

If you say they are utter savages,
then see how devout Lord [illegible] is.

When Edward the Seventh fell ill,
the nation remembered the Lord of Majesty.

The State spared no expense;
prayers were held in church with great pomp.

Those generals at whom the earth used to tremble
are in church, "bowing with those who bow".

When the war made them wretched and anxious,
they fell prostrate before God the Pure.

"The heads of proud, high-necked kings
lie on the ground of supplication at His court." [TN: "Bowing with those who bow" is Quran 2:43. The last couplet is Persian.]

# 5. My companion says, never mind if religion has gone

My companion says, "Never mind if religion has gone."
I say, "Brother, if this is gone, everything has gone."

National feeling we never had in the first place;
only unity of faith had remained, and now that has gone.

Beliefs surely affect human character;
what will there be in their place when that influence has been crushed?

Food in the belly, a few half-formed questions on the tongue:
the meaning of nation is gone, and the purpose of the soul has gone.

The students' courses are constantly being overturned;
even the course has departed, once [illegible] has gone.

Their inner unity exists in name only;
when have you seen a whole group go one way together?

Think, friends, what fate will follow this:
whoever rose up, the leader of community and creed, has gone.

[illegible]
alas, the thought of Mecca and Yathrib has gone from the Muslim.

In the assembly of the world, what rank will you deserve?
[illegible]

In the matter of service the old policy no longer holds;
wake up, the colour of the age of skill and trick is gone.

This is all we say, sir: think of how it will end;
what other refuge is there, if religion has gone?

# 6. When a certain Mafa'ilun brought a Chinese doll home from London as his bride

When a certain Mafa'ilun brought a Chinese doll home from London as his bride,
his friends wounded his heart with arrows of reproach.

His father said, "By God, he has sunk my ship! What a calamity!
In bad company this boy has done the deed of Noah's son.

I sent him for his education, and he went and settled on a marriage;
my son forgot whom he should praise, and only got himself married." [TN: A play on Arabic word-forms: mamduh, "the one praised", and mankuh, "the one married".]

The boy replied, "Revered sir, only listen a little:
what wrong have I done, if I have made the conqueror the conquered?" [TN: "Mafa'ilun" is a metrical foot of Urdu and Persian prosody, used here as a mock name, like "So-and-so".]

# 7. The self-selling misses have sent these afflictions at last

The self-selling misses have sent these afflictions at last;
they asked for so much gold that blood fell into our hearts.

The joy of our spirits was upset, the colour of our gatherings broken.
"Ho, cupbearer, pass round the cup and offer it,

for love seemed easy at first, but then came difficulties." [TN: This Persian piece is built on the opening couplet of the first ghazal of Hafiz's Divan, "Ala ya ayyuha's-saqi…"; the Arabic half-line means "Ho, cupbearer, pass round the cup and offer it".]

# 8. On this side the learning of the faith is lightless, belief gone from the heart

On this side the learning of the faith is lightless, belief gone from the heart;
on that side the heart is set on getting the college boat across.

On this side service is hard to get, and from idleness one begs;
"A dark night, the fear of the wave, and such a whirlpool in the way:

what do the lightly laden on the shores know of our state?"

No restraint of the sacred law is left, and freedom has no limit;
there is no discussion of whether this is right or wrong.

Even the elders give the ruling: "Read the code of Sir Sayyid![1]
Stain your prayer-mat with wine, if the Elder of the Magi tells you,

for the traveller on the path is not ignorant of the ways and customs of the stages."

What foresight was there, when the mind itself was not present?
We dwelt in the temple; the company of the infidel idol was charming;

we had no regard for faith, only the heart's pleasure in view.
"All my affairs, through self-will, have ended in ill fame at last;

how can that secret stay hidden, about which they make gatherings?"

If you would become the full moon, become the new moon, Hafiz;
do not rest; be swift on the road of seeking, Hafiz;

keep your flame fixed on Him day and night, Hafiz.
"If you want His presence, do not be heedless of Him, Hafiz:

when you meet the one you love, leave the world and let it go." [TN: The fifth line of each stanza (and in one case the fourth and fifth) is taken from the same Hafiz ghazal; the last half-line is Arabic.]

# 9. The word "God" is a mirror of hundreds of meanings

The word "God" is a mirror of hundreds of meanings,
and among them it is a synonym for "unknown".

Here everyone's share is suited to their state;
that, kind friend, is just what "destiny" means.

# 10. The spiritual guide has bred childishness in the nation

The spiritual guide has bred childishness in the nation;
he thought that the bloom of youth would be bred.

That was not bred at the boys' hands, but
holes began to be bred in the walls of the faith.

When the days of the nation's decline came, Akbar,
enemies of reason were bred in the highest ranks.

What is religion? Only the binding that holds the nation together,
from which every fair form of the community is bred.

Today they do not feel its harm;
a few tulips and [illegible] are still being bred.

Surely a time will come upon this garden
when the paths will burn, and wild rose and lily [TN: reading uncertain]

will wander, flying like leaves that have seen autumn;
no spring will come again, no garden will be bred.

When the sense of honour has left the father's blood,
the children too will be bred shameless and dull.

Religious practices will blow away like straw,
and heaps of discord will be bred.

The darkness of ignorance will close in round the heart;
no enlightened hearts can be bred in such breasts.

Who says you should not be loyal to the English from the heart?
Who says you should not breed agriculture and skills?

Who says you should not live in comfort and refinement?
Who says you should not breed a bloom in your style?

Who says you should not study learning, should not acquire reason?
Who says you should not breed a longing for London?

I only say: of the community [illegible];
do not yourself be bred a robber on the nation's road.

"Nation, nation" we hear all day and night; where is the nation?
No thread is left, and you are breeding a robe's hem.

Only the religious bond is your national existence;
if this breaks, no nest will be bred.

In some homes it is still possible that boys may be raised
with the [illegible] that is bred naturally.

this [illegible] will not be bred again.

They will be altogether outside the bounds of civilisation;
no feeling will remain for a lament to be bred. [TN: Every couplet of this ghazal ends with paida, "born, produced, bred"; the English repeats "bred" to keep the refrain.]

# 11. No doubt, rather than the New Light, it is far better

No doubt, rather than the New Light, it is far better
for a man to become a Christian.

That faith at least reminds him of God;
to become Ahriman is open unbelief.

The guide says, "You are ignorant, friend;
it is another thing to become a man of eloquence."

My moves too are a prelude to it;
first they teach you to become mouthless.

When lack of learning has silenced them,
it will be easy to become one land with that side. [TN: The "New Light" (nai roshni) was the common phrase for Western-educated modernism. Ahriman is the evil principle of Zoroastrianism.]
