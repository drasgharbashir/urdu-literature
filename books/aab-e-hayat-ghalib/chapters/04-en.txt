# Mirza Sahib's Life and Natural Habits (continued)

"Mir Mahdi, have you forgotten my habits? In the blessed month of Ramazan have I ever missed the *tarawih* (the special night prayers of Ramazan) at the Jama Masjid? How could I stay in Rampur in this month? The Nawab Sahib held me back and kept forbidding me, and kept tempting me with the mangoes of the rains, but, brother, I set out in such a way that I arrived here on the day of the new-moon night. On Sunday the holy month began. From that day, every morning I go to Hamid Ali Khan's mosque and hear the Qur'an from the revered Maulvi Jafar Ali Sahib, and at night I go to the Jama Masjid and say the *tarawih*. Sometimes, when I feel like it, at the time of breaking the fast I go to the Mahtab Bagh, break my fast there and drink cold water. Bravo, bravo, what a fine life it is![1] Now hear the real truth. I had taken the boys with me, and there they plagued the life out of me. To send them back alone, I feared that, God knows, if some mishap occurred, the disgrace would stay all my life; for this reason I came back soon, otherwise I would have spent the hot weather and the rains there. Now, if I live, I shall go alone after the rains and shall not come back here for many days. The arrangement is that the Nawab Sahib, from July 1859, which is now the tenth month, sends me a hundred rupees month by month. Now that I went there, he gave another hundred rupees a month by way of hospitality; that is, if I stay in Rampur I get two hundred rupees, and if I stay in Delhi a hundred. Brother, it is not a question of a hundred or two hundred; the point is that the Nawab Sahib gives as a friend and as a pupil, and does not regard me as a servant. Our meeting too was as friends: the embrace and the rising in respect that are the custom among friends, that was the form of our meeting. I had the boys present a *nazr* (ceremonial gift), that is all. In any case it is a blessing; one should give thanks that one's daily bread comes well; why complain that it is little? From the English government ten thousand rupees a year were fixed, and of that I got seven hundred and fifty rupees a year. One gentleman would not give more than three thousand rupees a year. In honour, the rank that is due to the sons of nobles remained: the titles 'Khan Sahib, most kind friend', a robe of honour of seven pieces, and a *jigha*, a *sarpech* and a string of pearls. The King loved me as he loved his own sons. Paymaster, superintendent, physician: my standing was no less than any of theirs, but the profit was the same small one. So, my dear, here too it is the same picture. I am sitting in a little room, the screen of wetted grass is up, the breeze is coming, a jar of water stands there, I am smoking the hookah and writing this letter; I felt like talking to you, and so I have had this talk."

A letter to Munshi Hargopal Tafta: "Well, now you have stayed on in Sikandarabad; why would you go anywhere else? You have already drawn out the money in the bank; now what will you live on? Sir, neither does my advice have any effect, nor is there any room for you to take it. There is a wheel that goes on turning; what is to happen goes on happening. If one had any power, one would do something; if it were a matter for words, one would say it. Mirza Abdul Qadir Bedil says:

What is the longing for rank, and what the disgust with possessions?
Pass beyond these desires, or do not pass: life is passing.

Look at me: I am neither free nor bound, neither sick nor well, neither happy nor unhappy, neither dead nor alive. I go on living, I go on talking, I eat bread every day and drink wine now and then. When death comes, I shall die too. There is neither thanks nor complaint; whatever I say is by way of telling a story."

The faith of the Mirza's whole family and of his elders was Sunni, but those who knew his secrets, and his writings too, show that his faith was Shia; and the charm of it was that it showed itself in the fervour of love, not in cursing and quarrelling. So many people used to call him a *Nusairi* (one of a sect that holds Ali divine), and he was pleased to hear it. In another place he says:

I am the Mansur of the sect that calls Ali God;
I raise the cry, "I am Asadullah, the Lion of God!"

All his relatives and true friends were Sunni, but in his closeness to them no sort of division was felt. He was also a *murid* (disciple) of the family of Maulana Fakhr-ud-Din. At court and among the courtiers he never disclosed this matter, and this was the way of most families in Delhi. Among his Urdu works there is a selected *diwan* (collection of poems) of 1,800 couplets, which was compiled and printed in 1849. In it there are some complete and some incomplete *ghazals*, and some scattered verses. Of *ghazals* there are about 1,500 couplets; of *qasidas*, 62 couplets; a *masnavi* (poem in rhyming couplets) of 33 couplets; *qitas* (verse fragments) among the miscellany, 111 couplets; 16 *rubais* (quatrains); and two chronograms of 4 couplets. As high as the Mirza's name stands in the world, his verse stands a thousand degrees higher in meaning; indeed many of his verses are set at such a height that our feeble minds cannot reach them. When these complaints were much talked of, the king of that realm of indifference, who was also king of the realm of poetry, answered everyone with one couplet of a *ghazal* of his:

No longing for praise, and no care for reward;
if there is no meaning in my verses, so be it.

He made another *rubai* too:

My verse is so very difficult, O heart,
that the accomplished poets, hearing it,
ask me to write in an easy way:
it is hard if I say it, and hard if I do not.

One day there was talk with my late master of Mirza Sahib's manner of subtle fancy, of his Persian constructions and of people's different tastes. I said: when one of his verses does come out plain, it works wonders. He said: "Good!" Then he said that people do not even know which is the Mirza's real verse; I will recite you his verses. He recited several scattered couplets; one is still in my mind: [TN: Azad's master was the poet Zauq.]

The sea of sins dried up for lack of water;
the hem of my garment had not yet even got wet.

There is no doubt that, by the virtue of his name, he was the lion of the forest of themes and meanings. Two things are peculiar to his manner. First, that creating meanings and subtle fancy were his special way[2]. Second, since he had more practice in Persian and a natural bond with it, he often put words together in ways that are not used in speech; but the verses that came out plain are such that they have no equal. The wits too did not miss their teasing. So once the Mirza too went to a *mushaira* (poetry gathering). Hakim Agha Jan Aish was a merry, cheerful man (see page [illegible]); in his *ghazal* on the set pattern he recited this *qita*: [TN: In a *mushaira* poets recite *ghazals* on a set metre and rhyme, the *tarah*.]

If you alone understand what you say, what have you understood?
The joy of speaking is when one speaks and another understands.

One understands Mir's verse, and one understands the Mirza's speech,
but what he says, either he himself understands or God understands. [TN: "Mir" and "the Mirza" in the third line are the earlier poets Mir Taqi Mir and Mirza Rafi Sauda.]

For this reason too, at the end of his life, he gave up the way of subtle fancy altogether. So look: his last *ghazals* are plain, and whatever the quality of the two kinds is will become clear. I learned from aged and reliable people that in fact his *diwan* was very large, and this is a selection. Maulvi Fazl-e Haq Sahib was a scholar without equal; at one time the record-keeper in the district court of Delhi, a pupil of Mirza Qatil Sahib, wrote good Persian verse and prose. In short, these two accomplished men were Mirza Sahib's close friends, and there were always friendly gatherings between them and talk of poetry. They heard many of his *ghazals* and looked at the *diwan*, and explained to Mirza Sahib that these verses would not be understood by ordinary people. The Mirza said: I have said so much already; what remedy can there be now? They said: well, what is done is done; make a selection and throw out the difficult verses. Mirza Sahib handed over the *diwan*, and the two gentlemen looked through it and made the selection. This is the *diwan* that today we carry about held to our eyes like spectacles. [TN: The second friend's name seems to have dropped out of the printed sentence about the record-keeper.]

*Ud-e Hindi* ("Indian Aloes-wood"): some commendations, some other prose pieces, and letters. Most of the letters are answers to people who asked the meaning of some difficult verse or inquired about some point of Persian or Urdu that needed research.

*Urdu-e Mualla* ("Exalted Urdu"): 1285 AH, 1869 AD. Some pupils and friends put together in one place as many of his Urdu letters as came into their hands, and the Mirza himself named this collection *Urdu-e Mualla*. The style of these letters is as though he were sitting in front of you scattering flowers; but what can one do, for even his talk was studded with the elegant cuts and fine constructions peculiar to Persian. If some phrases sound new to the ears of Indians of little learning, that is their affair; it comes from learning being little current. So he writes: "What a heart-bleeding coincidence! Now forgive the fault of *varang-vari*. So I must give up the comfort of Koil and, willy-nilly, stay with Babu Sahib. This rank is above my worth; may it be the pride of the realm of Hindustan." In some places he has translated a Persian idiom word for word, as has been written in the masters' verse about *tir* and *sehra* and so on. So in these same letters he writes: "You ask so much excuse." This phrase came from his pen because *uzr khwastan* ("to ask excuse"), the Persian idiom, was always on that master's tongue; Indians say *uzr karna* or *uzr ma'zirat karni*. "If you look with an eye to this rule, I have not a straw's worth of kinship with this man": this is a translation of *nazar bar in zabita*. "Munshi Nabi Bakhsh keeps a complaint about your not writing": *gila darand* and *shikva darand* is the Persian idiom. "How now, Maharaj: coming to Koil, singing *ghazals* with Munshi Nabi Bakhsh, and not bringing us to memory!": *yad avardan* is the true coin of Iran; Indians say *yad karna*. "What is known to you should not remain unknown to me": *har che bar shuma munkashif ast bar man makhfi namanad* ("whatever is revealed to you, let it not stay hidden from me"). [TN: *varang-vari* is printed so; the reading is uncertain.]

The style of these letters too is of a special kind, in which the sallies of wit and the playfulness of grace can be expressed very well. It was his own invention: he enjoyed it himself and gave pleasure to others; it is not anyone else's work. If someone wanted to write letters in this manner on a historical event, a moral thought, a learned subject or some particular worldly affair, it would not be possible. Since this book contains his real letters, it is a mirror of his outward and inward state. From it this much appears: the griefs and pains of the world always troubled him, and by his high spirit he blew them away in laughter. The full pleasure of these writings comes only to someone who is well acquainted with his circumstances, with the ways of the people he wrote to, and with the personal affairs of both sides. They are not understood by a stranger, so if people who are unacquainted and uninformed find no pleasure in them, it is no wonder.

In this book he has made *qalam* (pen) and *iltimas* (request) feminine, and *pinshan* (pension), *bedad* (injustice) and *barak* (barracks) masculine. In one place he says: "My Urdu will be more correct than other people's."

*Lata'if-e Ghaibi* ("Hidden Witticisms"): in this tract the words are addressed to Munshi Sa'adat Ali. Although the name Saif-ul-Haq is written in its preface, the style of the writing and its sallies say plainly that it is the Mirza. Saif-ul-Haq is in fact that same Miyan Dad Khan to whom there are some notes of Mirza Sahib's in *Urdu-e Mualla*. So in one note he writes to him: "Sahib, I have given you the title Saif-ul-Haq ('Sword of Truth'); you are the commander of my army."

*Tegh-e Tez* ("The Sharp Sword"): Maulvi Ahmad Ali, professor at the Hooghly Madrasa, had written *Mu'ayyid-ul-Burhan* in reply to *Qati'-e Burhan*; Mirza Sahib wrote an answer to some of its points and named it *Tegh-e Tez*.

At the end of *Sati'-e Burhan* there are some leaves under the name of Sayyid Abdullah[3]; they too are Mirza Sahib's.

# Notes

[1] From "the first of Ramazan" to here is pure playfulness, because the Mirza used to keep miles away from all the things in these sentences. This letter is from after the Mutiny; by that time these things had become a dream in Delhi.

[2] Pen name Auj, name Abdullah Khan; he had forty or fifty years' practice. He produced such lofty themes and subtle fancies that he could not bring them under control, and he bound them in fine words with such tightness and correctness that the theme could not even fit; so sometimes the meaning became something quite different, and sometimes nothing was left at all. He wrote *ghazals* in stony, difficult patterns, but in the themes and the search for words he had no sense of [illegible]. He laboured over them with close attention and enjoyed them all by himself. From chewing his lips, one part of them had turned white. Reciting some verses he would say that [illegible] when he made the verse. Of some he would say that he had gone on reciting them for six months together; he recited and wept, so that it was a sight worth seeing. When he recited a *ghazal* at a *mushaira* he would pass through the ranks of the gathering and go out in front. Some people of the city, and many *murshidzadas* (princes) in the Fort, were his pupils, but everyone called him master. He would go and recite to accomplished poets and would not leave without getting cries of "Bravo!" and wails and laments of praise, because he thought it his right. The late Zauq, for all his few words and his habit of silence, would say "Fine, fine, very fine," have the verse recited again, smile and show delight in his face, as though he were sitting in the rapture of the verse. And the Mirza, who was always looking for the makings of such fun (may God grant this gift), would hear his verses and say: "They are all infidels who call you master; you are the god of poetry, the god!" He would make a gesture of prostration and say, "Glory be to God, glory be to God!" In those days I was a keen beginner; taking me for his admirer he was very pleased with me, and he would say, "You alone understand our verse." If he met me in the road, he would stop when he saw me ten paces off, and recite from there, swelling with pride, any new verse he had made, and then walk on hearing and reciting verses. He would stroll for hours on the open ground below the Fort and keep reciting verses. He came to my poor house too and never sat less than three hours. One day he met me in the road and as soon as he saw me began to say: "I went today and recited it to him too." I said: "What?" He said in a thundering voice: "Even on a sheet and a half there are an opening and a closing couplet, Ghalib; / it is not easy, Ghalib, to be the master of a *diwan*." Then he told how at one gathering Momin Khan too was present, and everyone asked him for a verse. He had written a *ghazal* on a *ghazal* of Nasikh's, and recited it. At the closing couplet they were greatly astonished: "for what they call the seventh heaven is a leaf of my seventh *diwan*." They asked whether he was writing a seventh *diwan*. He said, "Yes, now it is the eighth *diwan*." They fell silent. Generally he made verses on events. Kunwar Ajit Singh gave Momin Khan a she-elephant (see page 601), and he said: "In his next births that Momin takes a house / who, turned astrologer, takes the gift of a she-elephant." In Delhi Shirin was a very famous courtesan; she set off on the Hajj, and he said: "It is fitting if Shirin has left Delhi and set off on the Hajj: / the proverb is, the cat ate nine hundred mice and set off on the Hajj." Twenty or thirty years have passed, and that talk is no more. I knew many verses by heart; my memory has been unfaithful; perhaps letters and paper will keep faith. What I remember I write down, and I regret his heart-scraping toil and its waste: "The fishes of the brows are on the forehead, within the frown; / the Ganges flows backwards within the Machchhi Bhawan. The workshop of the overturned world runs upside down; / the sun is an upturned candle in this assembly. I am that palm-tree by the stream of the river of Salsabil; / my boat of flowers is a sea-coconut. Revelation comes down to me from the whirlpool of the sky; / my guide is Khizr, the Gabriel of the river. I lie measuring the Black Water day and night; / my yardstick of the earth is a river [illegible]. The battlement of my pen has become the fortress of the desert realm; / my blister is [illegible]. The lustrous theme of the waterfall has much of the mountain in it; / our pen is the trunk of the river-elephant. A great ship hangs on a single anchor-thread at its tail; / in my working is the heavy body of the river. I go drifting on the wave of my own departure; / like a bubble, I am the river's drum of departure. Our wave is acquainted with the storm; / this is salt water, a river [illegible]. Auj is the pupil of the eye of the water-folk; / draw a river fountain from the wet eye. Madness was often putting chains on me; / even in childhood my neck-ring was often lost. When the gold of the rose was in the knot of the bud's purse, / the nightingale was often there making merry. If the rampart of breath holds this fancy of mine, / I would cross the Sirat without wings: such is my skill. From childhood love's house of madness has been mine; / my navel-string is buried in the deer's hoof. Who, O killer, remembers our earning of martyrdom? / We take our head cast in the mould of the sword. The passion of love pleases me, sweet and salt; it is not weeping: / the water I drink from tears is clear water. I turn back the reins of the blisters of smallpox; / - - - - - -" [TN: the second line is printed as a row of dashes]
