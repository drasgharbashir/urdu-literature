# (13)

Along with its excellences in language and poetry, Lucknow also advanced further in learning and scholarship than all the cities of India. In truth, as regards the sciences, Lucknow was the Baghdad and Cordoba of India, and the Nishapur and Bukhara of the farthest East.

Learning here began with the scholars (*ulama*) of Farangi Mahal, whose history has been touched on at the beginning of this essay. Learning no doubt came here from Delhi; but in the old days only one of the scholars of Delhi is to be seen, Shaikh Abdul-Haqq, who won lasting fame in *hadith* and the religious sciences. No trace is to be found there at any time of a seat of learning (*dar-ul-ulum*) like Farangi Mahal. After Farangi Mahal had become famous, it is true, the family of Shah Waliullah Sahib became very famous in Delhi, through whose grace and blessing the teaching of *hadith* is now carried on in all the cities of India. But if the teaching of *hadith* is the memorial of that renowned family of Delhi, the teaching of morphology and syntax, logic and philosophy, rhetoric (*ma'ani-o-bayan*) and the other subjects of the curriculum is the memorial of Lucknow's renowned university, Farangi Mahal.

Close examination and enquiry show clearly that rationalist scholars such as were born in Lucknow, and in Farangi Mahal in particular, were never born at any time or in any place in India. The decisive proof of this is that the books in use in the curriculum are either the works of the famous Persians of earlier times, or of the people of Farangi Mahal, or of those who had benefited from Farangi Mahal.

The Shia *mujtahid*s (jurists qualified to give independent rulings) also had their beginning in Farangi Mahal. Maulvi Dildar Ali Sahib, the first *mujtahid* of Lucknow, had at first studied the books of the curriculum in Farangi Mahal itself. Then he went to Iraq and sat as a pupil at the feet of the scholars of Karbala and Najaf, and on his return, with the endorsement and recommendation of the people of Farangi Mahal themselves, he was recognised as a *mujtahid* and became the religious leader of the Shia rulers of the time. Since he had been educated in Iraq, he brought with him a new taste for Arabic literature, and in literary scholarship the family of the *mujtahid*s and the Shia scholars of Lucknow always held, and hold to this day, precedence over the people of Farangi Mahal.

The literary taste of the Shia scholars made Lucknow the highest centre of literary education, which produced so precious a man of letters as Mufti Mir Abbas Sahib.

Among the religious sciences, in jurisprudence and its principles, theology (*kalam*) and doctrine; among the literary sciences, in syntax and morphology and rhetoric; among the rational sciences, in logic, philosophy, natural philosophy and metaphysics; and among the mathematical sciences, in Euclid and astronomy, the scholars of Farangi Mahal enjoyed special renown, and in the whole of India the real centre for the teaching of these sciences was Lucknow. Arabic literature, poetry and prosody the Shia scholars and *mujtahid*s of Lucknow had made their own.

*Munazara* (religious disputation), by which we here mean specifically theological debates and the mutual refutations of Shias and Sunnis: this art began in India with Qazi Nurullah Shushtari, who had come from Iran expressly to refute the Sunnis. From his time quarrels arose here between Shias and Sunnis. And at last, long after the Qazi, Shah Abdul-Aziz, the *hadith* scholar of Delhi, wrote the *Tuhfa-e-Isna Ashariya* in refutation of the Shias. Maulvi Dildar Ali Sahib wrote something in refutation of some of its chapters. Then came Maulana Haidar Ali, who was in fact a native of Faizabad but whose name shone in Lucknow. He wrote the *Muntaha-ul-Kalam*, which was regarded as the finest book in refutation of the Shias. At the same time Maulvi Lutfullah Sahib, who had studied in Lucknow and settled here, wrote several books of his own in which, besides research and refutation, there was also a playful wit. Mian Mushir refuted him with great vehemence, but the truth is that his book passed beyond the bounds of refutation and entered the territory of ribaldry and lampoon. Finally Maulvi Hamid Husain Sahib wrote very large and voluminous books in refutation of the Sunni faith, in which he refuted it with the authority of a *hadith* scholar; and now we see that Maulvi Abdush-Shakur Sahib too is winning prominence in this art on the Sunni side.

In our view, religious controversy, however much it may please the people of one party or the other, is entirely fruitless, and its harm exceeds its benefit. But here we need only show that in this art too Lucknow reached a height that no city had ever attained before.

Among the religious sciences, Lucknow was deficient in *tafsir* (Quranic exegesis), *hadith*, *rijal* (the biographical criticism of transmitters) and history. Of these, the art of *tafsir* existed in Lucknow to an ordinary degree, and there was no more of it anywhere else than there was here. It is true that some famous commentators have lived in some cities, but their depth and excellence remained confined to their own persons and ended with them. *Hadith* remained the speciality of Delhi. In the last period the late Maulana Muhammad Abdul-Hayy came back with a licence (*sanad*) for the teaching and transmission of *hadith* from the *hadith* masters of Mecca the Exalted and started a course of teaching too, but the art did not grow well here. The art of *rijal* is subordinate to *hadith*. The deeper one goes into *hadith*, the more one's insight into *rijal* grows. So the scholars of Lucknow remained as deficient in *rijal* as they were in *hadith*. There remains history: this art never flourished in India. No doubt, from the needs of society, there was a good deal of taste for history among those who knew Persian; but the scholars of India never gave this art more value than storytelling, because of which a very great deficiency generally remained among the scholars. And it was this that put into the head of every child in India the idea that "What have scholars to do with knowing the world? They are simple, straightforward folk bound for Paradise!"

But seeing the needs of the time, the scholars of both parties have begun to make suitable additions to their syllabuses, and on the other side there is the *dar-ul-ulum* of the Nadwat-ul-Ulama, which is paying particular attention to those necessary sciences that had until now been neglected.

But even with these shortcomings, what was achieved in Lucknow was far more than in other places.
