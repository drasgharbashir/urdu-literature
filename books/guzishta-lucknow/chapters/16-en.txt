# (15)

But although great scholars of high rank in the Arabic sciences sprang from the soil of Lucknow, it cannot be denied that Arabic education was confined to the leaders of the community and the guides of the faith. In India the court language was Persian. To obtain employment and to shine in cultivated and respectable society, an education in Persian was thought quite sufficient here. Not only in Awadh but in the whole of India, Persian alone had become the means of literary and moral advancement. Never mind the Muslims, the general inclination of upper-class Hindus too was towards Persian literature and prose composition, so much so that the finest manuals of epistolary style had been compiled and edited by the pens of Hindu authors. Tek Chand Bahar composed so unrivalled a book as the *Bahar-e-Ajam*, an incomparable and matchless treasury of the idioms of the Persian language, in which countless verses of native speakers are cited as authority for every idiom. In the early rise of Lucknow the names of Mulla Faiq and then of Mirza Qatil became famous; Qatil was a Persian scholar and a convert to Islam. He himself used to say in jest, *Bu-e-kabab mara musalman kard* ("The smell of kebabs made me a Muslim"), but the truth is that the study of Persian, his love of it and his desire for perfection in Persian scholarship compelled him to become a Muslim. Purely from this passion he travelled to Iran, wandered for years through Shiraz and Isfahan, Tehran and Azerbaijan, and reached such perfection in Persian literature that it would be no wonder if the native speakers themselves envied so accomplished a master of the language.

Mirza Ghalib has attacked Mirza Qatil in many places. No doubt Mirza Ghalib's taste in Persian was of the very highest order. He insisted again and again on the principle that no one's verse but a native speaker's can be an authority. But because in his time people from Awadh to Bengal were followers of Qatil, and Qatil's name was cited at every turn, Mirza Ghalib often lost his temper, and when Qatil's followers began to take him to task he said:

*Faize az suhbat-e Qatilam nist / rashk bar shuhrat-e Qatilam nist* (I have had no benefit from Qatil's company, / nor do I envy Qatil's fame.)

*Magar anan ki Farsi danand / ham barin ahd-o-ra'y-o-paimanand* (But those who know Persian / are all of this same opinion, covenant and pledge:)

*Ki z ahl-e zaban nabud Qatil / hargiz az Isfahan nabud Qatil* (That Qatil was not a native speaker; / Qatil was never of Isfahan.)

*La-jaram i'timad ra nasazad / gufta-ash istinad ra nasazad* (So he is not to be relied on; / his word is not fit to be cited as authority.)

*Kin zaban khass-e ahl-e Iran ast / mushkil-e ma-o sahl-e Iran ast* (For this language belongs to the people of Iran; / what is hard for us is easy for Iran.)

*Sukhan ast ashkar-o pinhan nist / Dihli-o Lakhnau z Iran nist* (It is a plain truth and nothing hidden: / Delhi and Lucknow are not of Iran.)

But it does not follow from this that the efforts Qatil made in Persian scholarship, and the life he spent acquiring knowledge and mastery of it, went entirely for nothing. No one can object to granting that no claim of Qatil's is acceptable until he produces the authority of native speakers, and such an idea can never have crossed Qatil's own mind. But this is not peculiar to Qatil; no one from India can be an authority in his own right. Mirza Nausha Ghalib himself cannot use any Persian idiom without producing proof from the people of Persia. If the Persian scholars of India can have any standing at all, it is only on the ground that their reading in Persian literature is wide and that they know the correct place for the use of every word. And from this point of view, in truth, Qatil stood much higher than Ghalib. Ghalib wandered all his life through the dust of India, and was harassed besides by the search for a livelihood. Qatil had been granted a time of ease, and for years lived in the holy soil of Iran, tramping from village to village.

In any case, Persian scholarship in Lucknow began with Qatil. And a little before him Mulla Faiq, whose family had come from Agra and settled near Lucknow, composed matchless books of the highest order on Persian literature and style and on Persian verse and prose. There had been writers and scholars of Persian in India before them, but the passion for codifying the principles and rules of the Persian language and its morphology and syntax, alongside Persian scholarship, began first of all in Lucknow, and appeared from their pens. Their books, in truth, are matchless and unanswerable.

After this Persian remained part of general education here, and the Persian syllabus was kept so elevated and difficult that it was in truth harder than the syllabus of Iran itself. In Iran, as is the custom of the people of every country, a plain, simple, eloquent language in which ideas are expressed with clarity is preferred, and the syllabus is of the same kind. In India the verse of such subtle-minded poets as Urfi and Faizi, Zuhuri and Ni'mat Khan Ali was put into the curriculum. The writings of such lovers of difficulty as Mulla Tughra and the author of the *Panj Ruq'a* began to be read and taught, from which it can be claimed that in this last period the mastery of the language in India had surpassed that of Iran itself. And the people of this place had written commentaries of the highest order on all the Persian textbooks. And the astonishing result of this is that while the poets of all the world's languages remain confined to the circle of native speakers, and if two or four poets arise among non-native speakers they carry no credit among the native speakers, poets of Persian arose in India in numbers equal to, if not greater than, those of Iran. Especially in the last century, when Lucknow's drum was beating in the world of progress and learning, every child here wrote Persian verse. Persian *ghazal*s were on the lips of illiterate courtesans and bazaar labourers, and even the *bhand*s (mimes) did their impersonations in Persian. The respectable occupation and livelihood of all the gentry of the small towns of Awadh was teaching Persian, and such excellent village teachers of Persian wandered the lanes of Lucknow that the people of Persia themselves would marvel at their mastery of the language. Their accent might not be that of native speakers, but in the research and precision of Persian idioms, turns of phrase and words they had reached such a level that they would not think an ordinary native speaker a threat.

How far the taste for Persian had advanced in Lucknow can be judged from the Urdu language of Lucknow. Even on the tongues of the illiterate and of women there are Persian compounds, turns of phrase and *izafat* (genitive constructions). And if those who attack the language of Lucknow have been able in all this time to find any objection, it is only this, that Persian has grown in it beyond moderation. But by the standard of advancement of that age, this very thing was the merit of the language of Lucknow and the proof that its society had risen higher. Even in Delhi, however many periods of advancement of the Urdu language are distinguished, the only distinction between an earlier and a later period can be that in the later the influence of Persian is greater than in the earlier.

Like the Muslims, the Hindus too were winning distinction in Persian. Although this had begun to show from the early period of the Mughal empire, and even then there were some famous and recognised Hindu scholars and poets of Persian, in Awadh this taste reached its utmost perfection. So accomplished Hindu scholars of Persian such as were found in the neighbourhood of Lucknow were found nowhere else. The Kayasths and the Kashmiri Pandits had made Persian education compulsory for themselves, and advanced so far that the mother tongue of the Kashmiri Pandits actually became Urdu, and there was very little difference between their Persian scholarship and that of the Muslims. Because the Kayasths were natives of this place their language remained *Bhasha* (the Hindi vernacular), but Persian education had so entered their veins and sinews that they began to use Persian idioms with great excess and incoherence, something not found among Hindus anywhere else. In those days people used to make fun of the Kayasths' language, but the truth is that instead of mocking it they ought to have valued it, for their language was the proof of their intellectual progress, just as nowadays those who know English think the use of English words in and out of place proof of their intellectual progress, and go on stuffing English words into their speech with great lack of manners.

In those days there were hundreds of Persian prose writers and poets in Lucknow, and Persian *mushaira*s were held regularly, just like Urdu ones. Persian had become the inner and outer garment not only of the gentry but even of the common people. And now, although Persian is no longer the court language and Urdu has taken possession of the seat of government, Persian's coin is still current in cultivated society to this day. The general view is that Persian has gone out of the schools and *maktab*s and is no longer needed for earning a living, yet without having read Persian a man cannot be fit to sit in cultivated society, nor become a complete man in the true sense.

In England the language of France was once the court language. Although it is long since it was driven from the court, social life and moral refinement cannot be acquired there even today without learning French. In eating and drinking, sitting and rising, dress, laughter and talk, in short in every manner of life, the rule of French remains as it was, and girls cannot become refined ladies without acquiring the French language. The same is the case with Lucknow: Persian has gone from the court, gone from correspondence, but still rules every department of social life, and without an education in Persian neither can our taste be correct nor can we acquire the art of saying things well.

At Matiya Burj (Calcutta), among the few people who settled there with the last ill-starred crowned head of Awadh, there was no educated person who did not know Persian. The language of the office was Persian, and there were hundreds of Persian poets among Hindus and Muslims. Even women wrote verse in Persian, and every child could express himself in Persian.

In present-day Lucknow, although the study of Persian has greatly declined, and the Hindus have abandoned it so far that the very language of the Kayasths, which used to be mocked in circles of linguistic taste and which even the *bhand*s used to imitate, has become a dream and a memory, yet among the old elders, and especially among the Muslims, much of the taste for Persian remains, because their very knowledge of Urdu becomes to some extent a means of knowing Persian. Among the Muslims there is still such a researcher of Persian as Khwaja Aziz-ud-Din Sahib, left to recall the old assembly of poetry, who in his mastery is unique in the whole of India. And among the old Hindus of advanced years too you will find several scholars of Persian, one example of whom is Raja Durga Prashad Sahib of Sandila, whose greatest distinction is that the times have changed, earth and heaven have changed, the air and water have changed, but he is the same to this day. He is there to give and receive praise for Persian scholarship, and like a worm-eaten leaf of past history he is worthy to be kissed, cherished and pressed to the eyes.
