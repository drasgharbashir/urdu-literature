# (7)

After Muhammad Ali Shah, Amjad Ali Shah adorned the royal throne. Muhammad Ali Shah had tried to give the heir to the throne an education of the highest class, and so kept him in the company of scholars and learned men. The result was that Amjad Ali Shah, instead of making any notable progress in learning, became in his manners and habits a staid *maulvi* (cleric). Once he had the reins of government in hand, his one ambition was that he, and all his subjects with him, should become devoted disciples of His Reverence (*Janab-e-Qibla-o-Ka'ba*, the chief *mujtahid*). But plainly the religious scholars and leaders of the community can have nothing to do with politics. They can be neither statesmen nor administrators. The only guidance to be had from them was that the Sayyids should be served and the state's money spent on helping and supporting the faithful. And even this, in the eyes of the devout and scrupulously pious ruler of Awadh, Amjad Ali Shah, could be satisfactory only when carried out by the blessed hands of the *Mujtahid* of the Age himself. So hundreds of thousands of rupees from the country's revenue were presented to him under the name of *zakat* (alms), and many other sums for charity besides passed through his hands.

For Amjad Ali Shah the idea of sanctity and ritual purity had become a disease. His observance of the religious law as he understood it left him no time to attend to the administration of the realm, with the inevitable result that all the arrangements Muhammad Ali Shah had made through his experience and alertness fell into confusion, and things reached such a pass that (in the words of Qazi Muhammad Sadiq Khan Akhtar) all the officials were wicked, evil-hearted and self-seeking. The subjects were ruined. Might was right. The oppressor and the criminal went unpunished. The treasury was empty. Bribery flourished, and whatever troubles arose, no one could put them down.

Yet despite this pious inertia and this commercial neglect and indifference, he founded the quarter of Hazratganj, which today is the cleanest, best-populated and most beautiful of all the quarters of Lucknow, the finest market of wealthy merchants and the liveliest part of the Civil Lines. He built a metalled road straight from Lucknow to Cawnpore. The greatest work of his reign was that the Iron Bridge was completed. The story of building this bridge is this. Ghazi-ud-Din Haidar had ordered its parts and pieces from England, but before they reached Lucknow the King had departed for the eternal world. When the pieces arrived from abroad in Nasir-ud-Din Haidar's time, he gave his court engineer, Mr Sinclair, the contract to put them together and erect the bridge, and ordered that the pieces be laid on the far bank of the river opposite the Residency. To mark the spot where these iron parts of the bridge were laid, a *ghat* and a Shiva temple (*shivala*) stand there today. Mr Sinclair had deep wells dug to set up piers in the river, and even had the piers built, but after that he could do nothing more, and he failed to complete the bridge. In Muhammad Ali Shah's time the bridge lay unfinished, but Amjad Ali Shah turned his attention to it in his reign and the bridge was completed. However, the iron bridge that stands today is not the bridge of Amjad Ali Shah's time. That was a hanging bridge, a suspension bridge, whose whole weight hung from four tall, massive iron pillars. In English times, when its parts had rusted and weakened and it seemed dangerous for general traffic, it was pulled down and another iron bridge was put up in its place, and that is the bridge that stands now.

In Amjad Ali Shah's time too his minister Amin-ud-Daula founded Aminabad, whose population and splendour are growing every day. Although Amjad Ali Shah did nothing in his time, and built no building of his own choosing that remains as his memorial today, perhaps as a reward for his piety and abstinence he was granted the natural fame that the two most famous, most populous, liveliest and wealthiest quarters of present-day Lucknow, Aminabad and Hazratganj, are memorials of his reign.

At last time turned the page of his reign too, and in 1276 of the Muhammadan era (1847 CE), when he was a little over forty-eight, he fell ill with cancer and departed this world, and was buried in Hazratganj, the quarter he had founded, inside the cantonment of Mendu Khan the cavalry captain. His *imambara*, in which he lies buried, stands on the road in the western part of Hazratganj; after his death Wajid Ali Shah built it at a cost of a million rupees. This *imambara* is a poor copy of Husainabad, and if it were illuminated like Husainabad, the eastern part of Lucknow too would become a world of light in Muharram. Although no endowment has been fixed for it, its income is not small either. In the shops on the outer face of the enclosure are the shops of many good merchants, and many Europeans and others live in the inner buildings, from whom a considerable sum is collected in rent. But it is also the kindness of the rent collectors that they light a few lamps on the tomb itself and in the *imambara* in Muharram.

(Now Amjad Ali Shah's eldest son, Wajid Ali Shah, adorned the throne of the realm.) His reign is the last page in the history of this Eastern court and the last stanza of this ancient Shia age. Since the annexation of the state took place in his reign, he became the target of every critic's arrows and reproaches, and it has been almost accepted that he was the cause of the state's fall. But at the time his reign ended, all the native powers of India were breaking up, and all the old, decayed governments of every kind were vanishing from the world. Why was the record of the Sikhs in the Punjab and of the Marathas in the Deccan closed, who are reckoned brave, strong and shrewd? Why were the Mughal empire in Delhi and the Nawab Nazim of Bengal uprooted? Yet there was not as much childishness in them as is said to have been in the occupant of the throne of Lucknow. In none of these four courts was there a Wajid Ali Shah, yet their ruin was no less than the ruin of Lucknow.

The truth is that at this time the measure of the Indians' negligence and ignorance was near to overflowing, while the power of the British state, and the foresight, ability, industry, efforts and high civilisation and refinement of the British people, were proving every day more deserving of their reward. It was impossible that the intelligence and talent, the good management and orderliness of the wise men of Europe should not triumph over the ignorance and self-forgetfulness of India. The age had taken on a new colour of civilisation throughout the world, and was calling aloud to every people that whoever did not keep pace with it in this taste would be wiped out. No one in India heard the voice of the age's proclamation, and all were wiped out. Among those wiped out was the state of Awadh, and to lay the burden of its fall on poor Wajid Ali Shah is contrary to the spirit of research.

His father, strict in religious observance, had tried to make Wajid Ali Shah like himself by keeping him in the company of scholars, and this colour did take on Wajid Ali Shah to some extent, and showed more clearly as he grew older. But Amjad Ali Shah could do nothing about the fact that his son and heir's natural bent was towards pleasure and the arts of amusement and delight. Although at his father's insistence he was well taught in reading and writing, his love of music prevailed. While still heir apparent, out of his own taste and against his father's wishes, he kept singers and *dhari*s (hereditary musicians) about him, learned to sing and play, grew intimate with loose women and with *dom*s and *dhari*s (musician castes), and in the end the pleasure he found in the company of beautiful women and singers he did not find in the refined company of the learned.

Unlike his father, he also had a taste for building, and while still heir apparent he had an airy garden made for his own festivities and pleasure, with one or two small, beautiful and elegant houses in it. Ali Naqi Khan, to whom he gave the robe of the ministry as soon as he took the throne, he had met while heir apparent at a courtesan's house. Ali Naqi Khan's youthful, playful temperament won him influence with the prince, and when the garden and buildings mentioned above, built under his supervision, were found pleasing, it was concluded that no one was better fitted than he for the ministry and the administration of the realm.

Wajid Ali Shah's reign began in this way: the young, dashing King showed unusual attention to dispensing justice and reforming the army. Two little silver boxes went before him in his processions. Anyone with a grievance wrote a petition and dropped it in them. The King kept the key himself. On reaching the palace His Majesty would take out the petitions and write orders on them with his own hand. In this way several new cavalry regiments and several battalions were raised. With his playful wit the King named the cavalry regiments Banka (Dashing), Tirchha (Rakish) and Ghanghor (Thundercloud), and the battalions Akhtari and Nadiri. His Majesty would ride out in person on horseback and stand for hours in the sun watching their drill and their skill in the arts of war, and in delight he would honour the expert soldiers with rewards and favours. For military drill he himself fixed Persian terms and words of command: *Rast rau* ("Forward march"), *Pas bia* ("Fall back"), *Dast-e-chap bigard* ("Left turn"). A few days later a small women's army of beautiful young women was formed, and they too were taught drill with the same terms.

But this first sketch of the new reign lasted only a few days. Not even a full year can have passed before he grew weary of these things. The old taste of his days as heir apparent returned. His intimacy with beautiful and loose women grew, the market of the musicians and dancers flourished, and in a short time *dom*s and *dhari*s were the pillars of the state and the notables of the realm. If any learned and gentlemanly taste was left in the King's heart now, it was poetry, for he wrote verse himself and appreciated poets.

In those days the cultivation of poetry in Lucknow had grown beyond all bounds. In Lucknow alone there were so many poets that if the poets of all India had been gathered together, their number could not have exceeded the poets of Lucknow. The old poetry of Mir and Sauda had become a thing of the past. Now the language of Nasikh and the ideas of Atish had settled in people's minds, into which the libertine verse of Rind and Saba and the *masnavi*s (narrative poems in couplets) of Nawab Mirza Shauq had breathed a spirit of sensuality; and it was this very taste that the true colour of the King's temperament wanted and liked.

Up to the first century of the Islamic caliphate, the manner of Islamic poetry was that poets fell in love with a particular woman, named her again and again in describing the beauties of her person and the charms of her ways, and addressed her in expressing their restlessness and longing. They often met her in secret, but never stepped outside the bounds of decency and chastity. A little later, in Arabia itself, the beloved became nameless, and in general the poets' beloved became a figure of their imagination, whom the libertines declared to be some beautiful woman or handsome boy, while the Sufis, by a little allegorical interpretation, declared him to be their Absolute Beauty, that is, the Creator of the world. This was the general rule: the veiled, discreet libertine taste remained in Persian poetry, and the same taste prevailed in Urdu poetry until that time. But Nawab Mirza Shauq made his poetry an instrument for falling in love with beautiful women in *purdah* (seclusion) and ruining them. And the calamity was that the language of his *masnavi*s was so beautiful, natural, polished and refined, and they were so crammed with amorous feeling, that even cultivated and refined people could not keep from reading and relishing them.

Wajid Ali Shah too read these *masnavi*s, and being, God be praised, a poet himself, he adopted this manner, put his many love affairs and hundreds of the libertine excesses of his early youth into verse himself and spread them through the country, and so became a self-confessed criminal in the moral world. I think that, to say nothing of kings, there can have been few even among ministers and nobles who did not give full vent to their sensual desires in early youth; but no one before had himself laid such shameless offences before the public as Wajid Ali Shah did. When Wajid Ali Shah got into his stride, though he could not surpass Nawab Mirza in poetry, he went two steps beyond him in proclaiming his feelings, thoughts and deeds to the world, and went so far that on some occasions he did not hesitate to use vulgar street taste and obscene words.

He fell in love with water-carriers' wives, courtesans, maidservants, women who came and went in the palace, in short with hundreds of women, and being heir to the throne he was very successful in his loves, the shameful stories of which can be heard from his own lips in his poems, writings and books. And this is why his character appears the most impure and darkest in history.

Since he had a boundless taste for building, as soon as he took the throne he began to build the Qaisar Bagh, which, though it may not be as strong as Asaf-ud-Daula's buildings, is matchless in beauty and splendour. In it a long rectangular area of many elegant and magnificent two-storeyed buildings stretched far, one side of which, facing the river, was dug away after the Mutiny, while three sides still stand. The government has divided them into lots and handed them over to the *taluqdar*s (great landholders) of Awadh, with orders that they live in them and keep them up in the same style.

The inner courtyard of the Qaisar Bagh, which was laid out with flower-beds, was called the Jilau Khana (forecourt). In the middle was the Patthar-wali Baradari (Stone Pavilion), which is now the Town Hall of Lucknow. There were several other buildings in it too, which no longer remain. Outside it, right next to it, were many royal buildings that had made this piece of ground a wonder of the age. These buildings lay outside the eastern gate of the Qaisar Bagh. As soon as people came out of this gate they found wooden screens on both sides, passing between which they reached the Chini Bagh (Chinese Garden). Turning left from there you came to a magnificent gate of mermaids, in which the chief minister of the state, Nawab Ali Naqi Khan, lived, so as to be always near His Majesty and to be called at once when needed. On the far side of this gate was the Hazrat Bagh, and inside, on the right, was the Chandi-wali Baradari (Silver Pavilion). This was an ordinary building of brick and lime, but it was called the Silver Pavilion because sheets of silver were set in its ceiling. Adjoining it was the Kothi Khas Maqam, in which His Majesty himself lived. And there too was the old mansion Badshah Manzil, built by Nawab Saadat Ali Khan.

Then, coming out of the passage between the wooden screens and turning the other way, a series of intricate buildings stretched far, known as the Chaukhi. The founder of these buildings was Azimullah, the King's personal barber (*huzuri nai*), from whom the King bought them for four hundred thousand rupees. The Nawab Khas Mahal (the chief queen) and the honoured ladies of the palace lived in them. Here Hazrat Mahal stayed during the Mutiny, and here she held her court.

From here a road came towards the Qaisar Bagh, beside which stood a great shady tree. Round its foot an exquisite circular marble platform had been built, on which, at the time of the Qaisar Bagh fairs, His Majesty would come dressed as a *jogi* (Hindu ascetic) in ochre clothes and sit by a smouldering ascetic's fire (*dhuni*). Beyond this platform was a magnificent gate called the Lakhi Phatak (the Lakh Gate), because a hundred thousand (a *lakh* of) rupees had been spent on building it. Going on from it you came back into the Qaisar Bagh. Eight million rupees of the state's money were spent on the Qaisar Bagh, and in the buildings on all four sides of it lived His Majesty's begums and his fairy-faced, moon-browed ladies, in whose place some people of the old days, seeing strange and peculiar figures now, used to say:

The fairy hides her face, the demon flaunts its charms and airs; / Reason is burned up with wonder: what a marvel is this! [TN: a Persian couplet, quoted in the original on the Urdu page.]

Outside the western gate of the Qaisar Bagh was Raushan-ud-Daula's mansion, which Wajid Ali Shah had confiscated and renamed Qaisar Pasand, and one of his beloveds, Nawab Mashuq Mahal, lived in it. Now it holds the court of the Deputy Commissioner. In front of it, on this western side of the Qaisar Bagh, there was a second forecourt as well.

Once a year a great fair was held in the Qaisar Bagh, at which the public too had the chance to come into the Qaisar Bagh and see the colour of His Majesty's pleasures. The King had seen the *rahas* (dance-drama) of Sri Krishna that is popular among Hindus, and had liked Sri Krishna's beloved-like ways in love so much that he devised a play from this *rahas* in the manner of a drama, in which he himself played Kanhaiya (Krishna) and the virtuous ladies of the harem played the *gopi*s (milkmaids), and parties of song and dance were in full swing. Sometimes, in the passions of youth, he would become a *jogi*; pearls were burned to make the ascetic's ash (*bhabhut*), so that even in poverty the marvels of royalty could be seen. At fair time the townspeople at large were allowed to join these gatherings, on condition that they came dressed in ochre clothes, with the result that even old men of eighty put on vermilion clothes, turned dandies, and filled the cup of their old age from the wine of the King's youth.

This was how things went on, and revels were being held in Lucknow in complete unconcern, when the Residents informed the British government of the state of affairs here, and the Board there decided that the country of Awadh should be annexed to the British dominions. To carry out this order English troops came to Lucknow, and suddenly, against all expectation, the King was told: "Your country has been annexed to the English dominions. Twelve hundred thousand rupees a year have been fixed for you, and three hundred thousand rupees a year for your ceremonial guard, which is amply sufficient for the needs of yourself and your dependants; and you are permitted to sit comfortably and carefree in the city and, free from the cares of your subjects, to hold your revels without hindrance." At these orders the city fell silent. The King himself pleaded and wept a great deal. The King's mother and his chief queen did their duty as advocates, but changing the Governor-General's order was beyond the Resident's power. The government of the East India Company took possession of the country of Awadh without any trouble or resistance, and the King, with his mother, the heir apparent, his principal ladies and his devoted companions, set out for Calcutta, meaning to go to England and appeal, prove his innocence, and have the order of annexation revoked.
