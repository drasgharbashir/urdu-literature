# (26)

In connection with music it is necessary to describe *soz-khwani* (the chanting of elegies). Although it is disrespectful to class this new religious art with the arts of singing and playing, which are contrary to the *shari'a*, the difficulty is that *soz-khwani* is in fact a particular kind of music. Renewing the memory of the martyrdom of the grandson of the Prophet [TN: Husain], upon him be peace, in Muharram began in India specifically with the Shias, especially from the time when the Twelver faith became the national religion of Iran and its people began to come and gain influence at the Indian court. But since in Delhi the religion of the crowned heads and the royal family was that of the Sunna and the Community (*Sunnat-o-Jama'at*), the particular things peculiar to the religious life of the Shias could not flourish there. So the cradle in which these arts were nurtured was the city of Lucknow and its former Shia court.

Just as religious zeal produced elegy-writing and *taht-ul-lafz-khwani* (recitation in the speaking voice) in poetry, so in music it produced *soz-khwani*, and then advanced both arts so far that they became independent arts, and arts that from beginning to end are peculiar to Lucknow. *Taht-ul-lafz-khwani* is reciting the elegies with gravity and ease, showing their meaning with gesture as one recites, in the way a poet recites his *ghazal* at a *mushaira*. And *soz-khwani* is presenting them with a melody full of pathos and tenderness.

The original and old way of reciting elegies was *soz-khwani*, that is, elegies were always presented with melody in the mourning assemblies, and this was the custom not only in Delhi but in all the cities of India where Shia gentlemen lived. Even in Madras and the Deccan elegies were chanted in this way with great fervour, and texts composed a century and a half or two centuries ago survive to this day. Presenting elegies in the manner in which poets recite their verse is purely Lucknow's invention, and we have already described, in connection with poetry, the feats that Mir Anis, Mirza Dabir and others displayed in it.

Although *soz-khwani* existed before, and everywhere, the *soz-khwan*s of Lucknow displayed such feats in it too that they made this art also their own. The former state of *soz-khwani* throughout India can be judged from the saying, "A failed singer becomes an elegy-chanter." Lucknow raised *soz-khwani* to such a height that even the market of accomplished singers grew slack before the *soz-khwan*s.

The *soz-khwan*s, like other men of art, came to Lucknow with Nawab Shuja-ud-Daula or in his time. It is written in the history of Faizabad that mourning assemblies were held in the palace of Bahu Begum Sahiba, Shuja-ud-Daula's wife, and Jawahir Ali Khan, the eunuch who was steward of her household and her whole estate, used to listen to the dirges of the elegy-chanters. But until then *soz-khwani* here was the same as was common everywhere.

Some say that this art began with Khwaja Hasan Maududi. He was the teacher of the author of the *Naghmat-ul-Asafiya* [1], and though self-taught, he had such mastery in the art of music that he had no equal far and wide. Although a Sunni by faith, he set particular tunes of music to *soz*es and taught them to his pupils, and the foundation was laid for this art to become regular and orderly. After this, when the time of Crackpot Haidari Khan came, his custom was to chant dirges in Muharram in tunes suited to his taste. Since he was a very great and accomplished singer, and the court was appreciative, he had marked success in this effort, and it was discovered that if it were developed, this art could take on a special and distinct glory of its own. From among the thousands of tunes of music those were chosen that were suited to the expression of grief and sorrow and to lamentation, and they were set to hundreds of *soz*es. Finally Haidari Khan taught his *soz-khwani* to Sayyid Mir Ali Sahib, a Sayyid of noble lineage, who in his religious zeal advanced this art greatly and became famous in his time as so great a master that when, in Nawab Saadat Ali's time, he was offended at something and resolved to leave Lucknow, Insha-Allah Khan interceded for him with his effective poetic manner and mocking wit, and the Nawab kept him back with consolation and esteem.

After this a singer of Tansen's family, Nasir Khan, came to Lucknow and shone. Seeing people's absorption in *soz-khwani* here, he too devoted his musical mastery to the chanting of dirges and won popularity and fame; and taking pity on a poor widowed Sayyid lady in his neighbourhood, he taught *soz-khwani* to her two sons, Mir Ali Hasan and Mir Banda Hasan. Their mastery surpassed that of all the earlier masters, and they proved without equal or peer in *soz-khwani*. It is they who have made *soz-khwani* a *rag* of the highest order, so much so that while even most singers do not remember the words of the real *rag*s of music, most *soz-khwan*s remember *soz*es that are the words of *rag*s, on hearing which the true *rag*s and the right tunes can be clearly understood.

Because of these elders the art of *soz-khwani* in Lucknow passed from the singers to the gentry, and many people began to appear who are not *dom*s or *dhari*s but gentlemen of good family and standing, yet have such mastery in *soz-khwani* that the market of the singers has grown slack before them.

At present Manjhu Sahib and one or two other gentlemen have such mastery and such fame in *soz-khwani* that everywhere throughout India eyes are spread like carpets in eager welcome for them, and the appreciation of the people of other cities always snatches them away from their admirers in Lucknow in the month of Muharram and on the special days of mourning.

This taste had its greatest effect on the women of Lucknow. As soon as the moving, heart-shattering tunes of the *soz*es came from the throats of Mir Ali Hasan and Mir Banda Hasan, they went down into the throats of hundreds of gentlemen, and through them into the radiant voices of the women of thousands of respectable Shia families. Women by nature have a greater taste for singing and music, and their voices are generally better suited to melody. When this principled and regular chanting of dirges reached the women, a tremendous charm arose in it, and in a few days a taste for chanting dirges arose not only among the Shias but among the women of the upper-class Sunnis too. And it came to this, that in Muharram and on most days of religious observance, wonderful, astonishing sounds of tender, plaintive runs and charming melodies rise from every house in the lanes and alleys of Lucknow, and there is no place where this atmosphere is not created. In whatever lane you stand and listen, you will hear such charming voices and such ravishing, transporting melody as you will never forget all your life. In the houses of the Hindus and some particular Sunnis there is silence, it is true, but wherever else you turn your ear, the sounds of the overwhelming melodies of the dirges come to you.

Since the keeping of *ta'ziya*s (replicas of Husain's tomb) is an occasion for chanting dirges, *ta'ziya*s began to be kept in the houses of both Sunnis and Shias out of the love of chanting dirges, and not only Sunni Muslims but thousands of Hindus too took up the keeping of *ta'ziya*s and began to chant dirges; from which it can be seen that the powerful cause of the great growth and flourishing of *ta'ziya*-keeping in Lucknow is the chanting of dirges.

In Lucknow some respectable, refined and educated women are such good *soz-khwan*s that, were it not for the barrier of *purdah*, male *soz-khwan*s could never prosper against them. It was a long time ago that one year, at the *Chehlum* (fortieth-day observance), I went with some friends to the *karbala* of Tal Katora and spent the night there in a tent. At two in the morning I suddenly woke, and the sound of so charming a melody came to my ear that it woke all my friends and made them restless. Drawn by that voice we all came out of the tent and saw: the stillness of the last part of the night, the moonlight flooding everything, and in it a crowd of women coming with a *ta'ziya*. All had loosened their hair and were bareheaded. In the middle one woman held a candle in her hand. By its light a beautiful, cypress-tall, delicate girl was chanting a dirge, reading from a few leaves, and several other women were singing with her. I cannot describe the atmosphere created by that stillness, that hour, that moonlight, those bareheaded beauties and that plaintive, tender melody. As soon as this company of delicate graces entered the gate of the *karbala*, the cypress-tall girl began this dirge in the tune of *Paraj*:

When the caravan of the city of Medina, plundered, / drew near to Syria, made captive,

Husain's head borne before it on a spear, / and behind it the ladies with heads uncovered,

This elegy, so fitting to the moment, suddenly created such an atmosphere that one could not tell whether through these verses the lady was painting a picture of the tragedy of Karbala, or of her own mourning procession and its entry into the *karbala*.

The fact is that no other thing has had so marked an effect on the women of Lucknow, and on the men with them, as *soz-khwani* and mourning. Its first blessing is that all the women have become very good singers and have begun to chant dirges according to the true principles of music. Its second blessing is that the whole population of the city, men and women alike, has acquired an affinity with music. What is seen in the lanes and alleys of Lucknow, that boys of the lower sort and bazaar people often break into song as they walk, and sing with such skill and pick up the most difficult tunes with such ease that outsiders are astonished, is really caused by this taste for the chanting of dirges and *soz-khwani*. And the praiseworthy thing is that *soz-khwani*, in spite of spreading among the common people and the illiterate of the lowest sort, remained on correct principles and was not allowed to depart from true musical taste, unlike other things that become irregular and corrupt as soon as they reach the common people.

Although the common Shias think *soz-khwani* a meritorious act, the Shia scholars have not yet given a ruling (*fatva*) that it is permissible. They are strict in observing the *shari'a*. To this day, in the mourning assemblies of the *mujtahid*s and of sober people only *hadis-khwani* (recitation of traditions) or *taht-ul-lafz-khwani* takes place, and even in the assemblies of the common people at which the scholars of the law are present, there is no *soz-khwani* before them. But it cannot be denied that, through its general popularity, *soz-khwani* has won a complete victory over the rulings of the scholars. The difficulty is that among the Sunni scholars of *hadith* and the Sufi elders there is much room for the permissibility of song, but perhaps in Twelver jurisprudence there is not so much room; otherwise this art would by now have obtained a licence of permissibility.

[1] Sharar's note: His mastery in music can be judged from this. In the days of the Maratha raids he was travelling from Lucknow towards Etawah, riding in a *miyana* (palanquin). On the way they passed through some village and heard that the Marathas were about to raid it. The bearers, who had been carrying him from very far away, suddenly set the palanquin down and said, "We have no strength to go any further." They were told a thousand times that this place was dangerous, but they would not listen. Khwaja Sahib, despairing of his life, made his ablutions and said the afternoon prayer, and, sitting where he was, began to sing a slow prelude (*alap*), and it had such an effect on the bearers that they were refreshed and carried him to a place of safety.
