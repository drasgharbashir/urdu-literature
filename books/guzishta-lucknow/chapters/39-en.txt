Having finished the discussion of eating and drinking and of dress, we now turn to those things which are specially connected with society and social intercourse, and which Lucknow made its own by setting on them the stamp of its own taste.

In every country of the world a civilisation of social intercourse and manners is established, which is mostly concerned with style and appearance, morals and habits, sitting and rising (deportment), manner of speech, manner of wit, house and furniture and so on; and after these things, with the equipment of life which that society needs. Naturally these things arise in every group, every class and every city and village, and even today, if you travel round the world, you will see everywhere the particular nature of society and its characteristics. But in places where some honoured court is established and learning and letters advance, the society there makes a large part of the country subordinate to it and becomes the reference point for the social life of its every city and village and the centre of the principles of culture.

In India the real centre of culture, civilisation and the manners of society was certainly Delhi, because for many centuries it had been the centre of government in India and the source and seat of learning and scholarship. All India was under its sway, and the governors of all the provinces were men trained in its society and schooled in its manners. In comparison with it Lucknow has no distinction and can have no superiority. But if there can be any reason for naming Lucknow in this connection, it is this: that by the chance of the times, in the last century, that same social life of Delhi was transferred wholly to Lucknow, and the nobles and gentry, the scholars and poets, the pious and the righteous of that place all came to Lucknow. And whatever court was broken up in Delhi came and gathered in Lucknow, because all the men of style there came here one by one, and owing to the security they found here they began to make further advances on their already advanced social life. And the charm of it was that in the social life of the people of Delhi that was established on coming to Awadh there was no outsider but the people of Delhi, so much so that even the old honoured inhabitants of Lucknow found no place in it at all.

So the social life of Lucknow is in fact the social life of Delhi and the last example of the advanced society of that place. In this last century there came to be two schools of the old civilisation of Delhi: one which existed in Delhi itself, and the other which was transferred to Lucknow. But there is no doubt that in the last century before the decline, the school that was in Delhi, owing to the weakening of the Mughal court and the disappearance of wealth, did not have such opportunity to step forward in the field of advance as the Delhi school in Lucknow had. And this was why in that period the civilisation of Lucknow was advancing while the advance of the old civilisation of Delhi had stopped. In short, these very advances are the characteristics of the society of Lucknow. Indeed on reflection it appears that the advance which civilisation and social life in Delhi had gained from the blessings of the old imperial court has also departed in the last period, owing to the ascendancy of ignorant trading castes and the dispersal of the old gentry of family to other cities or their withdrawal into their homes. And the truth is that since the breaking up of the royal court of Awadh, owing to the mingling of outsiders and the disappearance of the old cultivated families and their influence, the culture that had arisen in Lucknow too is departing day by day.

But we are not concerned with the society of ill-breeding and the insolent morals and manners that began to arise in Lucknow after the Mutiny and keep on advancing. Our purpose is only to show that culture which, nurtured in the lap of the royal court of Lucknow, had arisen in the society of this place.

In connection with this essay of ours on the social life of this place we wish to describe the following matters: (1) the house, (2) furniture, (3) style and appearance, (4) morals and habits, (5) deportment, (6) greetings and enquiries after health, (7) manner of speech, (8) manner of wit, (9) gatherings of joy and mourning, (10) *majlis*es, (11) gatherings of the *Maulud Sharif* (the Prophet's Nativity). Then after these we shall describe those things which are the requisites of company and the equipment of social life.

(1) The house. In Delhi and Lucknow the old taste regarding houses was that outward display and magnificence were reserved only for royal palaces and halls. The houses that nobles and merchants had built for their own dwelling, however spacious and fine they might be within, had outwardly the appearance of quite ordinary houses. And the prudence in this was that houses which were magnificent in appearance often took the fancy of kings, and their builders were seldom fortunate enough to live in them. At the same time there was also the fact that any subject's showing royal ambition in building a house was taken as insolence and rebellion, and it became difficult for him to live in safety.

For this reason you will not see in Delhi, apart from tombs, a single building of olden times that is magnificent and was built by some high-ranking noble or wealthy merchant among the subjects. In Lucknow too it was the same at first. In the times of Nawab Asaf-ud-Daula and Nawab Sa'adat Ali Khan the wealthy French merchant Monsieur Martin built one or two magnificent buildings, but the real intention in building them was that they should please the ruler of the city and be sold to him. Among these buildings is La Martinière College, which, owing to Nawab Sa'adat Ali Khan's parsimony, the State could not take possession of. This is the same mansion that at present is famous among the common people under the name of "Markin Sahib's *kothi*".

After this a minister here, Raushan-ud-Daula, had a fine building built for his own residence, whose fate was that it was confiscated by order of the government, and at the time of the annexation of the kingdom it was counted among the royal possessions. Thus in the English period, being government property, it came into the possession of the Government and was not given to Raushan-ud-Daula's heirs. But to this day it is called Raushan-ud-Daula's *kothi*, although the Deputy Commissioner Sahib Bahadur and his assistants hold their courts in it.

The style of the ordinary houses of the subjects here is entirely different from the villa-like houses of Europe. In Europe there is no need of a courtyard inside the house, because women, like men, not observing *purdah*, go out and take the air in the open. So unlike there, here it is necessary that there should be a courtyard inside the house, so that the women may enjoy the open air inside the house itself.

This need and the other demands of social life here have made the general form of houses here this: a courtyard in the middle, and the building round it. In this building one side is made the principal side (*sadr*), and there, on pillars of brick and lime, at least three and sometimes more arched openings are set up. The arches are generally on the model of the Shahjahani arches, that is, in them small cusps are handsomely joined to make a large arch. On the principal side there are often double or triple halls of such arches. The rear hall is sometimes made into a large room by fitting doors, and often it also happens that its plinth is raised about waist-high and it is made into a *shah-nishin* (raised alcove).

On both sides of these great halls there are rooms, and the ceiling of the hall is so high that at the side two rooms, one above the other, fit within the one ceiling of the hall.

Now on both sides of the courtyard, in proportion to its length, verandahs (*dalan*), rooms and closets are built, in which are the kitchen, the privy, the store-room, the staircase, the well, and the quarters of the *mama*s (maidservants) and *asil*s (household women servants). On the side facing the principal verandah too, if it seems necessary or if means allow, magnificent verandahs are built there just like those on the principal side. The door is usually at the side, that is, on those sides where the kitchen and the rooms for the servants' quarters are, opposite which on the inner side, facing it and to one side, a wall a little higher than a man's height is set up, so that the inside may not be in view from the door.

In the houses of the poor and those of middling rank, instead of masonry arches, wooden *seh-dara*s (three-arched openings) of the same style are often set up to make verandahs, in which on the principal side, and sometimes on the opposite side too, there are verandah within verandah. In the more complete houses of this kind there are *seh-dara*s and verandahs on all four sides, and at their sides single-doored closets open, which serve various needs, and in one of them is the outer door.

This was a general outline of the houses here. But keeping this general form, in many houses below and everywhere rooms and closets are opened out with such skill and elegance that one is astonished how so much accommodation has been got into so little space.

If you look at the history of the art of building, you will see that at first low buildings were made, then tall and strong but plain buildings began to be made. After this, for adornment and ornament, designs began to be made on them, inlay work (*pachchi-kari*) was invented, and colouring began to be done in wonderful ways. But despite all these accomplishments, walls were still of great broad thickness, and great halls and audience rooms were made in them.

The latest accomplishment in Indian building was that, by cutting and fitting like a tailor, a great deal of accommodation should be got out of a little ground. This accomplishment of building began in Delhi itself, there advanced to a very high degree, spread from there everywhere, and in Lucknow advanced more than in any other place.

Nowadays there are great master engineers who have built great and magnificent buildings. They will set up for display a most beautiful and splendid form of building, but it is the share of the old craftsmen alone to raise a magnificent building on a small piece of ground and, with geometrical skill, to get out of it so many verandahs, rooms, closets and little courtyards that the beholder's mind is set spinning. The inner screen walls are so thin, delicate, light and at the same time strong that it seems they are not walls of brick and lime but wooden screens.

This was the special character of Lucknow in building, which the former court nurtured. But now in the English period this accomplishment is vanishing for want of appreciation. The old craftsmen have passed away, and the one or two who remain are not valued.

But from olden times a clear difference has come down between the houses of Hindus and Muslims, which exists to this day. The Hindus keep the courtyard in their houses very small and narrow, and go on increasing the accommodation without regard to whether air and light will pass or not. In contrast, the Muslims want open airy houses, and increase the accommodation only to the degree that airiness and brightness are not affected. But despite this taste of the Muslims, the former craftsmen have got out so much accommodation even in their airy houses that beholders are filled with admiration.

Besides this, the accomplished masons (*mi'mar*) of that time used to make on doors, the arches of rooms, and the walls of verandahs and rooms such fine and high-class designs in various colours as can hardly be made now. And nowadays the art of painting has no doubt advanced, but the painting that the masons used to do on doors and walls has vanished, and owing to the love of simplicity of the new age is vanishing day by day. Even so there are still some master masons of this work here whose painting perhaps the masons of no other city could do. Not only designs; they can also make pictures of a high class on ceilings and walls.

It was not only the masons; the carpenters of that time also had the same accomplishment. They might not be able to make high-class tables, chairs and cupboards or railway carriages, but on pillars, arches and the frames and jambs of doors they used to carve such fine and delicate designs as could hardly be made today.
