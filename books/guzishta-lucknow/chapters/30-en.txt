# (29)

Nawab Abul-Qasim Khan was a noble of refined taste. A very rich *pulao* was cooked at his house. A broth (*yakhni*) of forty-two seers of meat was prepared and distilled, and the rice was steamed in it, and with such delicacy that as soon as one put a mouthful in one's mouth it seemed that all the rice had melted of itself and gone down the throat. And with that, it was so light that one could not feel the slightest heaviness in it. A *pulao* of the same or greater richness was prepared every day for Wajid Ali Shah's chief consort (*Khas Mahal Sahiba*).

With the deposed King of Awadh mentioned above there was at Matiya Burj a noble whose title was Munshi-us-Sultan Bahadur. He was among the most punctilious and refined lovers of good things. He was immensely fond of food, and although he had several accomplished cooks, he found no pleasure in a meal unless he had cooked one or two things with his own hands. In the end his good eating became so famous that Wajid Ali Shah used to say: "It is Munshi-us-Sultan who eats well; how can I eat well!" In my childhood I lived with him at Matiya Burj for six or seven years, and used to join him at his dining cloth. At his table I ate thirty or forty kinds of *pulao* and scores of kinds of curry, some of which I never had the luck to eat again. He was also very fond of *halva sohan*, which will be mentioned in its place.

In the last period and after the Mutiny, the late Hakim Banda Mahdi in Lucknow was immensely fond of eating and dressing well, and great men of wealth and taste are convinced that the food he ate and the clothes he wore very few people in that age could have had. An aged and honoured friend of ours says: "Our family was on very close terms with the said Hakim Sahib. One day Hakim Sahib sent for my father and uncle, saying that a wrestler was being entertained and they should come and enjoy the fun. My father went, and I went with him. On arriving we learned that the wrestler drank twenty seers of milk every morning, on top of that ate two and a half or three seers of dried fruit, that is, almonds and pistachios, and at noon and in the evening ate bread made from two and a half seers of flour and a goat of middling size. And he had strength and bulk to match this diet. He was impatient for his breakfast and kept pressing again and again for the food to be sent for quickly, but Hakim Sahib was deliberately putting him off, until the violence of his hunger made him frantic, and now he grew angry and began to get up. Then Hakim Sahib, promising to send the food, went inside. He put him off a little longer, and when he saw that the man could no longer bear his hunger at all, he sent a tray by a maidservant, at the sight of which the wrestler's spirits revived. But when he opened it, there was a little *pulao* on a small saucer, no more than a *chhatank* (two ounces) in quantity. The voracious guest flew into a rage at the sight of this rice, which was not enough for even one of his mouthfuls. He meant to get up and leave, but people talked him round and held him back, and perforce he took up the saucer, tipped it into his mouth and swallowed without chewing. After five minutes he asked for water, and five minutes after that he drank water again and belched. Now trays of food came from inside. The cloth was spread. Hakim Sahib himself came too. The food was laid out, and a plate of the same *pulao* of which a mouthful had been sent before, containing perhaps a quarter and a half seer of rice, was set before Hakim Sahib. Hakim Sahib offered the plate to the wrestler and said, 'Look, is this the same *pulao* or another?' He admitted that it was the same. Hakim Sahib said, 'Then eat now. I am sorry that there was a delay in preparing it and you were put to trouble.' The wrestler said, 'But please excuse me now. I am full from that first mouthful, and cannot eat a single grain more.' He was pressed a thousand times, but he absolutely held back his hand and said, 'How can I eat? Only if there were room in my stomach.' Hakim Sahib took the rice and ate it all, and said to him: 'Eating twenty and thirty seers is not food for a man; that is the food of cows and buffaloes. A man's food is to eat a few mouthfuls, but to get from them such strength and vigour as cannot come even from eating twenty or thirty seers of grain. You have been satisfied by this one mouthful. You are invited again tomorrow. Come tomorrow and tell me whether from this one mouthful you felt the same strength and vigour as you used to get from twenty seers of milk and seers of dried fruit, meat and grain, or less.' And Hakim Sahib invited all of us for the next day too. The next day the wrestler came and declared: 'Never in my life had I known such vigour and liveliness as I have had from yesterday until today.'"

Among the members of the royal family in the last period, Nawab Mohsin-ud-Daula and Nawab Mumtaz-ud-Daula were regarded as unrivalled in their love of and skill with the table and the kitchen. And it was their cook who used to prepare this *pulao* for Hakim Banda Mahdi Sahib. In those days there was the great establishment of the Malika-e-Zamaniya, whose kitchen was famous, with cooking worth three hundred rupees a day. In this same period a cook named Alam Ali was employed in the establishment of Prince Yahya Ali Khan. He cooked whole fish (*musallam machhli*) so incomparably that it was famous among all the nobles, and the cooks of other establishments tried a thousand times but could not produce the same thing.

In Nasir-ud-Din Haidar's time a man from Persia named Mahmu came and opened a cook-shop in Farangi Mahal, and his *nihari* (slow-cooked stew) became so famous that great nobles and even princes valued it. Appreciation raised his ambition, and he invented the *shirmal*, which is the pride of Lucknow to this day. Many kinds of bread are famous and current in different cities. The Muslims had come from Iran eating leavened bread and setting up *tannur*s (clay ovens) in the land of India, but until then the breads were plain, without any *ghee*. Seeing the Hindus fry *puri*s, the Muslims added *ghee* to their griddle breads and invented the *paratha*, and then began to give it many folds and layers. Then the first advance in this same *paratha* was the coming into use of the *baqarkhani*, which at first was a very elaborate bread of the nobles' tables. In Lucknow Mahmu greatly improved on the *baqarkhani* and baked the *shirmal*, which in taste, fragrance, refinement and delicacy surpassed the *baqarkhani* and all kinds of elaborate breads. To this day the *shirmal* is baked nowhere but in Lucknow, and if it is baked elsewhere, it cannot be baked so well. In a few days the *shirmal* won such general popularity that it became the chosen bread of Lucknow, so much so that a feast without *shirmal* is not thought complete.

The invention of the *shirmal* so raised Mahmu's standing that for royal assemblies and ceremonies he sometimes received an order for a hundred thousand *shirmal*s in a single day, and he too had made sufficient arrangements to supply as many *shirmal*s as were asked for. Mahmu's successor in these days was Ali Husain, who died several months ago. But *shirmal*s of so high a quality as can still be had from his shop today cannot be had anywhere else.

Even more delicious than the *shirmal* is the *nan-jalebi*, which is baked with particular care, and only those *rikabdar*s who know it can prepare it. And the cooks claim that no one can bake *nan-jalebi* better than the cooks of Lucknow. In *paratha*s Lucknow stands at the same level as other cities. No particular advance seems to have been made in them; indeed it is said that the good bread-bakers (*nanbai*s) of Delhi bake *paratha*s of a very high class and work a full seer of *ghee* into a seer of flour. But during my stay in Delhi I had *paratha*s baked several times by famous bakers. They certainly used a lot of *ghee*, but since they had not put *ghee* into the dough, the *paratha*s were fit to eat only so long as they were eaten fresh. As soon as they cooled they turned to leather.

Breaking bread and rubbing it together with *ghee* and sugar is a common and ordinary food, most used at *fatiha*s and votive offerings (*niyaz*). But the cooks of the royal kitchen here prepared so delicate a *malida* that some rulers were very fond of it, and its merit was that as soon as one took a mouthful it turned to syrup, and it seemed there was no need at all to chew or move the mouth.

In this matter of bread things advanced so far that *puri*s were made of milk alone, with no flour in them at all. From the curd of milk alone they produced the quality of kneaded fine flour, and in the end things advanced so far that *gilauri*s and other kinds of things began to be made of milk. In the same way a *paniri* of pure milk came to the tables, which was a very refined and delicate food and much liked by the nobles.

But the chosen dish of the Muslims, that is, their national food, is *pulao* and *qorma*, so the greatest delicacy and refinement were shown in these. We have said a great deal about *pulao*, yet some things remain. For wealthy and pleasure-loving nobles, chickens were reared by feeding them pills of musk and saffron, until the fragrance of these two things permeated their flesh and every vein and fibre was perfumed. Then a broth was made from them, and the rice was steamed in this broth.

The glory of the *moti pulao* (pearl *pulao*) was that it seemed lustrous pearls were mixed with the rice. The method of making the pearls for it was this: a *tola* of silver leaf and a *masha* of gold leaf were thoroughly dissolved in the yolk of an egg; then this dissolved mixture was filled into a chicken's gullet, and a fine thread was tied tight at every joint of the gullet; it was then given a gentle boil, the skin of the gullet was slit with a knife, and shapely, lustrous pearls came out, which were steamed in the *pulao* with the meat. Some *rikabdar*s made pearls of cheese and covered them with silver leaf. In any case, such novelties were devised as never entered people's minds anywhere else. Some *rikabdar*s displayed this artistry in preparing *pulao*: they made little birds of meat, cooked them very carefully so that their shape was not spoiled, and set them on the plate, making the rice look like grain, so that it seemed that on the plate before each guest birds were sitting pecking grain. There were puffed *samosa*s from which, as soon as they were broken, a *lal* (red munia) came out and flew away. In Hyderabad in the Deccan these were prepared, probably by the Lucknow *rikabdar* Pir Ali, who came there; they appeared on the table at state dinners and greatly delighted distinguished English gentlemen and ladies. They had first been invented at Nasir-ud-Din Haidar's table. But the bird *pulao* mentioned above was a far more delightful piece of artistry.

One *rikabdar* displayed the artistry of presenting at the table great boiled and fried eggs weighing a seer each, in which the white and the yolk kept the same proportion and arrangement as in ordinary eggs. Some *rikabdar*s cooked a curry of almonds that looked exactly like the seeds of *sem* (broad beans) and surpassed them in taste and delicacy. The cook of the minister of state Raushan-ud-Daula cut the kernels of green maize cobs into strands so finely that they never broke, and made of them a *raita* (yoghurt relish) of such high quality that whoever tasted it was enraptured.

Our miraculous calligrapher Munshi Shakir Ali Sahib has displayed an unequalled feat by writing the *Qul huwallah* [TN: Quran 112] on a grain of rice; but in the royal period a cook here produced spines all round poppy seeds like those of a cucumber, cooked them by a special method and presented them at the table.

Pir Ali, the famous *rikabdar* of Lucknow who was employed in the kitchen of His Highness the Nizam, used to cook a very costly and delicious *arhar dal* that used to be cooked in the kitchens of the former rulers of Lucknow and was known as the *Sultani dal*.

Some *rikabdar*s cooked whole bitter gourds (*karela*) so finely and cleanly that to look at them it seemed that steam had not even touched them, that they lay green and raw as before; but when you cut and ate them they were most delightful and delicious. Something of the same kind happened quite recently to our honoured friend Sayyid Ali Ausat Sahib. He relates that Nawab Ali Naqi Khan, one of the present hereditary nobles of Lucknow, said to me one day, "Wait a little before you eat your supper tonight; I shall send something." At night, as promised, at supper time his man came with a tray. In my eagerness I had the tray brought before me and opened, and in it there was only one plate, and on it lay a raw marrow (*kaddu*). I was very put out at the sight. In my utter disappointment I said to the maid, "Take it away and put it by; cook it tomorrow." But the prince's man laughed and said, "Cut it and eat it just as it is; there is no need to cook it." Now when I cut it, it proved a wonderfully delicious and savoury thing, such as I had never eaten.

The truth is that the *rikabdar*s displayed wonderful skill here in artistry of this kind. Pir Ali the *rikabdar* used to make a pomegranate of sweetmeat in which the rind outside, the seeds within, their arrangement and the membranes between them all looked real. The stones of the seeds were of almond, the seeds of pear juice, and the membranes between the seeds and the rind outside were both of sugar.

The *rikabdar*s in general prepared preserves, pickles and the like, and sweetmeats of every kind, in which hundreds of kinds of recipes and wonderful artistry and refinements were displayed. Everyone has eaten mango preserve, but here the *rikabdar*s prepared a preserve of whole green unripe mangoes, in which the green skins remained just as they were. It seemed as if fresh unripe mangoes had just been picked and dropped into the syrup.
