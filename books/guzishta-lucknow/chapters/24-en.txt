Along with music, dancing too made very marked progress in Lucknow as a distinguished art. Dancing existed in every nation, and in the most ancient times. Before the Pharaohs of Egypt, graceful and alluring women stood and danced to music. In the time of Christ it was through dancing that Herodias had the head of John the Baptist cut off. But in India it appears quite clearly that dancing, like singing, was part of worship, and that the art of dance here was always nurtured in the lap of religion. So those who knew and practised this art were specifically Brahmins, and their centre was either the *kathak*s of Ajodhya and Benares or the *rahas-dhari*s (players of the *rahas*) of Mathura and Braj. It is a strange thing that although in all the ancient temples of India hundreds and thousands of women performed their *mujra* daily before the images of the gods, and wherever there were great temples there was from the most ancient times a great body of dancing women, mastery of dancing always remained with men, and it was they who taught it to the young women.

Dancing is really the name for making bodily movements regular. If this regularity of movement is concerned with making the movements of many persons uniform and in step with one another, it is drill or military exercise, or that dance of the music halls of Europe which is called "ballet", and which is now often seen in the theatres of India. And if that regularity of movement is concerned with fitting it to the measure of music and the rise and fall of the voice, it is dance. The true, pure dance of India is just this: the movements and pauses of the body made to match and suit the rise and fall of songs and notes. This is the true dance, which became a very great and wide art in India. Hundreds of its *gat*s (sequences) and countless *tora*s and *tukra*s (rhythmic figures) were invented. After this the expression of feelings and thoughts by gestures and movements was also included in dance. The result was that singing sometimes becomes a commentary on dancing. Then, since people naturally liked the dancing of beautiful women more, the display of a beloved's coquetry and airs and the showing of graces of delicacy and daintiness also became part of it. With these things in view, the Lucknow school created a distinction between female and male troupes. Showing with delicacy, displaying a beloved's coquetry and airs, and maintaining belovedness and daintiness in every movement remained peculiar to dancing women, which, if it sometimes becomes insipid, makes the spectators listless and dull. By contrast, showing nimbleness in fitting movements to the measure, and expressing feeling with poetic charm, became the special province of the male troupes. Although both groups certainly take some account of each other's art, this distinction is clearly maintained.

We have already said that the gathering in Awadh and Lucknow of entertainers and troupes of courtesans who perform the *mujra* had reached its height in the time of Nawab Shuja-ud-Daula. Besides them, the *kathak*s of Ajodhya and Benares, who were here or close by, seeing the patronage, began to be drawn towards the centre of the court, and through the mingling of the two, the art of dance, advancing markedly, began to take on a special glory here.

Among male dancers there are two groups: one the Hindu *kathak*s and *rahas-dhari*s, and the other the Muslim Kashmiri *bhand*s. But the real dancers are the *kathak*s. And the Kashmiri troupes, it seems, to put life into their feats of mimicry, added to their company a young dancing boy, who grows his hair and ties it in a knot like a woman's, and, dancing with great agility, brings liveliness and freshness to the gathering with his nimbleness.

Among the Hindu *kathak*s some master or other has been present here in every period. These men name Mahadev-ji, Parbati-ji and Kanhaiya-ji as the founders of their art. In the time of Shuja-ud-Daula and Asaf-ud-Daula, Khushi Maharaj was a very great master of dancing. In the period of Nawab Saadat Ali Khan, Ghazi-ud-Din Haidar and Nasir-ud-Din Haidar, Hilal-ji, Pargash-ji and Dayalu-ji were famous dancers. From the time of Muhammad Ali Shah to the reign of Wajid Ali Shah, the dancing of Pargash-ji's sons Durga Prashad and Thakur Prashad was famous. Of Durga Prashad it is said that he was Wajid Ali Shah's teacher in dancing. After this Durga Prashad's sons Kalka and Bindadin became famous, and almost everyone acknowledged that there was no more accomplished master of dancing in all India than these two. The old masters won distinction in some particular point, but these two brothers, especially Bindadin, displayed mastery in all the arts of dance and proved themselves unrivalled masters in every respect; most of the famous dancers of today are pupils of these two brothers, and their house is the greatest school of dance in all India.

Kalka died a little while ago, and the truth is that with his death the savour went out of Bindadin's dancing. Bindadin is now 77 years old, and lovers of the dance still think seeing his *mujra* a memorable joy of their lives. His dancing to the *gat*, his showing the masterly *tora*s and *tukra*s of dance in their true form, his displaying such command and power in sounding his ankle-bells (*ghunghru*) that he sounds whichever bell he pleases, and after that his expressing of every word and every thing, are things that end with Bindadin. He shows each thing with a hundred graces, attitudes, delicacies and charming gestures, and there is such subtlety of fancy and inventiveness in it that a spectator who did not know could not even understand. It was the custom that Bindadin would show and Kalka would stand by and explain it. It was only through his explanation that people learned what mastery Bindadin was displaying in his art. In dancing his feet touch the ground so delicately that it is said that he sometimes danced on the edge of a sword, and not a scratch came to his sole.
