We have described the ceremonies of rejoicing as far as was needed; now it is necessary to describe the gatherings of mourning too. But these are general throughout India. So far as I have considered, no special character of Lucknow is seen in them. The cause of mourning is someone's death, so on the day of death relatives and friends are informed, and those whom no necessity prevents always come. The women who come pay the hire of their *doli* or conveyance themselves. At ceremonies of rejoicing and in ordinary visiting it is obligatory that the hire of women guests who come should be paid, but the house of mourning has been exempted from this trouble.

After this they bathe the dead. Among the Shi'as it is the custom that for the washing the bier is first taken to the washing-house (*ghusl-khana*), where the corpse-washers (*ghassal*), who are very practised in washing but are also known to be hard-hearted, wash the dead and dress it in the shroud. But among the Sunnis the dead is washed in his own house, and relatives and kinsfolk or friends wash him themselves. Often men and women who are more practised are called, and in many places it happens that some Maulvi Sahib learned in the law, or some other educated and knowledgeable elder, keeps telling them in what order it should be washed and what the washing of the dead according to the *sunnat* is.

After the washing the shroud (*kafan*) is put on, in which an *izar* and a *kafni*, which is known as the *kurta*, are put on, two sheets are wrapped over them, and strips torn from cloth are tied near the head and feet and at the waist so that they should not come open.

After this, if it is the bier of Shi'as, it is placed in a coffin (*sanduq*) with some *doshala* thrown over it, and the bier is carried under the shade of a canopy (*shamiyana*). And alongside someone keeps reciting certain verses of the *Sura-e Rahman* [TN: Quran 55] with correct recitation and articulation. Those who carry the coffin and canopy are generally *shuhde* (ruffians), whose profession for a long time has been to carry the dead. But the vulgarities and bad manners of these people have produced the idea among the Shi'as that they should carry the biers themselves, for which several committees have been established in the city, and their zealous and pious members are on the lookout so that when anyone dies they may take his bier into their own charge and carry it with religious propriety and precautions.

Among the Sunnis they lay the dead on some light *charpa'i* (string bed), throw a sheet over it and take it. If it is a woman's bier, they set up bamboo slats on the *charpa'i* in the form of an arch, fix their ends into the *charpa'i* on both sides, and throw a sheet over it. This is called making a *gahwara* (cradle), and the need for it arose purely out of regard for *purdah*. Among the Sunnis the relatives and friends themselves carry the bier on their shoulders and take it slowly, reciting the *kalima*, and the funeral prayer (*namaz-e janaza*) is said.

The grave here is generally dug as a *sanduqi* (box) grave, in which a broad trench is dug as deep as a man's chest. Then inside it, leaving ledges on both sides, a second narrow trench is dug. This too is not less deep than a man's waist. When the grave has been well cleaned, they lower the dead into it with great care so that it should not fall from their hands and be hurt. In the grave the head is generally placed towards the north, and the face of the dead is turned towards the *qibla* (direction of Mecca) by propping it with clods and the like. After this they undo the ties, and often uncover the face to show the relatives the last look of the dead. On this occasion among the Shi'as the *talqin* (instruction to the dead) is recited, the form of which is that some trustworthy and pious elder goes down into the grave, keeps shaking the shoulder of the dead and keeps reciting an Arabic text, in which the dead is addressed and told: when Munkar and Nakir [TN: the two angels who question the dead] come there and question you, give these answers; in the course of which all the doctrines of religion are taught. After this planks are set over the inner trench, and if there are cracks or chinks in them, they make sure by placing clods of earth that the earth will not get inside. In the grave camphor and perfume are already in the shroud; some people also put in a bottle of *kewra* (screw-pine essence). And after this, earth is thrown on from above, the upper trench of the grave is filled, and the shape of the grave is made.

People regard giving earth as a most important and necessary act. And when earth begins to be thrown into the grave, every one of those present, whoever he may be, three times takes earth in his hand and throws it into the grave, and recites three verses of the Quran whose translation is: "From it (the earth) We created you, into it We have returned you, and from it We shall bring you forth again hereafter (on the Day of Judgement)" [TN: Quran 20:55, *Minha khalaqnakum wa fiha nu'idukum wa minha nukhrijukum taratan ukhra*].

In any case, when the grave has been made ready, the same sheet that lay on the bier, or a sheet of flowers, is thrown over it, and reciting the *fatiha* and praying for forgiveness, people come back.

In the house of the deceased no hearth is lit on the day of his death; rather, after the bier has left the house, ready-cooked food comes from the house of some relative, which people eat on returning from the burial, and at the same time all the guests fill themselves with this food. For three days it is usually the case that no food is cooked in the house. This practice in fact began from the beginning of Islam and from the Prophet himself (upon him be peace), when, hearing of the martyrdom of Hazrat Ja'far Tayyar and seeing his family weeping and beating their breasts, he had food sent. But the edifice that people here have raised on this polished foundation is most absurd and shameful. As soon as someone dies, all the food prepared in the house is thrown away, the water in the pitchers and pots is poured out, and the reason the women give children for this is that the angel of death washes the knife with which he takes life in the things to eat and drink.

On the third day after death, and sometimes on the fourth day if a suitable day is looked for, the *soyam* (third-day rite) takes place. In fact its beginning was that this day was fixed so that people should come and perform the rites of condolence (*ta'ziyat*) and console and comfort the survivors. But thinking that it did not seem good for a large gathering to sit idle, the practice was adopted that those who come should sit and recite the Holy Quran, and having read one or two *sipara*s (thirtieth parts) bestow their merit on the soul of the deceased. In a few days the idea of condolence disappeared, and all that remained was how many people came that day and how many Qurans were bestowed on the dead. At the close of the gathering, first various people recite a few sections (*ruku'*) of the Quran and the short *sura*s at the end, and raise their hands for the *fatiha*. In this a new absurd practice has been adopted: a little ground sandalwood, oil in a bowl, and a few flowers are brought and presented before each of those present. Each person picks up a flower and puts it into the oil, and the sandal, oil and flowers are taken and thrown on the grave of the deceased.

That same day in the evening the first great *fatiha*-reading takes place, and food is cooked in the house for the first time. Although poverty has now so reduced the number of sympathisers that very few remain who send food to the house of the dead, and many poor households are compelled to cook food before this, yet the customary practice is that until the *tija*, that is, the *soyam*, one should live on food from outside.

The *fatiha*s of the *soyam* and the *chehlum* (fortieth-day rite) have acquired a strange character among the common people. The essence is only this, that as far as possible the poor and needy should be fed and the merit of it conveyed to the dead. In India, seeing the *terahwin* (thirteenth-day rite) and *barsi* (death anniversary) of the dead among the Hindus, the Muslims wished that they too should do things of this kind with fame and pomp. The urge of this desire produced ceremonies of mourning under the names of the *tija*, *daswin* (tenth), *biswin* (twentieth), *chehlum* and *dalesa* (sixth-month rite), in which what is done is the same conveying of merit (*isal-e sawab*), but with the air of show, making a name and feeding the brotherhood. Then, on top of this, a calamity occurred in that the belief arose among the common people that whatever is given in these customary *fatiha*s of ours is by God's command delivered to the dead exactly as it is. This belief gave the *fatiha*s the character of an entertainment of the dead. Those foods are given with greater care which the deceased liked. Whereas the principle of charity demands that the taste of the poor man who is fed should be considered, so that the merit may increase by pleasing him.

Not only this; in the *fatiha*s now it happens that four or five sets of dishes of food are taken out and arranged in order in a clean and pure place. Water too is brought in a cup and set down, because in eating the dead will also need to drink water. Then for him, beside the food, new and as far as possible fine and costly clothes, bedding and coverings, a prayer mat, newly tinned copper vessels, *lota*, *katora*, *patili* (cooking pot) and so on are also placed. And when all this equipment is ready, some *mulla* comes and performs the *fatiha*, that is, recites certain particular verses of the Quran and short *sura*s and prays: "O Lord, convey the merit of these things to such-and-such a person." By this method the common people are satisfied that these things have reached the dead, and all those foods and things are taken to the house of some needy or pious Muslim.

The idea of the deceased himself enjoying these things has taken such hold in people's hearts that some ignorant women of the lower class themselves sit by the *fatiha* things, adorned and dressed up, thinking that when the late husband enjoys these foods and clothes, why should he be deprived of the pleasure of their own beauty?

In the *fatiha*s far more food is cooked than the *fatiha* needs, [illegible] which is distributed according to means among the relatives and friends with whom portions are exchanged. It is given to the household dependants (*parja*), the washerman, the barber, the sweeper (*halal-khor*) and so on, who, because the *fatiha*s have become splendid ceremonies, have established rights of their own.

Although we have described all these proceedings in connection with the *fatiha* of the *soyam*, they are carried out with greater importance at the *chehlum*, which is said to take place on the fortieth day after death, but in actual practice takes place two to four days short of forty. And although the *fatiha*s of the tenth and twentieth days are also held with distinction, and every Thursday has become fixed for the *fatiha* of the elders of the family, the *fatiha*s of the *soyam* and *chehlum* are generally held with extraordinary care. And among the Imamis (Shi'as), at the *fatiha* of every death there is necessarily also a *majlis* of mourning for the Family of the Cloak (*Al-e 'Aba*), upon them be peace.

We have described the particular features of the ceremonies of mourning. As for the manner of sitting at the gatherings, it is the same as has been set out for the other ceremonies. These were the ceremonies of joy and mourning which are current as moral and social customs. The gatherings which religion has brought into use we shall describe next.
