Now we wish also to point out briefly which things the social life of Lucknow advanced by its need and appreciation, and which arts flourished here. In this connection many things will be mentioned, but we begin first with earthen vessels.

Earthen vessels are the world's first invention. Potsherds of ancient times have been dug up from every country and every land, which proves that man came to know how to bake clay in a kiln and make pottery at a very early stage of his advance. And probably in the Stone Age of the world, before the metals were extracted from the earth, man had learned to make vessels and bake them. In Egypt earthen vessels of the age of the Pharaohs, and in Babylon and Nineveh very well-baked bricks along with vessels for food and water, have been found. In the period of the Pharaohs the coffins in which the nobles of Egypt laid the bodies after making them into mummies were of clay. Not only this; the former world went on using potsherds and shards for a long time in place of paper.

The people of India too had learned this art in ancient times, and from the vessels of antiquity that have been found it appears that here too this art had advanced no less than in other places. In particular idol worship laid among the Hindus the foundation of clay images, which went on advancing day by day. And here there arose a caste of *kumhar*s (potters), whose family and ancestral profession is to make clay vessels and toys and bake them.

In Delhi the Islamic period produced a new group of *kasgar*s (*kasa-gar*s, bowl-makers), more advanced than the ordinary *kumhar*s, who are Muslims and make toys as well as vessels. And although the law of Islam declares the making of images absolutely unlawful, since this work is the *kasgar*s' means of livelihood, they are to some extent compelled to make and sell toys. The Muslim *kasgar*s are more advanced than the *kumhar*s in general social life and polish, and also in their art.

The Muslim nobles brought these *kasgar*s too with them from Delhi to Lucknow, and through the fondness of the nobles their craft began to advance here more, and markedly. Thus both *kumhar*s and *kasgar*s began to show in their work the same intelligence, ingenuity and invention that a painter shows in pictures and a poet in verses.

By good fortune the clay of Lucknow proved suitable for this art, which began to give the craftsmen the opportunity to display their accomplishments. And both vessels and toys began to be made such as could be made nowhere else. In vessels the advance was that such light, thin and clean, and with that well-shaped, vessels are made here as can be made nowhere. The clay of Amroha too is more suitable for this work, so that this art is making great advance there too, and there is a difference in style between the vessels and flower-vases of the craftsmen there and the vessels of Lucknow, and most people think that the work of the Lucknow craftsmen stands higher in the eyes of the refined.

Among ordinary things the pitchers (*ghara*) and *badhni*s (spouted pots) of Lucknow are lighter and more handsome than the pitchers and *badhni*s of all India. The roundness of the pitchers is most complete and perfect within its limits. The *badhni*s come very close to the shape of copper *lota*s. Among vessels, glazed earthenware (*sifali*) better than that of this place will perhaps rarely be found anywhere. But since the custom of eating from earthen vessels has entirely died out, the potters' attention has now turned away from them and decreases day by day. But the vessels in which the *kasgar*s here have given the highest proof of their accomplishments are *abkhora*s, *surahi*s, *jhajhri*s and *huqqa*s, and after them the *khir* *handi*s.

*Abkhora*s are vessels for drinking water. Although light, handsome and fine glasses of glass and enamel, and also the glasses and bowls of Moradabad and other places, have come very widely into use, yet in India there comes a summer season when water can give no pleasure in any vessel but earthen *abkhora*s, because water stays cool in them, and their own coolness gives the hand and lips such a pleasure of freshness as can be had from nothing else. Besides this, in new unglazed earthen *abkhora*s there is such a soul-reviving fragrance that the fondness for it led to the invention here of the perfume of earth (*mitti ka 'itr*). In short, this need kept the *abkhora*s alive, and all kinds of refinements were produced in them. *Abkhora*s were made so delicate, light and slight that they are called *kaghazi* (paper-thin), and are so fine that the lightness and fineness of the earthen *abkhora*s here put to shame even the delicacy of glass tumblers. Then designs are made on them and a coat of sand is applied so as to keep the water cooler. To suit them, matching saucers were invented. At last the shape of the *abkhora*s became so handsome and attractive that it must be seen. And the age had to acknowledge that the mastery which human craftsmanship has shown in the use of fusible metals it can also show in clay.

After *abkhora*s, among the vessels for keeping and cooling water are *surahi*s. The *surahi* is a very old thing, which was customary in ancient Iran and Egypt too. But the *surahi*s of Lucknow, through the excellence of the clay and the delicate taste of the craftsmen, became fine, paper-thin and very light. And then their form too became so beautiful that in both these respects the *surahi*s of no other place can rival them. Such a well-proportioned curve came into their mouths that the mouth alone of the *surahi*s of Lucknow is a thing that can be seen nowhere else. The *jhajhri*s too are just as delicate and light. Their belly is indeed like that of the *surahi*s, but on top, instead of a long neck, a *thingar* [TN: sense uncertain; apparently a short stubby neck] is fixed. In work, delicacy and fineness they too are no less than the *surahi*s.

*Huqqa*s. In these too there is an extreme need for coolness, so that the smoke may come cool. Paper-thin earthen *huqqa*s began to be made here so fine and well-shaped as can be had nowhere else. Then from new, freshly *anwase* [TN: *anwasa*, apparently "seasoned" or "first used"; sense uncertain] unglazed *huqqa*s, along with coolness and refinement in the smoke, such a fine fragrance of fresh clay arises that many high-ranking gentlemen of the royal period found no pleasure in any *huqqa* but these. 'Azimullah Khan, producing further beauty and refinement in them, left behind the '*Azimullah-khani* *huqqa*s as his memorial, which to this day are better, lighter and more generally popular than all kinds of earthen *huqqa*s. I once heard of the Poet Laureate of London, Lord Tennyson, that he was so fond of white clay pipes, which are called "clay pipes", and that his poetic love of refinement was so addicted to new pipes, that a basket full of new and untouched pipes was kept before him. He would take one pipe, fill it with tobacco, smoke it, and in a few minutes break it and throw it into another basket. Then when there was need again he would take another pipe, and after a few puffs break that too and throw it away. In this way he would sit all day filling, smoking and breaking new pipes [TN: the author reports this anecdote at second hand]. My view is that if Lord Tennyson had got the '*Azimullah-khani* *huqqa*s of Lucknow, he would have forgotten those clay pipes, because the coolness, refinement and excellence that are in their smoke are not to be found in clay pipes by a long way.

*Khir* *handi*s. Cooking pots are made everywhere, but the *handi*s of Lucknow are as true an imitation of copper *patili*s as will be found nowhere else. In particular the rose-coloured (*gulabi*) *handi*s, which are made for distributing *khir* and the like in portions, are made paper-thin and very beautiful like the *abkhora*s and *surahi*s. In them many delicate nobles now also keep *gilauri*s, because in the summer season *khasdan*s become burning hot and the *gilauri*s in them too become very hot, but in these *handi*s they stay so cool and acquire such an earthy fragrance that they become most refreshing. But even more than in vessels, the potters showed mastery in toys and clay images. The art of carving images, by grace of idol worship, is very old. The Egyptians, Babylonians and Iranians, the Greeks and Romans, all showed accomplishments in this art in their own times, examples of which can be seen today in the famous museums of Europe. In particular the people of Greece showed such mastery in carving stone images and maintaining the proportion of the limbs that even the present age, despite its endless advances, is amazed at their dexterity. And the images they made are considered the best "models" or standards for present-day sculptors and painters. But the craftsmanship that the unlettered, ignorant potters here are showing in clay toys, in maintaining the proportion of the limbs and making a true copy of nature, is not a whit less than the mastery of Greece. Looking at a man, they make a complete image of him as large as his body. Then in small images they make such likenesses true to the original of people of every style and every class that in their mastery one finds a trace of poetic subtlety. At Diwali the Hindus buy and distribute toys in great numbers, and out of this need, every year at this season, the potters here get the opportunity to display new inventions, ingenuities and subtleties in their art.

The various groups and sets of images that these potters have prepared must be seen. An English band, troupes of courtesans and *bhand*s, the gathering of old Nawabs, the courts of nobles, assemblies of various craftsmen, have a special character. Once, on the occasion of an exhibition, a potter here made an Indian village, in which, after showing within the settlement, among shops and houses, the coming and going of people of various styles and the passing of bullocks and carts, he showed in the surrounding fields the peasants ploughing and bringing water to the fields by channels. The flowing of water in the channels, and even the little ripples in it, were shown, and it was prominently shown that the bullocks working at the ploughs were very thin and their ribs could be clearly seen. In the same way I also saw a picture of the Lucknow of the royal period, in which the plan of the population, lanes and mounds of that time was shown. But alas, all these labours, as a passing enthusiasm, appear for a few days and vanish, and there is no place where examples of all these artistries are preserved. In London there is a museum of wax figures by the name of "Madame Tussaud's Exhibition", in which life-size figures of every kind of all the famous people of the age, and also those in which the artist has shown some particular mastery, have been collected. Some of the figures are such that it is impossible that every visitor should not be deceived by one figure or another. If such a museum of these clay images were established here and all the craftsmanship of the potters collected in it, my view is that besides being exceedingly useful for the advance of the art, it would also be profitable. A ticket could be fixed for admission to it, and my view is that no visitor from outside would leave without seeing it. But the trouble is that we ourselves have no taste and no zeal, and in everything we wish to remain dependent on the Government. If some wealthy young nobleman were to take a fancy to this instead of to debauchery, what a source of fame and service to the homeland it could be!

Toys of this kind have often been collected in museums, but they are very limited, and the rank of this craft in Lucknow is not merely such that a few toys should be kept among other wonders of the age. Here there should be a permanent exhibition of toys and clay images.
