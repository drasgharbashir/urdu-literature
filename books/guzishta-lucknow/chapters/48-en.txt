In the meantime the dowry (*jahez*) goods are brought out. Their list (*fard*) is brought and presented to the bridegroom's people, in which are entered all the jewellery, suits, vessels and [illegible] that are to be given. All the things are checked against the list, and now the bride is quite ready to depart. Her dress is no heavy worked suit; rather she wears a *kurta* of *tanzeb* over *suha*, that is, *tol*, and plain silk trousers. And in this too simplicity is so observed that not even an edging is put on, and the waist-cord (*izarband*) is of plain *nara*.

While she is being adorned and dressed, the *domni*s keep singing the "*babul*", that is, the song of leaving her parents' home, which is most full of longing and heart-melting. A strange scene of grief and sorrow is created. Everyone is sad and sorrowful. All the relatives, acquaintances and friends of the family embrace the girl one by one and bid her farewell with words of tender feeling. She herself weeps bitterly. And the *pinas* is brought up to the gateway. At this time the bridegroom is again called inside to come and take away his bride. He comes, lifts the bride in his arms and seats her in the *pinas*.

Before the departure, in the women's quarters the bridegroom is given *salam-kara'i* (greeting gifts), and all the relatives and kinsfolk, friends and acquaintances give according to their means. At the same time outside there is the *sharbat-pila'i* (serving of sherbet), in which a decanter of sherbet and a glass are brought merely as a custom; no one drinks, but all those present at the gathering put money into the sherbet tray according to their means and ability. And in this way whatever money is collected inside and outside in *salam-kara'i* and *sharbat-pila'i* is given to the bridegroom.

Now the *barat* sets out back towards the bridegroom's house with the same pomp and the same splendour. In the addition made to the procession on this return, first of all there is the bride's *pinas*, which stays in front of the bridegroom's horse and is most distinguished. An elaborate *chhapka* (canopy) is spread over it. On both sides women bearers (*kahari*s) walk alongside holding the *chhapka*. And round it is a crowd of the bridegroom's servants or particular people. And after the bridegroom come the *pinas*es of all the other women who accompany them.

The most prominent thing in this procession is the dowry goods. All these goods go behind the whole procession and musicians and in front of the bride's *pinas*, in this order: each copper vessel is placed in a separate basket (*changer*) and is in the hand of a separate labourer. China and glass vessels are set on trays (*kishti*). … … [TN: these elision marks are in the source edition.] After this come the boxes and so on, in which are the bride's suits. After them comes the bed, in which all the equipment, silk mattress, quilt, pillows, sheet, is ready, and the bedding is tied to the legs with silk cords, and at both ends of the cords hang silver tassels of a special style. The girl is given all the equipment of domestic life: mirror, comb, the necessary things for adornment, oil, perfume. And if there are the means, a silver *pandan*, *khasdan*, *lota*, *katora* and some other things are given. In any case all these goods are between the bands and the procession of the *barat* and the bridegroom. And behind everything, on *doli*s, are the cauldrons (*deg*) of food. This is called the food of the *bhaura* [TN: the bride's arrival], which the girl's people generally give to the bridegroom.

When the *barat* reaches the bridegroom's house in this state, music of rejoicing (*shadiyana*) plays. The *domni*s, having arrived beforehand, begin to sing *banre*, which are particular wedding songs. And in the clamour of these congratulations the bride is set down. In some families here too it is the bridegroom who lifts her down in his arms, and in some households the bridegroom's mother and sisters come and lift her down. As soon as she is taken inside and seated, the bridegroom is made to say a prayer of thanksgiving (*namaz-e shukrana*) on her skirt. The bride's feet are washed and the water is thrown into the four corners of the house. The *ru-numa'i* takes place, in which all the women and related men give money or jewellery with open hearts and look at her face, uncovering it.

This first night in the new house is for the bride a night to be passed under the strictest restrictions and bashfulness. She can neither speak to anyone, nor talk, nor look at anyone fully. Except to the women who have come with her she can say nothing to anyone. And to save her from this trouble, at daybreak her brother or some other relative arrives to take the *chauthi* (fourth-day visit), and as far as possible mounts her and takes her away early. This time too, although the bride goes with distinction and dignity, there is no need for a procession and band. The bridegroom too goes with the bride, and with him go seven kinds of vegetables and seven kinds of sweets.

The day passes, and that very night the *chauthi* is played in the bride's house. Taking off the *bar* (bridal) suit, the bride is dressed in the *charhawa* suit, which is heavier, more worked and more elaborate than all the suits. Dressing her in this suit, she is thoroughly adorned. From the bridegroom's side his sisters and related women also come, and in this gathering the bridegroom and bride fight each other with sweets, and the bridegroom's companions and the bride's women with vegetables and sticks of flowers; that is, they pelt each other with sweets and vegetables, and deal blows with the sticks. Sometimes in the fun the fight grows fierce too, and some women even receive slight injuries.

Two or four days after the *chauthi*, the bride comes again to the bridegroom's house, and after this there are generally four *chale*. The word *chala* comes from *chal* and *chalna* (going). The meaning is that the bride is invited from her in-laws' house, but this invitation is not to her parents' home itself but among the women of her parents' family. That is, her maternal aunts, paternal aunts and maternal uncles' wives, making the effort, invite her to their homes in turn, where she goes together with the bridegroom. And special care and arrangements are made for the entertainment of this new couple. The bridegroom and bride remain guests for only one day and night, and when they are sent off they are given a suit, *salam-kara'i*, jewellery and so on according to ambition and means.

This is the wedding of the people of Lucknow, of which, leaving out many customs, a summary outline has been shown to the readers of *Dilgudaz*. The manner of the villagers' wedding is changed in all other things except the marriage contract. There too there is the *manjha*, but the yellow *manjha* suit for the bridegroom is brought by his sisters and related women. The *manjha* does not come from the bride's house with pomp, procession and band. Neither does the *sachaq* come from the bridegroom's house, nor the *mehndi* from the bride's house; rather the purpose of the *sachaq* and *mehndi* is fulfilled in another way on the day of the *barat* itself. That is, when the *barat* reaches the bride's place, it halts a little distance from her house. From there, instead of the *sachaq*, under the name of *bari*, the bride's suit, and with it many other suits and the things of wifehood (*suhag*) which are considered necessary, some sugar and some parched rice (*khilen*) set out on trays, are sent with a band to the bride's door. The bridegroom's relatives and friends go with them, who show all these things openly to the bride's people and hand them over, and after drinking sherbet come back.

A little after this, in the same way, a *bari* comes from the bride's side, in which is the bridegroom's suit. This *bari* among the villagers takes the place of the *mehndi*. After this, putting on that suit, in which are the *jama*, *nima*, turban, *muqanna'* (veil), *sehra*, *badhi*s (garlands) of flowers, shoes and so on, he sets out. Now the *barat* goes to the bride's door and halts at the place chosen for the *nikah* gathering. Here all night long a gathering of music, song, dance and singing is in full swing. In the last watch of the night, according to the custom of the time, the Qazi Sahib comes and reads the *nikah*. The manner of the *nikah* is the same as was described among the city people. After the contract the girl's people give the food of the *barat*. In the city, except for the food of the *bhaura*, it is not obligatory to give food to the *barat*; rather the bridegroom himself feeds and refreshes them and takes them along. But in the villages the most important duty of the girl's people is to feed the *barat*, in which if there is the slightest shortcoming, in their view their nose is cut (they are disgraced) in the brotherhood (*biradari*).

This food is a full *tora*, in which *pulao*, *zarda* (sweet saffron rice), *qorma*, *khamiri roti*s (leavened breads) and *shirmal* are obligatory. And a full *tora* is given to everyone high and low without exception or distinction. In taking the food the boy's people with utter shamelessness and immodesty ask for food for every ant, and demand grain and fodder for the horses and bullocks far beyond need. And it is the duty of the girl's people that "no" should not come from their tongues. If they refuse to give anything, their honour is laid in the dust and all that they have done is ruined.

After this the manner of departure and return is almost the same as among the city people. But there is also a custom that in the villages women do not go with the *barat*, nor does any honoured lady come with the bride. One or two women of low degree do indeed come along in the capacity of midwife and maidservant. Besides this, in the villages very great hardships are also imposed on the bride. It is her duty that until the *chauthi*, down to the last moment, she should remain in her in-laws' house just as she is placed; she should neither eat, nor drink, nor go to relieve herself, nor speak, nor move, nor take her hand from her face, nor open her eyes, because all these things count as shamelessness and immodesty. And for fear that the bride should need to relieve herself after going to her in-laws' house, her food and water are stopped two days beforehand. And the greater misery is that the village bride is often married into another village, and in going and coming two or three stages have to be travelled. It is evident what severe hardships the poor bride must go through in such a state.

The reason for the giving up of the *sachaq* and *mehndi* in the villages and for the strictness in feeding the *barat* is probably that the *barat* mostly travels and goes from one settlement to another, for which reason it is not possible that one day one procession should go from here and the next day another procession should come from there to here, and then on the third day the *barat* should set out. Similarly, although the bridegroom mostly feeds the members of the *barat* from his own house before taking them, by the time they reach the girl's house all the members of the *barat* are as hungry as famished Bengalis and begin to behave like paupers.
