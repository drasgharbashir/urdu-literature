# (17)

We still have to describe many particular features of Lucknow, which mostly concern moral matters and the affairs of social life; but it seems fitting to give some brief account of the arts of war too.

The truth is that this last court of the East was founded when the soldiering of the Muslims, and of Indians in general, had begun to weaken. Or, more correctly, the old arts of soldiering had not so much died out as the old arts and weapons of war had become useless against the new drill and the new arms of warfare. The result was that those old arts of war, instead of passing from the Muslims or the people of India to some new, advanced and brave nation and flourishing there, vanished from the world altogether, and vanished so completely that the present generation is wholly unacquainted with the brave deeds and martial accomplishments of its forefathers. And now that we have taken up the pen to describe these arts, there is hardly anyone to be found from whom anything can be learned. We are most grateful to Prince Mirza Masud Qadr Bahadur, B.A., and to a very old elder of Lucknow, Sulaiman Khan Sahib (who is of the line of Hafiz Rahmat Khan Sahib, the famous former ruler of Bareilly), for what we are writing about these old arts of war we write with their help.

The arts of soldiering that grew up in Delhi, and after Delhi in Lucknow, really came from three different peoples, and from the mingling of the three they had advanced suitably. And the astonishing thing is that in spite of this mingling their original distinctions remained to the end. Some arts came from the warriors of the Aryan people, some were brought by the Turks and the brave men of Tartary, and some were the special arts of the Arabs, which had come here by way of Iran. The arts that were current in Lucknow, and of which accomplished masters were present here, appear to be the following:

(1) *Lakri* (stick fighting), (2) wielding the *patta* (gauntlet sword), (3) *bank* (knife fighting), (4) *banaut* (disarming), (5) wrestling, (6) the spear, (7) the *bana* (whirling staff), (8) archery, (9) the *katar* (punch dagger), (10) *jal-bank* (knife fighting in water).

## (1) Lakri (stick fighting)

This original art, which is called *phankaiti*, belonged to the Aryans and was current among the Aryans of both countries, India and Iran. After the Arab conquests, Iran's *phankaiti* came under the influence of Arab warfare, and the *phankaiti* there advanced further than India's. In India both these arts survived to the end in their distinct forms, and both schools were established in Lucknow. The Arab-influenced *phankaiti* of Iran was known here as *Ali-madd*, and the pure Indian *phankaiti* was called by the title *Rustam-khani*. In *Ali-madd* the fighter's (*phankait*'s) left foot stayed planted in one place, and the stances were changed only by moving the right foot forward and back. In *Rustam-khani*, by contrast, the fighter, when changing stance, moved right and left, forward and back as much as he wished or had room for, and suddenly fell upon his opponent. There was this further distinction, that the art of *Ali-madd* was reserved for nobles and gentlemen. Its masters never made a man of low or common class their pupil, nor let him learn their art. The art of *Rustam-khani*, by contrast, was common among the lowborn and the lower classes.

A formidable master of *Ali-madd* was attached at Faizabad to the household of Shuja-ud-Daula Bahadur, and after him to that of his widow, Bahu Begum Sahiba. He is mentioned in the history of Faizabad, and it appears that he was the very first master of this art, who lived at Faizabad and then came to Lucknow. A second master of the same art was Muhammad Ali Khan, who lived in our own quarter, Katra Bizan Beg Khan, and was regarded as the inventor of *Ali-madd*. The third master was Mir Najm-ud-Din, who went first to Benares with the princes of Delhi and from there came to Lucknow. His rule was to take only gentlemen as pupils, and on taking pupils he accepted wealth from princes and only sweets from gentlemen, and instead of using it himself he would take it and present it as an offering to the Sayyids of the line of Fatima. He lived in the time of Nawab Asaf-ud-Daula. A very great master was Mir Ata Husain, one of Hakim Mahdi's intimates. Another very great master was Biche Baz Khan, who because of his mastery became famous in the time of Ghazi-ud-Din Haidar as the inventor and founder of *Ali-madd*. It is said of him that he was a convert to Islam, but his way too was never to teach his art to any man of the lower classes, only to gentlemen. He left as his memorial in Lucknow a mosque that still stands beyond the Dhaniya Mahri bridge near Alamnagar.

*Rustam-khani* remained among the common people, and for that reason it was not peculiar to either Hindus or Muslims; hundreds of its masters were spread through all the villages and small towns of Awadh. Even so, the mastery and renown that Yahya Khan, son of Muhammad Siddiq Khan, won in *Rustam-khani* in Lucknow no one else attained. Nawab Fatahyab Khan, though one of the nobles of high rank, was also a great calligrapher, and had attained mastery in *Rustam-khani* too. Likewise a famous *banka* (swaggering bravo) wrestler of Lucknow, Babbar Langar Baz, was also a master of *Rustam-khani*. And if some little practice of it still survives, it is among the common people. The art of *Ali-madd* was reserved for gentlemen, and gentlemen have no longer anything to do with soldiering, so that art too has died out. *Rustam-khani* belonged to the common people, and they still fight and brawl today, so the practice of *Rustam-khani* survives among them to this day.

I saw one or two masters of *Ali-madd* at Matiya Burj, and the very last was Mir Fazl Ali, who lived in the quarter of Mahmudnagar.

## (2) Wielding the patta

The real purpose of this art was that if a man fell into a ring of enemies, he could hurl his blows on every side, drive them all back, evade them all, and break out striking them all. Leaping by planting the *patta* was the special accomplishment of this art, and the highest praise was that if ten arrows came at a man at once he could cut them all down. This art did not exist in Delhi. It came to Lucknow from the East, and was most current among the weavers, although in the end many gentlemen too, especially the Shaikhzadas of the small towns, took it up. Gauri Biche Baz, son of Ghulam Rasul Khan, was regarded as the greatest master of this art in Lucknow, and hundreds of stories about him were current among the people. But alas, even these tales are now being forgotten by the present generation.

Mir Rustam Ali's *patta* had an edge on both sides [TN: the text reads "سینے" (breast), evidently a misprint for "پٹے" (patta)], and wielding it he would cut his way through hundreds of opponents. A Shaikhzada of Asiwan, Shaikh Muhammad Husain, wielded the *patta* with both hands. So one day in the time of Ghazi-ud-Din Haidar the Resident Sahib Bahadur and some European guests wished to see the skill of some master of this art. Shaikh Muhammad Husain presented himself. Since he did not have his *patta* with him at the time, a splendid *patta* inlaid and studded with jewels was given him from the royal armoury, and taking it he displayed such feats that cries of applause rose on every side. And in the excitement of this applause and acclaim he went on wielding the *patta*, walked out of the gathering and went off, and reached his home. It was a saying among the people of the art that one who knows how to wield the *patta* will not let even ten swordsmen come near him.

A master of this same art in Lucknow was Mir Vilayat Ali Danda-tor (Stick-breaker). It was said of him that however stout a stick was in his opponent's hand, he would break it.

## (3) Bank (knife fighting)

Among the arts of war this was a very important and most useful art, which in principle ranked above the other arts, and young gentlemen learned it with particular effort and particular zeal. The real purpose of this art is to meet an opponent with knives. This art existed from ancient times both among the Hindus and among the Arabs, but their knives were different. The Hindus' knife was straight, with an edge on both sides, and the Arabs' knife was curved like a dagger, with an edge on one side only. But the last knife of the Arabs is the *jambiya*, which has four edges on four sides for some distance from the point, and makes such a four-sided wound that it is said to be hard to stitch. In short, the art of fighting with this weapon is called *bank*.

It is taught in this way: master and pupil sit face to face on their knees. But in teaching the Hindus' straight knife the rule was that both, while sitting on their knees opposite each other, kept one knee raised, while in teaching the Arabs' knife they sat fully on their knees. Along with the blows there were very formidable holds, before which the holds of wrestling were nothing. This difference too is mentioned, that in the Arabs' art there were seven basic blows and in the Hindus' nine. In the Arabs' *bank*, once a hold was fully locked, it was beyond the power of the one who had locked it to leave his opponent alive; while in the art of the Indians it remained in one's power to the end to release the hold and spare the opponent whenever one wished.

In this art there are not only blows but very formidable holds, in which both opponents stayed locked together for hours, trying with hold after hold to bind each other and wound him. The holds of this art were so true, so lofty and so principled that it was said that all the holds of wrestling and stick fighting came from *bank*. It was a saying among the masters of *bank* that *bank* is complete lying down, half when sitting, and only a quarter when standing. It should not be thought that the work of the *bankait* (knife fighter) is only to wound his opponent with the knife. No: his real work is to bind his opponent alive, render him helpless and bring him in captive.

Another particular feature was that the man of *bank* kept his art hidden as far as possible. Nothing in his dress, appearance or manners revealed that he was a fighting man. The *bankait*s kept the appearance of ordinary sober gentlemen, wore slippers and carried no weapon; indeed they had a vow not to keep even an iron penknife or a needle about them. They carried only a handkerchief, in one corner of which an iron ring was tied. Just this weapon served them in time of need. Or if they observed still greater refinement, they kept a rosary in their hand with a clumsy iron *qibla-numa* (compass) attached to it. Just this weapon was enough for them.

Among the Hindus this art had from ancient times belonged to the Brahmins alone. The Rajputs did not know it. The Brahmins did not teach them, nor did they try to learn it, thinking it contrary to their character. The reason probably was that sobriety was a condition of being a *bankait*, and the Rajputs were open soldiers. Instead of a compass or an iron ring the Brahmin *bankait*s kept a key tied to their sacred thread (*janeu*), and with it they would finish off an enemy with the greatest refinement and composure. Prince Mirza Humayun Qadr Bahadur says that this art came to Lucknow in the time of Shah Alam, when Mirza Khurram Bakht Bahadur came to Benares and brought one or two masters of this art with him. But we have learned from a reliable source, and from looking at the history of Faizabad, that a master of this art, Mansur Ali Khan Bankait, had come to Faizabad as early as Shuja-ud-Daula's time.

In the time of Nawab Asaf-ud-Daula the master of *bank* in Lucknow was Shaikh Najm-ud-Din. At about the same time another master of *bank* lived in Lucknow, known by the name of Mir Bahadur Ali. He used to claim: "Release a wild pigeon under a bedstead and watch. If it gets out from any side and flies away, take it that I am no *bankait*." This was not peculiar to him; this is the very definition of *bank*, and every master could claim it. A third master in Lucknow was Vali Muhammad Khan. In the time of Nasir-ud-Din Haidar the name of Mir Abbas, the pupil of a pupil of Shaikh Najm-ud-Din, was famous, and four of his pupils became renowned, one of whom was a dacoit, while the other three were cultivated gentlemen. The last master of this art was Mir Jafar Ali, who after the ruin of Lucknow went with Wajid Ali Shah to Matiya Burj. I saw him, and in my childhood I myself became his pupil, but I gave it up after learning for a month or two, and what I learned has remained like a dream. I do not know now whether anyone who knows it is still left.

## (4) Banaut (disarming)

The real purpose of this art is to knock a sword, a staff or whatever weapon it may be out of the opponent's hand, and with a handkerchief in which a coin is tied, or with one's bare hand, to deal the opponent such a blow that he is finished. It was said in Lucknow from the beginning that the great and formidable masters of this art were in Hyderabad in the Deccan, and on going there and enquiring I found that this art is indeed still alive there to some extent. Those who know say that if the man of the art who fights standing is unarmed, it is wrestling; if he has a knife in his hand, it is *bank*; and if he has a two-yard staff or a handkerchief in his hand, it is *banaut*. The men of *banaut* too keep their art hidden, and have a mutual pledge to teach it only to gentlemen, and they take from the pupil a pledge that he will never use his weapon against a man who is weaker or harmless. The stances of the men of *banaut*, which they call *paon-le*, demand the very highest agility and boundless precision, which older men cannot attain. Besides this, the men of *banaut* have a complete knowledge of all the veins and sinews of the human body, and know well at what spot pressing with just a finger or an ordinary blow will leave a man helpless and breathless. Although Hyderabad was famous for this art, there were many masters of it in Lucknow too. It is said that Muhammad Ibrahim Khan first brought it here from Rampur. Talib Sher Khan was a very formidable *banka* here and a master of the sword. When he heard Ibrahim Khan's claim, he took his sword and made ready to fight him. Muhammad Ibrahim Khan too accepted the contest. As soon as Talib Sher Khan struck with his sword, Muhammad Ibrahim Khan struck with his handkerchief, which had a coin tied in its corner, with such skill that the sword flew from Talib Sher Khan's hand and fell with a clang far away. He was left speechless, and everyone acknowledged Muhammad Ibrahim Khan's mastery.

After this the art remained in Lucknow to the end, so that even at Matiya Burj a man named Muhammad Mahdi, who was the steward (*daroga*) of Nawab Mashuq Mahal's household, was regarded as an accomplished master of *banaut*.

## (5) Wrestling

This art belonged specially to the Aryans, both in India and in Iran. The Arabs and Turks were wholly unacquainted with it. Among the ancient inhabitants of India too, who were there before the Aryans, no trace of this art is found. In Lucknow the holds and methods of overcoming an opponent developed greatly, but wrestling depends essentially on bodily strength, and in strength the people of Lucknow, try as they may, can never compete with the people of the eastern lands [TN: the sense requires "western"; Punjab lies to the north-west of Lucknow], especially of the Punjab. Nature has not given the air and water of Lucknow the capacity to produce from its soil elephant-bodied wrestlers like Ghulam and his kind. So the art of wrestling in Lucknow displayed only skill in holds and counter-holds, by which at most one could overcome a man twice one's size, but to overthrow anyone stronger than that was impossible. Stories of the wrestling-pits (*akhara*) of Lucknow and its wrestlers of old are very famous, but all of them for holds and counter-holds, not for strength. Once I saw a bout between a famous wrestler here, Sayyid, and a Punjabi wrestler twice his size. There is no doubt that Sayyid's fighting was very beautiful from the start. His turning and twisting and his agility were praiseworthy, but the end was that within an hour Sayyid was drenched in sweat, his strength had given out and he was out of breath, while the Punjabi wrestler, who was playing with him, was not affected at all. At last Sayyid himself left the ring and ran off, admitting defeat without a fight.

## (6) The spear

This is an old art of warfare, found among the Aryans, the Turks and the Arabs alike. The Arabs' spear was long, and its head three-sided. The Turks' spear was short, with a round pointed head, that is, conical. And the spear of the Aryans of India was long, but its head was thin, edged and shaped like a *pan* (betel) leaf. And the remarkable thing is that all three kinds of spear were found in Lucknow. The large spears were five yards long and the small spears three yards. The merit of a large spear was that it should be very supple, so much so that it would bend double, and the merit of the small ones was that they should have not a trace of suppleness. And accordingly the arts of wielding the two were different. The famous and original spearman (*barchhait*) of Lucknow was Mir Kallu, whose name had already shone in the time of Burhan-ul-Mulk. After him Mir Akbar Ali Barchhait became famous. Then many spearmen began to come from Bareilly and Rampur. In the time of Ghazi-ud-Din Haidar, when the King took a liking to hunting elephants, those who knew the art of the spear were greatly valued, and in battles this weapon came to be of the most use. Alas, this ancient weapon, with which great ancient nations had won renown, still survives in Lucknow in abundance today, real or imitation, but serves only for wedding processions.

## (7) The bana

This art too belonged to the lower classes, and to some extent survives even now. The strokes and blows of fighting with the *lath* (staff) came from it. The aim and object of the *bana* too is that a man whirling a *bana* or *lath* should break out of a ring of enemies. *Bana* was the name of a long stick with a knob at one end; some had knobs at both ends, and whirled it so that no one could come near. Some tied cloth round the knobs, soaked them in oil and set them alight, and whirled them in such a way that the fire did not touch them at all, while the enemy kept far off because of the fire.

## (8) Archery

This is the old weapon of all the warlike nations of the world and the gun of ancient times, in which great feats were displayed, and gentle and common alike thought training in it essential. It is the weapon with which Raja Ramchandra-ji and his brother Lachhman-ji struck down Ravan and his mountain-bodied champions. Although the invention of the gun had reduced its importance, archery was still considered the highest accomplishment of soldiering. Bows were kept so stiff that drawing the string was not easy for everyone; indeed the stiffer a man's bow, the further his arrow flew and the deadlier it was. In the days of their conquests the Arabs displayed amazing feats of archery. At the conquest of Damascus an Arab bride named Umm Aban, married only five or ten days, in revenge for her martyred bridegroom rained such powerful arrows that the first arrow struck down the enemy's standard-bearer, and the second pierced the eye of the enemy's brave commander, Thomas, so firmly that no one could pull it out, and at last the shaft was cut off and the head left in the eye.

The Pasis and Bhars of Awadh knew this art well from early times. Then new masters came from Delhi. And in the time of Asaf-ud-Daula, Ustad Faiz Bakhsh, at a sign from the ruler, shot an arrow at Mirza Haidar's father, who was coming along riding an elephant, with such speed that no one saw him take aim and the man himself knew nothing of it, though the arrow had broken through his waistband (*patka*) and passed out. He remained unaware to the end. On reaching home he undid his waistband and found it soaked in blood, and at that moment a fountain of blood spurted from the wound and he died in an instant.

The method of teaching it was difficult too, but now this art has perished among all the civilised nations of the world, because modern firearms have made it entirely useless. But it survives to this day among the wild tribes of India, who use arrows generally for hunting and killing wild beasts, and sometimes also in fighting among themselves.

## (9) The katar

This was an old weapon belonging specially to the Aryan people, and in later times it was used mostly by thieves and robbers. With it one did not attack an opponent after challenging him, but attacked him while he was off guard. Probably for this reason gentlemen in Delhi, and especially in Lucknow, had entirely given up using it. Everyone wore a *katar*, but no one knew how to fight or strike with it. The mark of skill in striking with it was that one could strike whenever one wished without leaving even a scratch on the enemy's body, and, whenever one wished, drive it through up to the hilt. With it thieves often attacked an unwary and sleeping opponent at night and finished him off in secret.

## (10) Jal-bank

This was the same art of *bank* described above, combined with swimming. The aim was to get the better of an enemy in deep water and bring him back bound, or finish him off in the water itself. There is no mention of it anywhere else in history, but in Lucknow a master swimmer, Mirak Jan, invented it and taught it to hundreds of pupils. On the face of it, it was invented in Lucknow itself, and some masters of swimming here know it even today, while elsewhere there is no trace of this art at all.

We shall describe later the progress Lucknow made in swimming.
