# (52)

Although great advance was made in the *khasdan* for keeping *gilauri*s of *pan*, and no pains were spared over its beauty and charm, yet when it was seen that in the summer season tinned copper *khasdan*s become burning hot, and that keeping elaborate *gilauri*s in them, besides drying them, makes them so hot that eating them gives discomfort instead of refreshment and the mouth becomes dry instead of soothed, then for keeping them in this season new unglazed earthen *handi*s were adopted, in which the *pan*s stay cool, their freshness and refreshing quality increase further, and a most fragrant earthiness (*sondhapan*) arises in them. These paper-thin *handi*s are made in Lucknow so light, handsome and fine as a leaf as can be made nowhere else. When they are soaked in water, *gilauri*s placed in them and brought before one, the *pan* will be eaten later, but at the very sight of them freshness comes to the eyes.

Then the refinement of the nobles, with the thought that it is troublesome to soak them again and again, and that until they are wet with water there can be no pleasure in them, covered them with cloth so that the cloth should keep them wet. And since ordinary white cloth soon gets dirty, and from keeping *gilauri*s red stains appear on it here and there, red *tol* was stretched over them instead of white, which neither gets dirty quickly nor can the stains of *pan* disfigure it. For greater adornment, segments are made on the *tol* of these *handi*s with fine silver *dhanak* (narrow braid), things which have adorned and decked out the *pan* *handi*s and made them brides.

Copper *khasdan*s too are generally kept enclosed in covers, and covers of the same kind are customary for *pandan*s and *husn-dan*s, which are made elaborate with great care according to rank and condition, in which not only protection but also adornment is kept in mind.

In the same way *tol* with *dhanak* is also stretched over *surahi*s, owing to which the water in the *surahi*s stays well cooled, and at the very sight of them one wishes to drink water without thirst.

Those who eat *pan* often need to spit out the *pik* (red juice), for which getting up again and again is no small trouble. And then in rooms where an elaborate floor-covering is spread, a place to spit is found with difficulty and far off, and even if a place is found, the house is spoiled by the stains of the *pik*. So in connection with *pan* the need arose for another vessel for spitting. This vessel is called the *ugaldan*. The *ugaldan* is no new thing with a special connection with Lucknow. *Ugaldan*s were probably first invented in Delhi, and were transferred exactly as they were to Lucknow. Their shape was this: below a round base, above it a round top-shaped body, then above that a lotus-like mouth. These *ugaldan*s began to be made everywhere of copper, brass and zinc. At Bidar the incomparable *bidri* work of that place was made on them, and in Lucknow engraved work on copper. Then they were made at Moradabad and the fine engraving of that place was done on them. Then in Lucknow earthen *ugaldan*s of the same shape began to be made.

But the defect in them was that their lower part was light and the upper part, because of its greater spread, heavy. The result was that they often fell over through carelessness or inattention and the floor-covering was spoiled. To remove this fault, at Jaipur [TN: printed *Bepur*; probably Jaipur], Hyderabad, and after that at Moradabad, *ugaldan*s of another shape began to be made, which were perhaps an invention of Delhi too. Their shape is like the *hurak* (hourglass drum) of the *kahar*s or the *dugdugi* (small drum) of the juggler. And in Lucknow too many people had to adopt *ugaldan*s of this kind. Although the old style has not yet been given up here, and very large *ugaldan*s of that style are still made, now in many houses there are also ones of this new style. But the truth is that Lucknow has no special share in the invention and advance of the *ugaldan*, although its use in Lucknow is greater than in all the cities of India.

Now low and spread-out English spittoons of a new shape also come, which are of china, entirely of china, but they are probably for spitting while smoking cheroots. They are quite unsuitable for spitting out the juice of *pan*.

After the *khasdan*, among the equipment that accompanies nobles and well-to-do people there is also the water *lotiya*, which is kept with the attendants. Generally these are plain or engraved copper *lotiya*s of middling quality. Those to whom God has given the means, and with that whom nobility and wealth have also freed from observance of the *shari'a*, keep silver *lotiya*s with them.

The *lotiya* is an old thing of the time of the Hindus; it was a round vessel without a spout, whose mouth was smaller than its belly. And since there was often need to draw water from a well, the *lotiya* and cord always went with every traveller on a journey. And among the Hindus of the villages, and also among the lower-class Muslims there, it is still customary to this day in the same former style. The Muslims in their time fixed a spout to this *lotiya* to make the use of water easier.

I do not know whether this custom existed among the nobles of Delhi too. And those who were accompanied by attendants always had a *lotiya* with them too, which served for drinking water, rinsing the mouth and other needs. But Lucknow has had a great share in the present shape of the *lotiya* and its beauty, which we shall describe in connection with copper vessels.

In summer a fringed fan covered with coloured cloth was also kept with the attendants, and in later times an umbrella too became obligatory, which the servant held over his master's head in the sun.

Among the inner needs of houses, for washing the hands, the *sailabchi* (basin) and *aftaba* (ewer); and since soap was not in use, the *besan-dani* (gram-flour box) too were necessary things. The *sailabchi* and *aftaba* are old things of the wealthy households of India, which had been in use in Delhi since God knows when, and came to Lucknow in their ancient style and dignity. Here the *sailabchi* remained the same, and although now the *tasla* (basin) has come more into use in its place, the truth is that it cannot be a substitute for the *sailabchi*. The *sailabchi* is a round-bellied vessel whose mouth, made a little smaller, spreads round very widely in the form of a shallow dish, and over the mouth a perforated cover (*parda*) is placed, through which all the water falls in washing the hands. This cover can be lifted whenever one likes and cleaned thoroughly. A little grass is put on this perforated cover so that splashes should not fly as the water falls. A very great merit and refinement in it is that the dirty water, which is repulsive to look at and arouses disgust in those of refined temperament, does not remain before the eyes. But in place of the *aftaba* the *lota* became current in Lucknow. In fact the *aftaba* itself was the *lota* of olden times, on which the taste of Lucknow worked to produce the shapely form of the present *lota*. The shape of the old *lota* that was called *aftaba* was this: it was a conical vessel of copper in which there was no distinction between belly and neck. Whatever circumference it had near the base went on gradually decreasing upwards, at last becoming the neck itself, until the edges were turned over to make a mouth, and a curved spout was fixed on one side. *Lota*s of this shape are still to be found in Hyderabad today, which give proof of their antiquity and of being the first form of our *lota*s. Their shape was like the earthen water vessels of Egypt and Syria, or the china jug that stands on the washstand among the English. And from this it is thought that the Muslims must have brought it with them from Arabia and Iran. A few days later the influence of Indian civilisation made its first alteration in it, that the belly became round and separate and distinct from the neck. But owing to closeness to its origin the elongation remained, that is, the width and spread were not in proportion to the height. Up to that time the roundness of the belly too was not spherical but oval. This is the shape of the *aftaba* that is mentioned in the earlier *masnavi*s and tales of Urdu. In Lucknow what happened was that the belly changed from oval to spherical, and its circumference and spread increased in proportion to its height. A well-proportioned slope came into the necks, and the spout too became wide at the start and narrow near the tip, curved and very handsome. This is the present *lota* of Lucknow, than which no more handsome and shapely *lota*s are made in any city of India, and fanciers everywhere order and send for them from Lucknow. The proportion that has arisen here in *lota*s is seen in all of them, from the small *lutiya* to the largest *lota*.

A suitable alteration of the same kind has taken place in all copper vessels, which we shall describe later, because this is not the place to point it out.

The *besan-dani* is in fact a spoutless copper *lotiya* in which *besan* (gram flour) is kept filled. Generally after meals, to remove the greasiness, *besan* is taken from it and rubbed on, and then washed off with water. Some people, but very few, keep *ubtan* or *khali* (oil-cake) in the *besan-dani* instead of *besan*, because *besan* is a thing to eat, and wasting it in washing the hands is in their view unlawful or improper. But now this custom has become much rarer, because *ubtan* is not made except at weddings, and with *khali* its strong smell comes into the hands.
