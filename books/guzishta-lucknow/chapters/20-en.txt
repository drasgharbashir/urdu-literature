# (19)

The fighting of wild beasts in Lucknow was confined to the sovereign and the nobles of the court, for keeping them, preparing them, controlling them in a fight and protecting the spectators from harm by them are things beyond the means, never mind of the poor, of even great nobles. And so the fighting of wild beasts was seen in the neighbourhood of Lucknow only so long as the former court lasted. No sooner was that court dissolved than those fearful arenas were deserted too.

But the fighting of birds was not like this. Rich and poor alike could indulge a taste for it, and every enthusiast could, with effort, prepare a cock or a quail fit for fighting. The birds that were set to fight in Lucknow with enthusiasm and interest are as follows: (1) cocks, (2) quails, (3) partridges, (4) *lava*s (bush quails), (5) *guldum*s (bulbuls), (6) *lal*s (red munias), (7) pigeons, (8) parrots. Each of these games needs to be described separately. Lucknow's pigeon-flying and quail-fighting are generally well known, and today's educated people, devoted to modern civilisation, often make fun of them. They know nothing at all of the degree of perfection to which these people had brought each of these pastimes and games, making each a regular art. But when they go to Europe and see the same kind of frivolous pastimes there too, they will at least be ashamed of the words they so often blurt out about these enthusiasts of their own country.

## (1) Cock-fighting

Cocks of every kind and breed fight, but the cock kept specially for fighting is the *asil* (pure-bred) cock. And the truth is that there is no braver creature in the world than the *asil* cock. Even the lion does not truly have the bravery of the cock. He will die, but he will not turn away from a fight. The authorities here believe that the *asil* breed was brought from Arabia, and this seems likely too, for at the present time there are more and finer strains of *asil* in Hyderabad in the Deccan than anywhere else, and that is the one city in India where Arabs have settled and reside more than anywhere else. So the strains of cocks in India came by way of Iran. One of the famous cock-fighters of Lucknow used to relate that his cock had by chance lost a match. Broken-hearted, he went off to the land of Iraq, spent several months in worship at Najaf-e-Ashraf, and prayed day and night: "O Lord, for the sake of Your sinless Imams, grant me a cock that will lose to no one in a fight." One night he was given glad tidings in a dream: "Go into the wilderness." As soon as he woke in the morning he took the road to the hills and deserts, taking a hen with him. Suddenly a crowing came from a mountain gorge. He at once went near and let the hen loose; at the sound of her the cock came out, and he at once caught it by some trick and brought it back. Its breed was such that he never again had to be ashamed in the ring (*pali*).

The taste for cock-fighting continued here without a break from the time of Nawab Shuja-ud-Daula to the end. Nawab Asaf-ud-Daula was immensely fond of it. Nawab Saadat Ali Khan, for all his shrewdness, was devoted to cock-fighting. His enthusiasm had such an effect on society that, never mind the nobles of the court of Lucknow, even the Europeans then living here took up the same taste. So General Martin, whose mansion is a notable building of Lucknow and a school for European children, was a cock-fighter of the first rank, and Nawab Saadat Ali Khan used to lay wagers with him and set cocks to fight.

The method of cock-fighting in Lucknow was that the cock's spurs were bound so that it could not do harm with them. The beak was pared with a knife to make it sharp and pointed, and the two matched cocks were released into the ring. The cock-fighters stayed close behind them. Releasing a cock against another cock was itself an art, in which the aim was that one's own cock should get the chance to strike first. Now the two cocks began to fight with beaks and feet. The cock-fighters urged and incited their own cocks, shouting: "Yes, my son, well done!" "Yes, my son, cut him!" "Again, right there!" At these challenges and encouragements the cocks pressed forward striking with feet and beaks in such a way that it seemed they understood and did as they were told. When they had fought until they were wounded and exhausted, they were picked up for a while by agreement of both sides. In the language of cock-fighting this picking up is called *pani* (water). Then the cock-fighters wiped their wounded heads, sprayed them with water, sucked the wounds with their own mouths, and used such devices that within a few minutes a fresh ardour arose in the cocks, and refreshed, they were released into the ring again. In this way *pani* followed *pani*, and the fight ended after four or five days, sometimes after eight or nine, when one cock went blind or took such a blow that he could not rise, or for some other reason became unfit to fight, and was then considered to have lost. Or sometimes a cock's beak would break. In that case too, as far as possible, the cock-fighters would bind the beak and fight him.

The game in Hyderabad, unlike here, is very harsh. There the spurs are not bound; instead, they are pared with a knife into points like spear-heads, with the result that the fight is decided within an hour or an hour and a half. In Lucknow the method of binding the spurs was probably adopted so that the fight would be drawn out and could be enjoyed for longer.

In preparing cocks for fighting, the cock-fighter's skill shows, besides feeding and keeping, in massaging the limbs, in *phui*, that is, spraying with water, in shaping the beak and spurs or binding the spurs, and in removing fatigue. For fear that picking up grain from the ground might damage the beak, they are often fed grain from the hand.

This taste was at its height up to the time of Wajid Ali Shah. At Matiya Burj cock-fighting matches were held at Nawab Ali Naqi Khan's mansion, and some Englishmen used to bring their cocks from Calcutta to fight. Besides the kings, many other nobles too were fond of cock-fighting: Mirza Haidar; Nawab Salar Jang Haidar Beg Khan, brother of Bahu Begum Sahiba; Major Soares, who lived in the time of Nasir-ud-Din Haidar and set his cocks to fight against the King himself; Agha Burhan-ud-Din Haidar too was a lover of cock-fighting. At the house of the last-named noble there were two hundred to two hundred and fifty cocks up to the last, kept with the greatest cleanliness and refinement, and ten or twelve men were employed to look after them. Mian Darab Ali Khan was very fond of it. Nawab Ghasita too kept up this taste to the last. The respected Pathans of Malihabad were also very fond of it, and very good strains of *asil* cocks were preserved among them. There were many famous cock-fighters here who were regarded as unrivalled masters of their art: Mir Imdad Ali; Shaikh Ghasita; Munawwar Ali, who had the gift of telling from the sound of a cock's crow that it would win; Safdar Ali; and a first-class pensioner (*vasiqa-dar*), Sayyid Miran Sahib, was also famous. In this last period the following became famous: Tafazzul Ali Jamadar, Qadir, Jivan Khan, Husain Ali, Nauroz Ali, Nawab Muhammad Taqi Khan, a noble of high rank of Bayana, Mian Jan, Dil, Chhanga, Husain Ali Beg, Ahmad Husain. None of them is alive now.

These are the men who brought cock-fighting here to the highest degree of perfection. But I think the taste for cock-fighting is at present greater in Hyderabad in the Deccan. Many nobles, *jagirdar*s and *mansabdar*s there are fond of it, and they have matchless strains of cocks too, which they guard carefully.

## (2) Quail-fighting

The taste for quail-fighting came to Lucknow from the Punjab. Some Kanchans of the Punjab, whose women follow the profession of selling their virtue, came to Lucknow in the time of Nawab Saadat Ali Khan and brought with them *ghagas* quails, which they set to fight. Some famous courtesans of the present day are descended from these people. There are two kinds of quail: one the *ghagas* and the other the *cheng*. In the Punjab there is only the *ghagas* quail. It is bigger, stronger and more powerful than the *cheng*. In Lucknow there are both *ghagas* and *cheng*. The *cheng* is smaller and more delicate than the *ghagas*, but tougher in fighting and very spirited, and its fighting is grander and more beautiful. In any case, it was in Lucknow that it was discovered that the *cheng* quail is better suited for fighting.

For a quail fight no great arena was needed, nor even to leave the house and come out into the courtyard; one could sit with decorum on a clean carpet inside a room and watch the fight. So Lucknow society liked it very much. Very fine, beautiful and light cages were devised for quails, decorated with tiny ivory pegs, and the quails were kept in them.

The game goes like this. First, by *muth*, that is, by soaking it repeatedly in water and holding it pressed in the hands for hours, its wildness is removed, until it begins to call and peck. After this its body is conditioned by keeping it hungry and giving it purgatives, among which sugar candy (*misri*) is particularly used. Then late at night, or at midnight, someone shouts "Koo!" into their ears, which is called *kukna*. In short, by these devices the fat is pared away, the clumsiness removed, and the body becomes very agile and strong. This is the preparation of a quail, and the more fully it meets these requirements, the fitter you may take it to be for fighting.

At the time of the fight a little grain is scattered all round on the carpet and the quails are taken out of their cages. First the beaks of both quails are shaped with a knife and made very sharp. Then they are released facing each other. A quail's fighting resembles a cock's. It bites with its beak and kicks with its feet. With its beak it wounds the opponent's face and covers it in blood, and with its feet it sometimes tears open even the opponent's crop. The fight lasts fifteen or twenty minutes, or sometimes longer, and at last the vanquished opponent takes to flight; and once he has fled he never again stands his ground in a fight against any quail.

There are three degrees in a quail's advancement, and three periods of its renown are recognised. First is the *naya* (new one), which is caught, tamed for the first time and set to fight. If he wins many fights and does not run, then as soon as the fighting season is over he is put into an ordinary cage. This is the time when he sheds his old feathers and grows new ones. This is called *kariz bithana* (setting to moult). As soon as this time is over, in the second year comes the second degree and period of his advancement, and he is called *naukar*. Then after this, when he has moulted again and is prepared for fighting in the third year, he is called *kariz*, and this is the third period and the highest degree of his advancement.

It is generally accepted that in fighting the *naukar* is stronger than the new one and the *kariz* stronger than the *naukar*. A new quail can hardly fight two pecks against a *kariz*. First-class quail-fighters and noble enthusiasts fight only *kariz*es, and fighting new quails is quite an ordinary game. All kinds of tricks and devices are also used in fighting. Some people put on their quail's face some bitter and poisonous substance or perfume, so that the other quail, after a peck or two, begins to draw back and turn away from the fight, and if he goes on fighting even so, he dies after the fight. Some play the game of *kaif* (intoxication): an hour before the fight they feed their quail something so strongly intoxicating that in the fight he becomes insensible and forgets to run, and fights like a madman until he has driven his opponent from the ring.

In Lucknow the taste for quail-fighting produced such accomplished quail-fighters as cannot be matched anywhere. Some had acquired the skill of taking one look at someone's good, famous quail and making some ordinary quail look exactly like it, and then switching them on some occasion in the course of conversation. Well, that was a shameful theft; but some masters acquired the skill of preparing quails that had run and setting them against fine *kariz*es and winning. Among the masters of the *kaif* game, one gentleman prepared pills of *kaif* of the very highest quality, which sold at ten pills for a hundred rupees, and people took them eagerly.

These men's greatest mastery is seen in the treatment of quails: they cure such sick and broken-down quails, and diagnose their diseases and prescribe suitable ingredients with such skill, that physicians and doctors would be astonished. Great efforts were made to breed quails in captivity and raise chicks from eggs, but there was no success in this.

Quails were also given very grand names, such as Rustam, Sohrab, Shuhra-e-Afaq (World-Famous). In the rings the very largest wagers are laid, and I have myself seen a wager of as much as a thousand rupees. Some kings too had this taste. Nasir-ud-Din Haidar used to enjoy watching quails fight on a table before him.

Among the old quail-fighters the names of Mir Bachchu, Mir Umda, Khwaja Hasan, Mir Fida Ali, Chhanga, Mir Abid and Sayyid Miran will be remembered. Forty years ago at Matiya Burj I found Ghulam Abbas, the steward of Munshi-us-Sultan, Chhote Khan, and Ghulam Muhammad Khan of Khalispur, who were very old men of advanced years, most accomplished in this art. Ghalib Ali Beg, Mirza Asad Ali Beg, Nawab Mirza, Mia Jan, Shaikh Momin Ali and Ghazi-ud-Din Khan also won great renown in the last period.

Hunting quails is also a matter of great interest to the people of Lucknow. Formerly it was purely a hobby, thanks to which many men who had never seen the country outside the city got a breath of the air of fields and forests. But now many people earn their bread by it.

It is said that in the cold season quails come out at night and fly along in the upper air. Hunting enthusiasts prepare loud-calling quails that keep calling all night long. Such quails are called *phandait* (decoys). A net is usually spread around some field of *arhar* (pigeon pea). Hearing the calls of the decoys, the quails begin to come down and drop from above, and in the course of the night many gather. As soon as it is morning they are beaten from every side and driven towards the net, and as soon as they are caught in it they are seized one by one and shut up in bags.

## (3) Partridge-fighting

This too is entertaining. The partridge fights by leaping up more than other birds, but apart from village people and the lower classes, nobles and gentlemen never had a taste for it. Partridges are prepared by tumbling (*lot*) and by making them run. To rouse their ardour and fury they are fed white ants. But this is no great game, nor was it adopted in cultivated society. It was and is, however, common among the lower classes of Lucknow.

## (4) Lava-fighting

The *lava* is a small kind of partridge, smaller even than the quail. Instead of over grain, it fights over the *sada*, that is, the female. When it is to be made to fight, the female's cage is brought and set in front of it. People in the state of Rewa and such places were especially fond of it. In Lucknow too it was liked and taken up to some extent. The truth is that the *lava*'s fighting is more beautiful than the quail's. It opens its shoulders as it fights and grapples, and rises and falls opening out like a flower. Some nobles of Lucknow had taken a liking to it. At Matiya Burj, in the household of the late Wajid Ali Shah, there was a great master of *lava*-fighting who had prepared very fine pairs, and when he brought them before the King and made them fight, it was most enjoyable. *Lava*s too are prepared mostly by tumbling and hunger. *Lava*-fighting was the custom before quail-fighting, but in the end quail-fighting became so popular that the taste for the *lava* faded. It is also hunted in a curious way. Like the quail, it flies along in the upper air. People tie a pot on a thatch, like the one used for decoys. Over its mouth they stretch a membrane, and tying a thread to a little stick, they push the stick through the membrane into the pot and begin to draw the thread through their fingers. An uncouth humming sound begins to come from the membrane, which the *lava*s like so much that they come down out of their flight, and in the morning they are caught in the net just like quails.

## (5) Guldum-fighting

The common people call the *guldum* the *bulbul*, but this is a mistake. The *bulbul* is a songbird of Badakhshan and Persia, while this bird has a red patch (*gul*) under its tail (*dum*), because of which it was named *guldum*. Its fighting too is more common among villagers and bazaar people. Polite society never looked on it with interest, but its fighting is not without charm. They fight over grain, and in the fight both opponents fly up together and fall locked together.

## (6) Lal-fighting

*Lal*s are only for keeping in cages, and not suited to fighting. But self-indulgent man has made even them fight to amuse himself for a while. First, it is hard to tame *lal*s to the point that they can be taken out of the cage and released without flying away. Second, they must also be in such high spirits that they are ready to fight another *lal*. So getting them to fight at all is difficult. But once they do fight, they fight hard, grappling and flying up, and keep on fighting for a long time. The fighting of *lal*s lasts longer than that of other small birds. The taste for *lal*-fighting was very slight among the people of Lucknow. Only one or two masters arose who fought them; otherwise the general inclination was against it, and those who liked it were the common people and the bazaar folk.

## (7) Pigeon-flying

Pigeons are among those tame creatures for which people in every country and every land, from ancient times to the present, have always had a taste to some degree. There are many kinds of pigeon, among which the flying ones are the *girahbaz* (tumblers) and the *gola*s; and of those kept purely for their beauty and fine colouring the best known are the *shirazi*, *guli*, *nasavari*, *gulve*, *laqqa* (fantail), *lotan* (ground tumbler), *chaupa-chandan* and so on. The *yahu* pigeons, because they coo day and night and keep repeating "*Ya Hu*" (O He, that is, God), were particularly liked by the devout, and many *faqir*s and Sufi elders were fond of them.

It is said that the *girahbaz* were first brought from Kabul. At first these were generally the ones flown; the *gola*s came later, their stock coming from Arabia, Persia and Turkestan. The glory of the *girahbaz* is that when they go up in the morning they keep circling for hours in the sky directly above the house, so that if you put a basin full of water in the courtyard they will always be seen in it. Some stay up the whole day and come down in the evening. They know their own house and are devoted lovers of home.

The *girahbaz* is so remarkable that a pigeon of my own once got caught at someone's house, and he clipped its wings. Three years later, when it got the chance and its feathers had grown, it came back, went into its own compartment and began to fight the pigeon that was now living there.

But a flock of *girahbaz* of more than ten or twelve does not fly. When people developed a taste for flying flocks of a hundred or two hundred pigeons, the *gola*s were adopted. The art of pigeon-flying had already advanced so far in Delhi that it is said that when the procession of Bahadur Shah, the last heir of the Mughal empire, went out, a flock of two hundred pigeons flew above in the air alongside it, keeping His Majesty in their shade.

The pigeon has a very great attachment to its home. The feat of carrying the dovecote on a handcart, stopping wherever one was told, flying the pigeons and calling them back to the dovecote had also first been seen in Delhi.

In Lucknow pigeon-flying began in the very first period of this ruling dynasty. So Nawab Shuja-ud-Daula was very fond of pigeons. A man named Sayyid Yar Ali, a native of Bareilly, presented himself at court as a consummate pigeon-flyer, and he was greatly valued. Nawab Asaf-ud-Daula and Nawab Saadat Ali Khan were also fond of it, and in the time of Ghazi-ud-Din Haidar and Nasir-ud-Din Haidar pigeon-flying reached a very high level here. A master pigeon-flyer here named Mir Abbas displayed the feat that whoever wished to invite him, on payment of five rupees, wherever he lived, he would arrive with his dovecote and fly the pigeons from that man's own house, fly them and call them back with a whistle. Not one pigeon would dare to come down anywhere else. The taste had grown so much that at some nobles' houses nine hundred hen pigeons alone flew together, and some nobles flew as many cock pigeons or more.

From Khost (a region on the Afghan frontier) came pigeons of a particular colour called *tapat*, which were very costly. Many nobles spent thousands of rupees to fly them.

An innovating gentleman in Lucknow performed the feat of taking two young pigeons, cutting off the right wing of one and the left wing of the other, and stitching the two together where the wings had been cut to make a double pigeon; and he reared them with such care that they grew up and began to fly. He prepared many such double pigeons. It was a frequent custom that when Nasir-ud-Din Haidar crossed the river from the Chhattar Manzil in his barge and sat in the Dilaram mansion to enjoy the view of the river, this man would release his strange double pigeons from this side, and they would cross over and settle near the King. The King was greatly delighted to see them and gave him rewards.

A gentleman named Miran Ali had acquired the skill of colouring a pigeon to look however he wished. In many places he would pluck out a feather and set a feather of another colour in its socket so firmly that it set like a natural feather, and in many places he used dye. But the colour was so firm and fast that it would not fade in the least. The colour lasted a whole year. But when the feathers fell in the moult, the original colour came out again. Each of these pigeons of his sold for fifteen or twenty rupees, and the nobles took them eagerly. He would also make a *palatniya*, which occurs one in a hundred thousand, and is matchless in the boundaries of its colours and its patches.

A great pigeon-flyer, Nawab Pale, flew *girahbaz* pigeons like *gola*s. His feat was that over whatever place and whatever house he wished, he could make them perform at a signal of his *chhipi* (pigeon-flyer's pole), that is, the pigeons would begin to turn somersaults in the air.

Wajid Ali Shah had collected many new pigeons at Matiya Burj. It is said that he bought a pair of *resham-pare* (silk-winged) pigeons for twenty-five thousand, and bred a strain of green pigeons of a certain kind. When he died there were more than twenty-four thousand pigeons, over which hundreds of pigeon-keepers were employed, and their steward, Ghulam Abbas, had no equal in the art of pigeon-flying.

Enthusiasm and skill had also brought unequalled advances in the coloured pigeons kept for show. This is not merely a rumour: I have seen with my own eyes a *shirazi* that filled the whole of a cage a yard wide, and a *guli* that could pass through the bangle of a twelve-year-old girl.
