The style of men among the Muslims from ancient times had been: hair on the head, clipped moustaches, and the beard round and trimmed. Religious people, scholars and ascetics left the beard entirely alone according to the custom of the Prophet, and in shortening the moustache sometimes went so far as to shave it off. But the style of the nobles and gentry was that limits were set for the beard, below near the throat and above on the cheeks, and the hairs that grew too long were cut, giving the beard roundness and fixing a limit to its length. The first to bid farewell to the beard was the Emperor Akbar, and after him Jahangir too had no beard on his face. This may have influenced the courtiers of Akbar and Jahangir to some extent, but the general style of the Muslim nobles remained what it had been before.

After the court was established in Lucknow, the shortening of beards began, and gradually beards disappeared from most faces. Probably this was the effect of the fact that, owing to their common religion, the court here was coming under the influence of the Iranians, and there, from the time of the Safavid kings, the beard had no longer kept among kings and nobles the importance it had had since the beginning of Islam. Among the Muslims either shaving off someone's beard was a punishment or a means of humiliating and degrading him, or else in Iran not keeping a beard had become part of the dignity of nobility and rule. Nawab Burhan-ul-Mulk, the first founder of the Nishapuri family of Lucknow, had a trimmed beard on his face. Shuja'-ud-Daula shaved his beard, and from then on all the nobles and kings here went on shaving their beards. The inevitable result was that the custom of the beard died out among ordinary Shi'as. Then in later times many Sunnis too had their beards clipped or shaved off. After the fondness for shaving the beard arose, all kinds of styles began to appear. Someone grew small sideburns below the ears, someone had *galmuchchhe* (whiskers joined to the moustache), someone kept large *galmuchchhe*. The townsfolk of the country round Lucknow and some Sunnis of the city also adopted the style of keeping a beard, but, in the taste of the Rajputs and Indian Pathans, parting it in the middle at the chin and drawing the hair on both sides up towards the ears; and to keep the beard in this style they would keep a *dhata* (cloth band) tied for hours. Then along with this upswept beard the moustaches too were combed, tied and drawn upwards.

Thus this very style was regarded here and throughout India as the mark of soldiering and bravery.

As regards the head, in the blessed time of the Lord of the Universe (peace be upon him) the general taste was to have long hair on the head, which was shaved or clipped at the time of the *hajj*. But in Arabia itself, a few days after the rise of Islam, shaving the head became the general custom, and this custom appears in Iran too. And when the Muslims first came to India their style was generally this: shaven heads and turbans on them. Among the Hindus at the time of the coming of the Muslims it was the custom to keep hair on the head. This style pleased the Muslims here. Thus in the last period, except for the scholars and the pious, and the *shaikh*s and Sufis, the general style of high and low in Delhi was to have hair on the head that came down to the ears; except for the *banka*s, who were always bringing out new fashions.

In this style the gentry of Delhi came to Lucknow. On coming here delicacy of temperament increased, the fondness for self-adornment advanced, and combing with great delicacy and neatness, they began to set flat bands of hair on the forehead like women. And such a style arose that adolescent boys acquired a womanlike attractiveness. Then a few days later, when the women, learning from the English, began to brush back their hair leaving the forehead well open, some men also adopted this style.

Now after the Mutiny, when the English style and cut began to be adopted, here too, as with the people of all India, the hair was cut in English fashions, and as many beards as had remained on faces also took their leave.

The style of women's hair in Lucknow was probably the same as in Delhi. But here under the monarchy, in the plaits of brides and of women who adorned themselves, there were *mobaf*s (plait-ribbons) of large coloured *dupatta*s, which, given plenty of twists, were plaited and hung down from the back of the head to the waist. And when more elaboration was wanted, *jaura* (silver ribbon) was wound round them, and it seemed that a great heavy thick plait was silver from top to bottom. On the forehead arched bands of hair were set, and between them, round the *chand-tikli* (forehead pendant), designs were made with gold or silver *afshan* (powdered tinsel) and spangles.

Henna on the hands and feet was obligatory for women. But along with them, men of a romantic temper had also begun to apply henna in large numbers, on seeing which outsiders found effeminacy in the men of Lucknow and gave them names.

(4) The fourth thing in social life is morals and habits. In this matter the people of Lucknow gained particular prominence. This is the thing especially worth noticing in Lucknow, and it is the most important to discuss. In fact in Lucknow Asian culture reached its highest advance, and in no other place do people keep in mind those rules of social life to which the people of Lucknow have become accustomed.

Culture (*tahzib*) is in fact the name for those moral refinements which a people comes to regard as the demands of good breeding. Nowadays we often see people saying that ceremony in social intercourse and the refinements of social life are a kind of useless hypocrisy. But this is their mistake. In that sense dress and the arrangement of one's living are also useless hypocrisy, and all the things involved in leaving the life of a beast and adopting the life of a human being can be called useless hypocrisy. The truth is that those who do not know human culture and have no skill in meeting cultivated people have made this their excuse: that we do not know such show-making ways of townspeople or cultivated people. But if you consider, humanity itself is show. Dressing well, having good household goods, eating well, and keeping cleanliness in mind in every task are all show.

The first principle of moral culture is that in social intercourse the other person should be given precedence over oneself in every matter of pleasure and benefit, and one should keep oneself behind him and on a lower level than him. To rise to one's feet in someone's honour, to vacate the seat of honour for him and seat him there, to sit respectfully kneeling before him, to listen attentively to what he says and answer in a tone of humility: all these are ways of giving the other precedence over oneself. And to the degree that these were customary among the well-bred gentry of Lucknow in the days of Lucknow's youth, they were found nowhere else.

These are things connected with the manner of social intercourse. But when these same things are fully formed in morals and habits, the quality of self-sacrifice (*isar*) arises in a man, and he becomes ready to keep his friends company in every way and to help them in everything. In the royal period this quality had arisen in its full perfection among the people of Lucknow. And it was the result of this that a great many people had arisen here who apparently had no means of livelihood. Their friends supported them by such hidden means that no one could ever even find out. And because their means of livelihood were hidden, they joined the company of the greatest nobles, decently dressed and in noble style, and their eyes were not lowered before anyone. Lucknow was full of such people when the revolution in the kingdom took place, and all of a sudden their means of living vanished.

This splendid self-sacrifice of the nobles had fixed this as the standard of good breeding here: that one should behave towards others with such courtesy and show such generosity in looking after them that there should not be even a trace of laying them under obligation. In all the great cities of the world there are great merchants and wealthy men who give away hundreds of thousands of rupees to the deserving, but it is evident from their manner that they have not spent a single *paisa* without self-interest. In contrast, the friend-cherishing and gentleman-favouring of the people of Lucknow was such that the world could see no difference between the giver and the receiver.

There is no doubt that when after the revolution in the kingdom great nobles became poor and destitute, and the group that had been living on hidden means of livelihood began to starve, the nobles became unable to show the quality of generosity and self-sacrifice. But the outward courtesy that had entered their nature remained as it was. And the result of this was that many people came to such a state that by their words they hold out hope of the highest hospitality, but if you become their guest the contrary appears. Many people have taken this for hypocrisy and empty words. But alas, this is not hypocrisy but generosity of spirit without the means. Do not find fault with such people, but pity their condition.

But it cannot be denied that in the time of wealth, since the greater part of the population of the city was living on the hidden help of nobles, gentry and friends, the quality of labour, hardiness and knowing the value of time generally died out among the people of Lucknow. And the pursuits they took up kept taking them farther day by day from the highway of national progress. Their pursuits were nothing but amusement and play. Freedom from care and relief from worry about a livelihood made them fond of pigeon-flying, quail-fighting, cock-fighting, *chausar* (cross and circle board game), *ganjifa* (round playing cards) and chess, on which they began to spend most of their income. And the whole population was a stranger to the words "thought for tomorrow". There was no noble who was not devoted to one or other of these frivolous pursuits, and whose fondness had not drawn others into it too.

No city in the world is free of debauchery and pleasure-seeking, and God forbid that debauchery of the ill-mannered and tasteless kind found in Europe should arise in our cities. But the foundation laid in Lucknow in the time of Shuja'-ud-Daula of forming connections with courtesans (*randi*) kept advancing day by day. It became part of the style of nobles that, to satisfy their fancy or to display their grandeur, they always kept a connection with some public seller of beauty. A man as able, shrewd, cultivated and polished as Hakim Mahdi, who rose to the rank of Prime Minister, owed the foundation of his advancement to a courtesan named Piyaru, who paid the customary fee out of her own pocket and got him the governorship (*nizamat*) of a province. A small marvel of these excesses was that it was a saying in Lucknow that "until a man has had the company of courtesans he does not become a man". In the end people's moral condition was ruined. And down to our own time there were in Lucknow certain courtesans to whose houses it was not thought improper to go openly and shamelessly and to remain in their company. In any case, this thing spoiled their habits and characters to a great extent, although as a result of it they also acquired skill in deportment.

As for the morals and habits of the women, our general claim in this matter is that among people who have a taste for fornication the women cannot be chaste. Nevertheless it cannot be denied that in Lucknow the morals of the women were not so spoiled as those of the men had been. Sociability and meeting their women acquaintances with courtesy and respect were as much found among the women as among the men. At one time spinning at the wheel was considered a genteel pursuit of gentlewomen. Now, although the spinning mills have made this pursuit entirely useless and fruitless, fashion and wealth had already made the women here give up this pursuit before that. Here in its place the women were more fond of sewing and threading, embroidery, managing the cleanliness of the house, getting work done by *mama*s, slave-girls and personal maids, and dressing and adorning themselves. And the wives got so little leisure from household tasks and the clothes of husband and children that they could not become addicted to the amusements to which the men had become addicted. In truth, in that age the men sat at home and played, and the whole business of the household and the world ran only by the women's efforts.

But in the palaces of the nobles, when all business came into the hands of *mama*s, *Mughlani*s, personal maids and wet-nurses (*anna*), troupes of *domni*s were employed to perform *mujra* before the high-ranking *begam*s. And in palaces where *domni*s were not permanently employed, the ordinary *domni*s of the city came and went frequently, and day after day they would stand at the gateway with *tabla* and *sarangi*. Hence hundreds of their troupes existed in the city. The humour of the *domni*s, so far as I know, is most obscene and vulgar, and their company can have no good effect on women. Thus just as the courtesans were the cause of the men's immorality, the *domni*s became the cause of corrupting the women's morals.

But the families of the gentry were kept safe from the company of the *domni*s, and so their women remained safe from this harm, and they are the finest example of good character and morals. It is the character of the women of Lucknow that they are ready to sacrifice everything of theirs for their husband, and regard their own existence as an appendage to their husband's existence. And unlike the women of some other cities, who are far ahead of the women of Lucknow in skill at housekeeping, it has never occurred to the women here to hide their money from their husbands and save it separately, or to hesitate to spend their wealth even in their husband's illness. The women of Lucknow are not as skilled as the women there, and in household work they are slovenly by comparison, extravagant in the extreme, and fond of tasty food; but in standing by their husbands and sacrificing their lives for them they are the first of all.
