Out of all the refinements described, the dishes generally chosen for feasts and for portions (*hissa*) sent out were together called the *tora*, which necessarily contained the following foods: (1) *pulao*, (2) *muza'far* (saffron rice), (3) *mutanjan* (sweet rice with meat), (4) *shirmal*, (5) *safeda* (sweet rice not coloured with saffron), (6) bowls of *burani* (yoghurt dish), (7) trays of *shir-birinj* (rice pudding), (8) *qorma*, (9) fried *arvi* (colocasia) in meat, (10) *shami kebab*s, (11) preserve, (12) pickle or chutney. In many places some of these things were left out of the *tora* or others added, but in Lucknow these were generally the accepted dishes, and at feasts and in portions there was seldom anything other than these. At feasts these things were laid out on the dining cloth before each person on separate plates, and if they were to be sent somewhere, this same *tora* was placed on wooden trays and sent with due ceremony.

Among the English it is the custom to decorate the table with flowers, bouquets and ornaments of every kind. There was so much of an example of this here too, that in the *tora*s distributed among nobles, Nawabs and princes a bouquet of paper flowers was also placed among the dishes, which the common people and those of middling rank gave up as superfluous.

According to the rank and standing of the honoured establishments and high households to which food was sent, the number of dishes in the *tora* also increased. To the King's palace, for His Majesty in person, went a *tora* of a hundred and one trays, the cost of which was estimated at five hundred rupees. Among the rulers of Awadh, Wajid Ali Shah's father Amjad Ali Shah was a very sober, pious and God-fearing ruler. He avoided royal pomp, observed the law of the *shari'a* fully, and did nothing without the permission of His Reverence the *Qibla-o-Ka'ba*. In the ardour of his piety he considered it unlawful to spend the country's money on himself, and asked all his kinsmen to send him cash instead of food when they invited him. The result was that people would send five hundred rupees, but along with it, to please him, a *tora* was always sent too, for which it was not obligatory to have a hundred and one trays.

The style of the trays in ordinary society was this: wooden trays, over them a dome-shaped cover of coloured slats, and over that a white cloth wrapper tied above the top. And in the royal kitchen and among honoured nobles it was the custom also to put lac on it and seal it, so that no one should have the chance to tamper with it on the way. Then over the wrapper was a very elaborate coloured, often silk, tray-cover (*khwan-posh*). In the great establishments these tray-covers were necessarily of satin and *kamkhwab* or brocade, and sometimes merely edged with gold lace (*lachka*) or worked with embroidery (*karchob*).

It is possible that this practice was current at the Mughal court too and came from there to Lucknow, but we have not seen these refinements in Delhi on the high scale on which we saw them in Lucknow. Here, in the smallest matters of eating and drinking, these refinements have become obligatory and second nature. If even an ordinary person asks merely for water, the servant will bring the glass most elegantly, placed on a salver with a *jhajhra* (net cover) over it, and present it with respect.

This taste, this refinement and these refinements produced in Lucknow, within only a hundred years, such accomplished cooks as were famous and valued in every city and every court of India; and in all the Muslim courts and states of India, wherever I went, I found cooks from Lucknow, who had influence with the particular nobles and rulers and were highly valued. It cannot be denied that there are now great and accomplished cooks in Hyderabad in the Deccan, Bhopal and Rampur. But if you trace their origins, trace their families and consider the history of their advancement, it will be proved that they are either cooks of Lucknow, or of the line of cooks who came from Lucknow, or pupils of some cook of Lucknow.

We have finished with the kitchen, but sweetmeats remain to be mentioned. Making sweetmeats is the work of Hindu *halva'i*s (confectioners), and it is their sweetmeats that the general public has come to know. But in preparing sweetmeats the Muslim *rikabdar*s stand higher. The *rikabdar*s cannot meet the needs of the common people, for that is the province of the Hindu confectioners. The *rikabdar*s prepare sweetmeats for particular nobles and nobles of taste and refinement, which are matchless and very delicious.

There are two kinds of confectioner in Lucknow: Muslim confectioners and Hindu confectioners. The way of the Muslim confectioners is that if ordinary sweetmeats are bought, the goods of their shops are no better than those of the Hindu confectioners' shops; but if you order a special kind of elaborate sweetmeat from them, it is far better than the Hindu confectioners' sweetmeat, and very refined and delicious. But in general in Lucknow *jalebi*s, *imarti*s and *balushahi*s are made very well.

It is hard to distinguish among sweetmeats which are originally the Hindus' and which came to India with the Muslims. But judging by names and taste it appears that *halva* is a purely Arab thing, which came from Arabia by way of Iran to India and brought its name with it. But apparently no general ruling can be made in this; there are distinctions. The moist *halva* generally found at the confectioners' and eaten with *puri*s is a purely Hindu thing, which they also call *mohan-bhog*. But the four kinds of *halva sohan*, *papri*, *jauzi*, *habshi* and *dudhiya*, appear to be purely the Muslims'; just as there is no doubt about the halvas of Arab taste current in southern India, especially in Madras. They are truly pure Arab halvas that came to India directly from Arabia.

But many of the Hindu confectioners' sweetmeats too appear to be inventions of the Muslim period. For example, the name of *barfi* shows that it was invented by Persian taste. *Balushahi*, *khurme*, *nuqti*s, *gulab-jamun*, *dar-bihisht* and others are also inventions of the Islamic period. The *jalebi* is called *zalabiya* in Arabic, and it is plain that the word *jalebi* is a corruption of *zalabiya*, so it too deserves to be included among these Arab and Persian sweetmeats. The *pera* is a purely Indian sweetmeat, and *imarti*s too are Indian, but I have been told that the *imarti* was invented in Lucknow itself. At present Lucknow has no distinction as regards these sweetmeats. It holds the same rank as all the distinguished cities of India; indeed a strange spectacle is seen, that in Lucknow the confectioners of Agra and the Punjab are more famous, while in other cities I found the confectioners of Lucknow and its neighbourhood the more prominent. Really it depends on a shop's doing well, for the better a confectioner's shop does, the more chance he has to advance in sweetmeats.

The real verdict about confectioners is that the Hindu confectioners stand much higher. The true connoisseurs of sweetmeats are the Hindus, not the Muslims. The Muslims, perhaps because they eat meat, generally have a greater taste for savoury foods. The Hindus, by contrast, are more fond of sweetmeats. They fill their stomachs with sweetmeats alone, which is impossible for Muslims. And because of the Hindus' fondness, Mathura, Benares and Ajodhya, which are the religious centres of the Hindus, excel other cities in the variety and taste of their sweetmeats.

But in making *halva sohan* many other people besides the Muslim *rikabdar*s won fame. In the last period the famous calligrapher of this place, Munshi Hadi Ali Sahib, won particular renown for *papri halva sohan*. He would work twenty-five or thirty seers of *ghee* into a seer of *samnak* (sprouted-wheat flour), and on its cakes he made wonderful beautiful *tughra*s of every kind, which displayed his feats of calligraphy and ornamentation along with the making of the *halva sohan*. After this, at Matiya Burj (Calcutta), I saw many times with my own eyes Munshi-us-Sultan Bahadur, who was the son of a noble of Lucknow, work two or two and a half seers of *ghee* into a *chhatank* of *samnak*, which came to about forty seers per seer. His *papri halva sohan*, instead of being yellow, was bright and white like washed cloth.
