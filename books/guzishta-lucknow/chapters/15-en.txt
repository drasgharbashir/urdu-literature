We have shown how, through the blessing of the Shia family of *mujtahid*s and the Sunni scholars of Farangi Mahal, learning and scholarship rose to such heights in Lucknow in the time of this last court, and what intellectual attraction and authority they gave to its surroundings. But we have still to discuss Greek medicine (*tibb-e-Yunani*).

This most noble art, which is concerned with preserving the human world and advancing the human race, arose in every ancient country in a spontaneous way and from piecemeal experience; but in ancient times, towards the West, the people of Greece made very marked progress in it, and in the East the famous ancients of the Hindus brought it to the highest degree of perfection. When the Caliphate was established among the Muslims, this art came from both places, and skilled physicians of both lands were physicians at the court of Baghdad. At first, for a century or two, all the recognised physicians of the Abbasid court were Hindus, Christians or Jews, but not Muslims. Yet the accomplished physicians of that age, whatever religion they followed, were nurtured in the lap of Islam and were eminent figures of the Islamic world, and it was at their hands that the art of medicine began to be compiled and systematised with a new glory and in a new form, in which, after some correction and alteration, the theoretical arrangement of the Greeks was kept, but the experience of every country and every nation was taken alike.

Some time after this, famous Muslim physicians began to arise, and they began to make the art of medicine their own through their independent judgement and their own experiments, until Ibn Sina wrote and placed in the hands of the world so matchless and unanswerable a book as the *Qanun* (Canon), before which all the nations of East and West bowed their heads. Meanwhile the court of Andalusia advanced even further than the East in general principles and in experiment, and medicine became the special art of the Muslims, of which they were everywhere the resort and refuge. Every nation was acquiring it from them, and on it was built the structure of Europe's present-day doctoring, which was connected above all with the Islamic medical school of Andalusia.

But in these last centuries, when the decline of the Muslims began, its effect showed first in their sciences and arts, and most of all in medicine, and in most countries it fell into the same state as at the beginning of the rise of Greece: people of ordinary ability, without ever having looked at the highest books of the art, treated people from their own piecemeal experience. The result was that within a short time Egypt and Arabia, Syria and Iraq were left without physicians who knew the art. The art survived only in Iran and India. But in the last century Iran too was left without physicians. And in all the lands of Islam there was such ignorance about this ancient art of theirs that when the French and English doctors of Europe appeared, high and low alike saw them as a gift of God, and no one had any sense that this very art had been ours, and that we too once had physicians.

If anything saved the medicine of the Muslims from extinction, it was India alone, where to this day the Greek-system physicians (*hakim*s) are competing successfully with the new branches of European medicine; and although there are charitable hospitals in village after village, people still have a confidence in the treatment of the Greek-system physicians that they do not have in the doctors.

In former days many masters of this art lived in Delhi, among them Hakim Arzani, Hakim Shifai Khan, Hakim Alavi Khan and Hakim Muhammad Sharif Khan, physicians of the highest fame and excellence. From the time of Burhan-ul-Mulk the skilled physicians of Delhi began to come to the land of Awadh. In Shuja-ud-Daula's time especially, apart from one or two physicians of Delhi, all the rest came here. The history of Faizabad shows that to every one of the great households there some Greek-system physician was attached, who was treated with much respect and consideration and, besides a monthly salary, was honoured daily with gifts and favours.

From the time of Asaf-ud-Daula, when Lucknow became the centre of patronage for every accomplishment, many physicians of old family from Delhi settled here, and after a while medicine, like language and poetry, became an art peculiarly of this place. So Lucknow produced physicians of such high rank and great worth as Hakim Masih-ud-Daula, Hakim Shifa-ud-Daula, Hakim Mirza Muhammad Ali, Hakim Sayyid Muhammad Murtaish, Hakim Mirza Kuchak, Hakim Baba and Hakim Mirza Muhammad Jafar, who were truly *mujtahid*s of their art and had the whole learned capital of the ancients before their eyes. Little by little medicine advanced so far here that there can hardly be a quarter of Lucknow without some famous family of physicians. Besides the hundreds of quarters of the city itself, thousands of surgeries were open in the villages and small towns round about, and the famous and renowned physicians at the courts and in the cities of India were all from Lucknow and its neighbourhood. So a physician from the small town of Mohan won at the Gaekwad's court at Baroda an honour such as very few physicians can have won. In short, the soil of Lucknow produced such famous physicians that the stories of their Messiah-like healing are on every child's lips to this day.

In the last period of the court of Awadh, a distinguished pupil of Sayyid Muhammad Murtaish, Hakim Muhammad Yaqub, opened his surgery and won such general authority that from him was founded a very great and famous medical family, which today, without exaggeration, has no equal in the whole world. Among the late notables of this family were Hakim Muhammad Ibrahim, Hakim Hafiz Muhammad Abdul-Ali, Hakim Muhammad Ismail, Hakim Muhammad Masih, Hakim Muhammad Abdul-Aziz and Hakim Hafiz Muhammad Abdul-Wali; and Hakim Abdul-Hafiz Sahib, Hakim Abdur-Rashid Sahib and Hakim Abdul-Mu'id Sahib are now displaying the wonders of their healing. Would that they had not left their family art and fallen into other ambitions!

In Delhi the family of Hakim Muhammad Sharif Khan still exists, in which such masters as Hakim Mahmud Khan and Hakim Abdul-Majid Khan have lived, and Haziq-ul-Mulk Hakim Muhammad Ajmal Khan Sahib keeps his elders' name fresh to this day with his own excellences. In Delhi Hakim Muhammad Ajmal Khan Sahib has also opened a school of medicine, and by founding the Tibbi and Vedic Conference he is raising his art greatly. Opposite him, in Lucknow, Hakim Abdul-Aziz Sahib founded the Madrasa Takmil-ut-Tibb, from which every year scores of physicians are trained and spread to every corner of the land, bearing witness to Lucknow's authority in medicine.

In any case, if the Muslims' nominally Greek art of medicine is alive anywhere in the world today, it is in India, and in India it has only two centres: Delhi and Lucknow. But in Delhi there is only the one family of Hakim Mahmud Khan, while in Lucknow there are scores of such families. Some other physicians are also to be seen practising in Delhi, but they are new physicians of the present day who have established their surgeries. In Lucknow, although there are many new physicians, there are also many families in which medicine has advanced for centuries.

There is one more difference between the physicians of Lucknow and those of Delhi. We do not know whether the present medical curriculum was drawn up by the physicians of Delhi or of Lucknow, but the physicians of Delhi do not follow it fully as the physicians of Lucknow have done. The same books are taught there too, but in Delhi the physicians' practice departs to a large extent from their codified medicine. The reason is that in adopting the drugs of the Vedic (Ayurvedic) system they did not merely bring these new ingredients into their practice, but were also careless enough, in bringing them in, to overlook their own ancient codified and accepted principles, especially the discussions of temperament (*mizaj*), and to prescribe ingredients whose temperament, actions and properties they do not fully know. The greatest complaint heard there at present is that in the curriculum of the Delhi medical school, besides anatomy, other principles of doctoring have been adopted in such quantity and with such carelessness that the real art of medicine, instead of advancing, is being wiped out entirely. The same carelessness they showed earlier in adopting Vedic principles is now being shown in taking up the principles of doctoring. In this state of things the end that seems to await our ancient art of medicine in Delhi is most dangerous.

By contrast, in all the medical families of Lucknow, especially the family of the late Hakim Yaqub and the Takmil-ut-Tibb, efforts are made to preserve the true principles of medicine and to advance them while keeping them within their own sphere. Their practice is still not in the least separated from their art and their books, and they are going along such a safe path that there is hope that Islamic medicine may perhaps escape the ravages of time. Yet even these people are still very far from rendering true service to the art. The soul of medicine is pharmacy, which is a branch of our ancient science of *kimiya* (chemistry). On this art was founded Europe's present miraculous science of chemistry. In this art the books of the early Muslim authors have not yet entirely perished; many remain. It is the work of the teachers of medicine to study them again and again and understand them, to work them out with thought and reflection, and to bring them into the curriculum; then, benefiting from new experiments, to exercise independent judgement on their principles and rules, and to put their own art of pharmacy on a regular footing, without which all the excellences of medicine often prove fruitless and of no use.

But even with this shortcoming, the advancement and strength Lucknow gave to medicine is far greater than Delhi's, and is found in no other part of the world.
