We have said a great deal about the entertainers, the art of music and the arts derived from it. But in the same connection it remains to describe the bazaar bands. So today we shall show what influence Lucknow had on these bands, and with this we shall close the discussion of music. The ensembles of instruments that go with wedding and other processions are of six kinds: (1) *dhol-tasha* (drums), (2) *raushan-chauki*, (3) *naubat*, (4) *turhi* and *qarna* (horns), (5) *danka* and bugle, (6) the English band, which is called the "organ band" and is gaining more currency day by day.

The first, the *dhol-tasha*, is the ancient national instrument of India, which the English, calling it "tom-tom", often mock out of their unfamiliarity and ignorance. In 1886, when hundreds of specimens of Indian social life and of the arts and pastimes of this country were shown at the English exhibition ground of "Earl's Court", I saw with my own eyes the specimen of this instrument shown there: an extremely dark-skinned man, wearing nothing on his body but a dirty loincloth, would come and stand naked before the general crowd. A drum hung from his neck, and in the most savage manner, without any measure or order, shaking his head like a madman, he would begin to beat the drum hard with a stick, and it was said that this was the Indian instrument, the "tom-tom". But this is the ignorance and folly of those people. It is a very complete instrument, and playing it is a regular art in which a measure of the highest order is kept.

In Lucknow it generally has two, and sometimes three or four, large *dhol*s, at least one and often two or three *tasha* players, and at least one cymbal player. The cymbal is also found in Iran and elsewhere, and *tasha*s are current in Egypt and elsewhere too, but the *dhol* is a purely Indian thing. This band came to Lucknow from Delhi with the armies and the pleasure-seekers. But in Delhi its ensemble had only *dhol*s and cymbals. The *tasha*s were added in Lucknow, and as soon as they came into use they seemed so necessary and important that it was as if they had put life into this band. Although in most cities there are only *dhol*s and cymbals, in Lucknow the *tasha*s have become an essential part, and *dhol*s are never played anywhere without them. But it is plain that the one who displays the most skill in this band is the man who plays the *tasha*. It is he who sets the measure, and the *dhol* players follow him in it. The quality of *tasha* playing is that the strokes should fall so fast that one roll cannot be distinguished from another, and that from these continuous and unbroken rolls, by rise and fall, measure and *gat* should be produced. In Lucknow there were such masters at playing this band that they made this ordinary instrument, which everywhere else was unprincipled, very regular, and even now there are such deft players here that the drummers of no other city can play before them.

In Lucknow after the *Chehlum* a *ta'ziya* goes out that is called Bakhshu's *ta'ziya*. Its procession has now taken another form because of the quarrel between Shias and Sunnis, but ten or twelve years ago its character was this: since it was a memorial of a great lover of the Prophet's family from the royal period, and those who now carried it were poor and resourceless people, unequalled and unrivalled masters of every kind of band took part in it as a meritorious act, and on the pretext of earning merit displayed the excellence of their arts to the people of the city. And so it was their custom that wherever they halted, admirers surrounded them, and they would stand for hours in the same place as if challenging, "Is there anyone who will come and play before us?" Great master singers would applaud them, and they, roused, would play still better. Above all, the *tasha* players among them were great master *dhari*s, who had mastery in music and created novelties in the *gat*s.

What greater proof could there be of the importance and principled nature of the art of playing *dhol* and *tasha* than this: I saw with my own eyes in Calcutta that Wajid Ali Shah, the last deposed crowned head of Awadh, who was an unrivalled master of music, when the *mehndi* procession set out from his Asmani Kothi on the seventh of Muharram, would himself hang a *tasha* round his neck and play it. Great singers had great *dhol*s round their necks. The nobles of the court stood round in a circle, and he played the *tasha* with such delicacy and skill that even listeners who knew nothing of it were enraptured, and the singers' cries of "Bravo!" outdid even the uproar of our *mushaira*s. In the same way I several times saw him play the *dhol* too.

In any case, in this most ancient instrument of India too, Lucknow society made its alteration, and an alteration that is most welcome and necessary. If anyone comes and sees the skill of the *tasha* players here, he will realise how fitting an alteration it is and how important it has made the *dhol* and the cymbal.

The second ensemble is the *raushan-chauki*. The *raushan-chauki* is a very old band, and if not all of it, then its most important parts were brought by the Muslims, for the *shahnai* is its most important part, and it is said to be the invention of the Shaikh-ur-Rais Ibn Sina. Just like the human voice, the *shahnai* renders true notes with the highest skill of vocal art as no other instrument can. In the *raushan-chauki* there are at least two *shahnai* players and one drummer, at whose waist two small drums are tied. The drums keep the measure. One *shahnai* player keeps giving the drone to hold the true pitch, and the other displays the movement of the voice and practised vocal art, and he is the real performer, who renders *ghazal*s, *thumri*s and so on in wonderfully charming notes.

The *raushan-chauki* was the special court band of India, which played at the meal-times of kings and the highest nobles. At night, at the hour of rest, the *raushan-chauki* made its round about the royal palace, and its melody gave great delight from afar. Under the Mughal empire it was thought a very important and refined band, and in Delhi it had been current since God knows when. No doubt the players of the *raushan-chauki* came to Lucknow from Delhi, but masters of it had also long been present in these parts. In most of the temples of Benares the *raushan-chauki* is played in the morning to this day, and if you go and listen from somewhere close by at first light, it is a great delight.

In Lucknow, in wedding processions generally, the *raushan-chauki* players stay near the bridegroom. Especially in Hindu wedding processions they are given rewards at every step along the way. In my view and experience no better *raushan-chauki* players than those of Lucknow are to be found anywhere today. The sense of measure with which the people of Lucknow render everything in charming tunes with true notes, the *raushan-chauki* players of no other place can achieve. Their skill and knowledge of their art can be judged when someone listens with interest and keeps applauding. In this same Bakhshu's *ta'ziya* already mentioned, the *raushan-chauki* players too displayed their skill, and strove so hard that after them no one else's *shahnai* gave any pleasure.

The third ensemble is the *naubat*. Of our old melodies of pleasure this is the grandest band. In it there are two or three *shahnai* players and one kettledrummer, who sets two very large and magnificent kettledrums (*naqqara*) slanting before him and beats both together with sticks. The sound of these kettledrums is very great, and echoes very far through the surrounding air. And with them there is also a cymbal player.

The *naubat* too is a historic band, and was long used for the display of majesty. In Islamic history we find it at the courts of Damascus, Baghdad and Egypt as well. In Baghdad, in the middle Abbasid period, the *naubat* played at the gate of every noble and was considered a mark of respect and greatness. And it appears that it came to India with the Muslims. It may have existed in India before, and though there was no *shahnai*, the kettledrum and cymbal alone may have been played; but its real present form is the one that came here after being arranged in Iran and Iraq.

With the processions and armies of kings and nobles of high rank the *naubat* was a most essential thing. Before the processions of resolute crowned heads the *naubat* went playing on elephants. In battles the victorious party had the *naubat* played loudly to proclaim its victory and supremacy. The Emperor Aurangzeb Alamgir, after conquering Hyderabad, had the *naubat* played on a hill near it, which is called Naubat Pahar to this day. Under the Mughal empire the King granted the right of the *naubat* to the nobles and officers of the highest rank at court, who had the *naubat* played at their gates and in their processions. Some high tower was chosen for the *naubat* players, so that over the gates of most royal palaces a *naubat-khana* (drum house) was built, examples of which are seen in every great city where some great court has been.

Following this ancient custom, it is still the practice in Lucknow that when there is a wedding or some joyful occasion at a wealthy man's house, long poles are set up at his door and covered with red cloth, tinsel and so on, and a lofty *naubat-khana* is built for the time being, where they stay all day and play many times at different hours. In the same way, when wedding processions or *ta'ziya* processions go out, artificial *naubat-khana*s of the same kind, made on platforms, go at the head of all on the shoulders of bearers, and the *naubat* is played on them all along the way.

In former days, especially at the court of Lucknow, this same *naubat* had become the means of telling the time. In those days the division of time was not into these twenty-four hours that have now become current among us through the use of English clocks. In those days the reckoning of time was this: the day and night have eight *pahar*s (watches), four *pahar*s of the day and four of the night, and each *pahar* had eight *ghari*s. In every *naubat-khana* a vessel or trough was kept full of water. In it a bowl with a tiny hole in its bottom was placed empty. It floated on the water. Water kept coming slowly into it through the hole, and the hole was made to such a measure that in one *ghari* it filled with water and sank. Then, after starting, when the bowl sank for the first time one *ghari* was struck. When it sank a second time two *ghari*s were struck. In this way eight *ghari*s were struck in succession, and with the eighth *ghari* the *gajar* was sounded; that is, first eight distinct strokes were struck, and then countless strokes were struck in rapid succession on the gong (*ghariyal*), signifying that the *pahar* was complete; and the series of *ghari*s began again from one.

At the gates where there was a *naubat*, it played for about one *ghari* at the end of every *pahar*. In this order there should be eight *naubat*s for the eight *pahar*s of night and day, but the custom was that only seven *naubat*s were played. The first *naubat* was played at dawn at the time of prayer, that is, at the beginning of the last watch, and was called the morning *naubat*. The second, when one *pahar* of the day had come; this was called the *naubat* of "a *pahar* of the day risen". The third, when the sun was at the meridian, that is, at exactly twelve o'clock; this was called the noon *naubat*. After this, when eight *ghari*s were complete, the third *naubat* was played, and this was called the *naubat* of the third *pahar*. After this, at the end of the fourth *pahar*, at the time of the sunset prayer, the *naubat* was played, and this was called the evening *naubat*. After this, when the fifth *pahar* was complete, the fifth *naubat* was played, which was called the *naubat* of "a *pahar* of the night gone". Then, when the sixth *pahar* had passed, the sixth *naubat* was played, which was called the midnight *naubat*. After this, when the seventh *pahar* was complete and three *pahar*s of the night had passed, the *naubat* was not played, so as not to disturb people's rest; only the *gajar* was sounded. Then after this, at the end of the eighth *pahar*, the morning *naubat* was played. [TN: Sharar's numbering of the daytime *naubat*s is inconsistent ("third" occurs twice); it is translated as printed.]

This was the reckoning of hours that was current at the Mughal court, and in Lucknow until the annexation of the kingdom; and in Calcutta, as long as Wajid Ali Shah lived, the *pahar*s and *ghari*s were struck by this reckoning. But in so short a time this reckoning has so far disappeared that there can hardly be anyone now who knows the reckoning of *pahar*s and *ghari*s. But the trouble is that, although the division of day and night has changed, the old reckoning has entered the very veins of our language. We say: "I'll come in a *ghari*." "I'll sleep at *dopahar* (noon)." "I'll eat when a *pahar* of the day has risen." But we do not know how long a *pahar* is or what is called a *ghari*. We often hear "The *pahra* has been set" and "the soldiers of the *pahra* (watch)", but we do not know that the word *pahra* comes from this *pahar*, because in those days everyone had to serve his duty for a *pahar* at a time.

This old reckoning of the division of time is the Hindus', but in former days the same reckoning was current in Iran too, and the *naubat* was played by it. By our present reckoning a *pahar* was three hours.

The *naubat* players in Lucknow too were of so high an order that they went from here to every place and every city, or were pupils of the masters here. But no advance or addition was made in the *naubat*. The number of players stayed the same, the instruments stayed the same, and the manner of playing stayed the same. Yet this much did happen: the pieces and tunes that the Lucknow school of music had selected and made popular in society at large began to be heard in the drum houses too. But even so, the ancient manner of playing the *naubat* remained within its bounds. The picture Amir Khusrau gave in his verse of the *naubat* playing of his time gives a good idea of the manner in which the *naubat* was played then. The same manner is current to this day, and very little difference has come into it. But even so, the influence of the music of Lucknow on the tunes and songs they play on the *shahnai* is apparent as soon as one hears them.

The *turhi* and the *qarna* are very old national instruments of India, specially associated with armies. The form of the *turhi* suggests that it came to India with the English and became current in the early period of their arrival; but the *qarna* is a purely Iranian instrument, and its sound has such awe and majesty that it is the better suited to strike awe on the field of battle. These two instruments too are used in the processions of Lucknow, but not as independent bands: with military detachments or regiments there is one *turhi* player or *qarna* player, who sounds his instrument at short intervals to announce the presence of his party. The counterpart of these two instruments is the ancient Hindu instrument the *narsingha*, which is often played with Hindu religious processions. These instruments came from Delhi and stayed as they were, and perhaps there is no room for improvement in them.

The bugle and *danka* that are now seen in the wedding processions of Lucknow are really a vulgar combination of earlier and later instruments. By *danka* is meant the kettledrum that in former days was carried on horseback with armies and mighty conquerors; as soon as the stick fell on it, such awe came over people that even the great trembled at heart. The bugle, or "beugle", is the instrument of the English army by which the troops were ordered to move and to do other things as required. So now, by joining the bugle to the *danka*, a new ensemble has been made that is seen with wedding processions. But since these are hired men in a very degraded condition, their dress, their horses and their very faces are so mean and wretched that instead of adding splendour they make for vulgarity and a shameful spectacle.

Now, last of all, and the band making the most progress, is the English band. It was brought purely by the English and did not exist at all before them. In Lucknow, God knows why, its players are only the *mehtar*s (sweepers), who do this work besides cleaning latrines. Probably the reason is that at first both Hindus and Muslims had such social aversion to Christians that if they touched any vessel it was polluted for ever. And this instrument had to be learned from the English and put to the mouth, so no one but the sweepers dared take it up. In any case, it has now become almost the necessary profession of the sweepers.

Since this work was taken up here by a group that is the most despised and degraded and has no contact at all with music, there was no hope that this art could make the least progress here. But it was not so. A desire for advancement arose among the sweepers themselves, and since the tunes of Indian music had spread through the society of the city and entered its taste, the sweepers were compelled to render their tunes on this Western organ. From the English, or from the soldiers who played the English instruments, they had acquired only the ability to play English instruments, or perhaps they had learned a few tunes of Western music. But now that they began to play the current pieces in Indian tunes, they went on advancing day by day.

I have heard English bands everywhere, and everywhere the same English pieces are played that they learned from their English bandmasters. Nowhere did I see that the players of this instrument had cast it in the mould of Indian music. If you look closely, it is only in Lucknow that you will find the very *ghazal*s or *thumri*s that the *raushan-chauki* men are rendering on the *shahnai* being rendered by the English bandsmen on their instruments, and with such skill that one cannot help wanting to listen.

Through the zeal of the sweepers hundreds of English bands have been formed in Lucknow, some of which have twenty-five or thirty players and some six or seven or only four or five. Altering the uniforms of the British soldiers to suit Indian taste, they have also made themselves uniforms of every colour. And if those uniforms are clean and new, when they wear them and march with a wedding procession playing the organ band, they look very fine and very grand.

The distinction of the uniform belongs to these people alone. Players of the other kinds of band never thought of devising any uniform for themselves. They wear very mean and dirty clothes. But the sweepers of the English bands have raised their standing by devising uniforms of every kind for themselves, and by bringing Indian music into the English organ they have also increased their esteem among the people.
