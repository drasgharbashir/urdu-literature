In connection with deportment we should also point out that, unlike Europe or Arabia and Persia, in India there was no custom of clubs and societies for meeting one another and enjoying company. In Europe everywhere such clubs or societies are established, to which people go to meet friends and people of like taste and enjoy their company. Among the Arabs, Iranians and Turks, tea-houses or coffee-houses have become the means of social intercourse and the exchange of ideas. Just as you see that wherever there are two or four Englishmen they set up a club of their own, and in leisure hours go there to read the newspapers and meet friends, so in any city where there is a sufficient number of Iranians and Arabs, some tea-house or coffee-house of theirs opens, and in it, whenever you look, some group of theirs is always present, drinking tea and smoking the *huqqa* there, eating, and sitting together chatting.

In contrast, in India there was never a custom of clubs or tea-houses of this kind, nor is there to this day. The English Government tried in various cities to produce this taste, and bearing great expense had tea-houses opened, but there was no success. Thirty or thirty-five years ago, in the Chowk of Lucknow itself, the late Mir Muhammad Husain Sahib, Director of Agriculture and Commerce of the Nizam's State, before going to Hyderabad, had a tea-house opened with the help of the Government, in which the furniture too was good and drinks of every kind except unlawful ones were kept ready. But no one paid attention, and in the end Mir Sahib had to suffer a loss and close it.

The old taste in company here is that in every quarter (*mohalla*) or every circle of the population some well-to-do or wealthy man makes arrangements in his own house for people to come and sit. For the entertainment and hospitality of friends he provides the necessary things, *huqqa*, *pan* and the like, at his own private expense, and those of like taste come regularly and without fail. The company lasts long, there are witticisms and jests, and so long as the company lasts there is hospitality with *huqqa* and *pan*. And then the colour of their gatherings changes according to the taste of the companions. If the members of the gathering have a taste for letters and poetry, there is talk of poetry, prose, and the appreciation and weighing of verse. If they are scholars and learned men, learned discussions are started with a scholarly taste. If it is the company of cultivated nobles, good manners are displayed with the utmost refinement, polish and decorum in style and dress, the equipment of pleasure, eating and drinking, and in the use of everything and the adoption of every taste. If it is the company of romantic pleasure-seekers, moon-faced women of the bazaar also join it, and the airs of coquetry and of indulging coquetry are seen. It should be kept in mind that, unlike Europe, respectable and chaste women cannot join any company of men here, and when a woman is seen in a gathering of friends, be certain that she is a seller of her chastity, a courtesan of the bazaar. The result is that in Europe, because respectable and polished women join in company, the rank and standing of women of the bazaar in society have fallen so low that the door of no respectable family can open to them, nor can they set foot in the clubs and societies of the gentry. In contrast, to some extent throughout India, and likewise in Lucknow, women of the bazaar have gained the rank of sitting side by side with cultivated and polished nobles in their gatherings. And here this taste advanced so far that some honoured courtesans also established in their own houses such gatherings for sitting together, to which many cultivated people too are not ashamed to go. In Lucknow the houses of Chaudhrain Bi Haidar Jan and some other courtesans of the same standing were regular clubs of the gentry, in which, on the part of the hostess, that is, the said Bi Sahiba, ample hospitality with *huqqa* and *pan* was offered. English taste has now at least brought this much reform that, although all kinds of new immoralities have arisen, sitting openly in courtesans' houses and enjoying their company has begun to be considered somewhat improper.

In any case, the clubs of Lucknow were the houses of well-to-do people and nobles. Here it was, and still is, considered most improper to cook a *sajhe ki handi* (shared pot), or for those present at a gathering to contribute shares and eat and drink *huqqa*, *pan* or food together, each at his own expense. Here mention of subscriptions was a cause of shame to the community and against good breeding. And all entertainments here, whether on an occasion of rejoicing or merely for friendly company, were given by one person alone. Another, if he has the means, can give a whole entertainment of his own, but cannot say: "Take five rupees from me for the food at your entertainment and include me too."

Among the merchants of Delhi there is the custom of *patti parna*, that is, many merchants together collect a subscription, and with this sum an entertainment or a gathering of dance and song is held in some house or in pleasure grounds outside. But we are certain that this practice was devised by the trade of that place after the fall of the empire. This was never the taste of the gentry of Delhi, for if it had been among the gentry there, it would have been in Lucknow too, which in social life is the pupil of Delhi and the bearer of its former genuine taste.

(6) Greetings and enquiries after health. The sixth thing in the manners of social life, which is more important and necessary than everything else, is greeting and asking after the health of whomever one meets. The ancient, religious and simple greeting of Islam is "*As-salamu 'alaika*" (Peace be upon you), and if there are many people, "*As-salamu 'alaikum*". Along with this, after this greeting, those people, if they met anyone in the morning, used to say "*Sabbahakumu'llahu bi'l-khair*", that is, "May God make your morning pass in well-being", and if they met in the evening, "*Massakumu'llahu bi'l-khair*" (May God make your evening good). This was the greeting and enquiry of the Arabs, which they taught as they went westward as far as Andalusia and came eastward as far as India. In Europe the Franks learned this same manner of greeting from them, and in the East the Iranians, Turanians and Indians learned it. Thus in Europe the original greeting, which was the special mark of Islam, disappeared, and only the prayers after the greeting, "*Sabbahakumu'llahu bi'l-khair*" and "*Massakumu'llahu bi'l-khair*", remained. Their translation, "Good morning" and "Good evening", we hear to this day from the lips of the English in greeting. In French "*bon matin*", "*bonjour*" and "*bonsoir*", that is, may your morning, day and evening be good, are said. There is no doubt that these Western peoples received this lesson in greeting from the Arab conquerors of Andalusia. [TN: this derivation is the author's own conjecture.]

In India and Iran, since the worship of elders was customary and this thing had entered the very veins and fibres of these Eastern peoples, the bare words "*As-salamu 'alaikum*", which established the equality of the members of the community, seemed to the wealthy, in the ardour of their pride and arrogance, very insipid and beneath their dignity. Especially when the imperial court was established here and the crowned heads wiped out all the Islamic manners in having themselves honoured and revered, and ordered the courtiers to stand before them with folded hands and to bow in their honour, just as servants stand before God with folded hands and perform *ruku'* (bowing) and *sujud* (prostration), then, following the royal courts, ordinary nobles and wealthy men too fixed other honorific words for greeting instead of "*As-salamu 'alaikum*", for example "*taslim*", "*kornish*", "*adab*", "*bandagi*". And by the grace of the self-worshipping, God-forgetting Muslim nobles, all these words are at present our greetings. In Arabia, with saying "*As-salamu 'alaikum*", no movement was made except a smiling face. Only after the greeting was the hand shaken with one hand; in the handshake the hand was moved, and with it "*Sabbahakumu'llahu bi'l-khair*" or "*Massakumu'llahu bi'l-khair*" was said. In memory of this Arab manner of greeting, in Europe now they say "Good morning" and so on with a slight movement of the head, and joining hands give a shake. In contrast, in India the full greeting now is that, along with saying the words mentioned above, they put the hand on the head or forehead and bow to the degree of *ruku'* or somewhat less. This bowing and putting the hand on the forehead is purely a Hindu influence and a relic of polytheistic feelings. In both these things there is the suggestion that we bow our heads at your feet and kiss the ground before you.

Not only this; at the courts of kings and nobles the number of salutations was fixed. In some places seven salutations were made and in some three. In meeting ordinary elders and friends one salutation was enough. In Lucknow, since there was greater regard for manners and the observance of rank, the greeting of juniors to elders, and also of those of middling rank to honoured people, is this: along with uttering one of the said words, the right hand is raised to in front of the chest or face and moved several times. For juniors this is to this day a most approved and dutiful greeting. This moving of the hand several times signifies several salutations. Similarly many people use *taslim* and *kornish* in the plural forms, saying "*taslimat*" and "*kornishat*". This too is a relic of the multiplicity of salutations.

Now we describe separately the meaning, dignity and origin of the current words of greeting mentioned above. *Taslim* in Arabic means "to greet". Apparently, giving up "*As-salamu 'alaikum*" and using a verbal noun seems rather absurd, but in the society here it was thought that saying "I am greeting" rather than greeting expresses more respect. *Kornish* is a word of the Turkish language, which came here with the Turkish conquerors of India. It means bowing in salutation, so in it too there is the idea of bowing down to kiss the ground and the feet. *Adab* is simply the plural of *adab* (respect); the intention of uttering it in place of a greeting is "I perform all the ways of respect and reverence that there are." *Bandagi*: this is a word more abject and polytheistic than all the words of greeting. *Bandagi* means to worship and to perform devotion. In greeting its intention can be nothing other than "I perform the duty of servitude (*'ubudiyat*)", which according to the beliefs of Muslims can be said towards no one but God.

In contrast to these Indian greetings, the literal translation of the words "*As-salamu 'alaikum*" that are said in Arabia is "Peace upon you", or in plain Urdu, "May you remain safe"; that is, greeting is in fact a prayer for the safety of every hearer. Islam advanced on this in that the "*salam*" is God's message which the Prophet of God (peace be upon him) conveyed to the Muslims, and until the Day of Judgement every Muslim will go on conveying this message of his to another Muslim. In the definite article (*al*) on *salam* in *as-salamu 'alaikum* there is a clear allusion to this, that the very peace which is the message of the Prophet should reach you.

Understanding these meanings and the purpose of the original greeting of Islam, anyone can judge that this greeting is a means of bringing to mind the equality of the community and of creating nationhood and brotherhood among all the followers of the Prophethood of Muhammad. But alas, the Muslims gave it up, and our useless pride now makes us think that for some ordinary Muslim to say *as-salamu 'alaikum* to us on meeting is to insult us. On top of this, since the difference between Shi'a and Sunni has produced this state of things, that both, instead of becoming one community and proving one body, wish to remain separate and distinct from each other, not only the common people but even the later scholars and authors of both parties have taught their own party to hate the habits and ways of the other. The result of this tendency was that while even in Arabia and Persia the greeting of both Shi'a and Sunni was "*As-salamu 'alaikum*", the Shi'as of India, and especially of Lucknow, flinging "*As-salamu 'alaikum*" at the Sunnis' heads, have reserved for themselves the words "*Salamun 'alaikum*". In reciting the *ziyarat* (salutations) of the Imams they say the same old words, for example "*As-salamu 'alaika ya Aba 'Abdi'llah 'alaihi's-salam*" (Peace be upon you, O Abu Abdullah, upon him be peace), but when they greet acquaintances they say "*Salamun 'alaikum*", because "*As-salamu 'alaikum*" is the greeting of the Sunnis.

Nevertheless this former Arab greeting has mostly become reserved, among both Sunni and Shi'a, for religious people, or forms part of a religious dignity and style. Otherwise in the society of the nobles "*adab*" and "*taslim*" are in general use. Many people also say *bandagi*, but this word is more current among women.

In Lucknow the old greeting of cultivated and polished people was that the younger to the elder, the poor to the rich, should say *taslim* or *adab* bowing low. In reply elders would say to juniors "Live long", "Grow great", "Be fortunate". Nobles, for the poor, would only raise the hand without bowing, or along with raising the hand repeat these same words *taslim* and *adab*, or say *bandagi*. But the way of equals in replying was that if greetings were exchanged while passing on the road they would say *taslim* or *adab* bowing in the same way, and if they were sitting in some gathering they would stand fully up and reply bowing.

After the greeting one would say to the other "*Mizaj-e sharif*" (Your noble health?) or "*Mizaj-e aqdas*" or "*Mizaj-e 'ali*" or "*Mizaj-e mubarak*" or "*Mizaj-e mu'alla*", and the other would say with joined hands "I pray for you". Throughout almost all India this is the manner of greeting and enquiring after health of cultivated and polished people. But in Lucknow, and in a few cities where an Indian State is established and some court exists, greater care is taken in performing these forms, and any falling short in them is considered bad manners.

But now for some time, especially in Lucknow, among the common people, artisans and the lower classes, owing to the disappearance of the former court and its manners, saying "*As-salamu 'alaikum*" has become very customary. Would to God the nobles too followed it, and the distinction between low and high were entirely removed.
