The second group of male dancers is the *bhand*s. The manner of their performance is this: a young, good-looking boy whose hair is long like a woman's, wearing bright and glittering clothes and with bells tied on his feet, dances and sings. The accompanying music is steeped in rhythm and stirs the heart. His dancing has unusual nimbleness, playfulness and cleverness, and his singing too suits the same style and taste. Besides the accompanists there are seven or eight or more *bhand*s, who raise shouts of "Bravo!" at his dancing and singing, beat time as they are moved, and often, with indecent excesses, keep making laughable remarks on his movements, postures and graces. And as soon as the boy has sung for a while, they come forward and do their impersonations and display their skill in repartee and mimicry.

In Lucknow there are two groups of these people: one the Kashmiris, who came from Kashmir, and the other natives of this place, whose profession was at first something else, but for whom mimicry has now become their special art.

Mimicry, and especially mimicry together with dance and song, was a very old art of India, which was highly advanced at the court of Raja Bikramajit, that is, even before Christ. But at that time dramas of the highest order were shown in it, and the truth is that it was a most refined and polished mimicry. At the ceremonies of the lower castes of India it is still the custom that when they dance and sing themselves, they also perform comic impersonations along with it.

In the Muslim period before the Mughal empire no trace is found of *bhand*s and mimics. Possibly they existed, and the chroniclers of that period did not think them worth notice. But under the Mughal empire the *bhand*s had won special prominence. They are traced from the time of Aurangzeb Alamgir, when the nobles and sovereigns of Delhi had been released from the toils of conquering and governing the country and had come to regard courtliness and pleasure-seeking alone as their ancestral right. But in fact these *bhand*s did strange things in the society here. They are the national satirists of this country, and they did very nearly the same work that the *Spectator* and the *Tatler* did in England. The first *bhand* of Delhi is said to have been Karela, who lived in the time of Muhammad Shah. Angry about something, Muhammad Shah ordered, "Drive the *bhand*s out of our country." The next day, when the King's procession went out, the sound of drums beating and *bhand*s singing came from above. Raising his head in surprise he saw Karela and some *bhand*s perched up a date palm, beating their drums and singing. He stopped the procession and asked: "What is this impertinence? And why has our order not been obeyed?" They said: "Refuge of the World! The whole world is under Your Majesty's sway; where could we go? So we resolved to go to the world above, and this is the first stage." At this answer the King and all his companions burst out laughing, and their offence was pardoned.

After coming to Lucknow these people were so highly valued that Lucknow became the real centre of these troupes. As far as I know, there are at present no *bhand*s in Delhi, or if there are, they are very few and unknown. In Bareilly, it is true, there have been troupes of *bhand*s from old times, and many of the *dom*s and *dhari*s (hereditary musicians) of Lucknow came from Bareilly, which shows that the Khans of Rohilkhand too were patrons of music and entertainers, through whose generosity these people flourished well in Bareilly and Moradabad, and from there too accomplished *dhari*s and mimics came to Lucknow. Their real centre, however, has now become Lucknow.

Their jokes, their sallies of banter and their wonderful feats of mimicry have remained famous in Lucknow. We have already told our readers the cutting remark a *bhand*, at Nawab Saadat Ali Khan's prompting, made before the greatest *banka* of the time. Another incident of the same period deserves to be remembered. Some noble gave a shawl as a reward, but the shawl was worn and old. One mimic took it in his hand and began to examine it closely, fixing very intent eyes on it. Another asked, "What are you looking at?" He said, "I am looking to see what is written on it." He asked, "Well, what is written?" He took out his spectacles, put them on, and read haltingly with great difficulty: "*La ilaha illallah*" (There is no god but God). He asked, "Only that? Isn't '*Muhammadur-rasulullah*' (Muhammad is the Messenger of God) written?" He answered: "How could '*Muhammadur-rasulullah*' be written? This is from before our Prophet's time!"

A Nawab Sahib of Lucknow was known as "the Nawab of the Pond" (*Garhiya-vale Nawab*), because there was a pond near his house. At his house there was a gathering of dance and song on some occasion. A *bhand* came before the gathering in a flurry and said to all his companions, "Get up, get up, pay your respects!" They all said, "To whom should we pay respects? Is there anyone here?" He said, "The Nawab Sahib is coming." And saying this he opened a pot, and a big frog leapt out and sat in the middle of the gathering, and he began to say to everyone, "Get up quick, get up quick!" His companions asked in astonishment, "But for whom should we get up?" He said, "Haven't you recognised him? This is the Nawab of the Pond!"

It was said of these people that whoever's house they went to dance at, they would certainly impersonate him, and it was impossible that they should not have a dig at him. And the truth is that the beautiful way in which these people taught lessons to the nobles and grandees and warned them of their lapses would have been possible in no other way. In the same way, in their mimicry they put on so complete a disguise of the person they impersonated, and showed so true a character, that people were enraptured. Just as nowadays "Babu English" is mocked in the company of the English, so in those days the Persianised Urdu of the Kayasths of this place was mocked. These *bhand*s used to impersonate them, and show the character of the *Divan-ji* (Kayasth clerk), at so high a level that people were lost in wonder.

Here a second Karela the *bhand* lived until the time of Nasir-ud-Din Haidar. After him Sajjan, Qaim, Rajabi, Naushah, Bibi Qadr and others became famous. Ali Naqi Khan, with his wife, who held great sway, came to see Qaim's *sabil* (stall for giving water in Muharram), which he decorated finely and at which he served sherbet. As soon as he saw these honoured visitors, Qaim came forward and said with joined hands: "May God keep the Nawab Sahib safe and keep the Begum Sahib *qaim*!" [TN: a pun: *qaim rakhe* means "keep steadfast", but also "keep with Qaim".] It was so cutting a remark, yet both the Nawab and the Begum could only give him a reward. Qaim's feat was that once for three and a half hours he did nothing but make faces of every kind.

In the last period the troupes of Fazl Husain, Khilauna, Badshah Pasand and Kya Khub were very famous. Even now Ali Jan is something to be thankful for. These are the names of the dancers of those troupes who won great renown in dance and had no equal.

But the *domni*s (women singers of the *dom* caste) had a greater influence on Lucknow society than all these. In all the small towns and every city there have been for ages *mirasan*s and *dharhin*s who sing at weddings, whose style, like that of the *daf*-players, always stayed the same. But the *domni*s made wonderfully marked progress in Lucknow. Leaving the *dhol*, they took up the *tabla*, *sarangi* and *majira* (small cymbals) like the courtesans and male troupes. Advancing beyond singing alone, they began to dance; and not content with that, they began to perform impersonations too in women's gatherings, like the *bhand*s. They became the greatest element in all the wedding ceremonies, and so captivated the begums of wealthy households that there was no palace and no noble house in which some troupe of *domni*s was not employed. Many of them were matchless in singing and dancing, and had such radiant voices that the women's gatherings became grander, more attractive and more delightful than the men's. Above all, their playfulness and inventiveness in the gatherings were so charming that men often longed for some chance to see a performance by *domni*s, for the *domni*s would on no account consent to sing and dance in the company of men. Even now there are *domni*s in great numbers, and in the same style and manner, but the mastery has gone. Such famous *domni*s, with such a sense of rhythm and such fine voices, have passed away in Lucknow as no singers anywhere can have equalled.
