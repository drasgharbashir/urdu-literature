No one knows exactly when the settlement of Lucknow was founded, who its founder was, or where its name comes from. But this is what can be said, drawing on the traditions of various families and on conjecture.

They say that when Raja Ramchandra, having conquered Lanka and completed his term of exile in the forest, took his seat on the throne, he gave this land as a fief (*jagir*) to his brother Lachhman, his companion and fellow sufferer. Through Lachhman's residence or arrival here a settlement grew up on a high mound by the river, which from that time was named "Lachhmanpur", and the mound became known as "Lachhman Tila" (Lachhman's Mound). In this mound there was a very deep cave or well whose bottom no one could find, and it was popularly said to go all the way down to Shesh Nag [1]. This belief stirred feelings of devotion, and Hindus would go in good faith and pour flowers and water into it.

It is also said that Raja Janamejaya, grandson of Maharaja Yudhishthira, gave this region as a fief to ascetic holy men, *rishi*s and *muni*s (sages), who built their hermitages (*ashram*s) on every inch of it and became absorbed in meditation on Hari. After a long time, seeing them weak, two new peoples came from the Himalayan Terai and took possession of the country. They resembled each other and seemed two branches of one race: one was the "Bhar" and the other the "Pasi". It was with these people that Sayyid Salar Masud Ghazi fought in the year 459 of the Muhammadan era (1030 CE), and probably it was they whom Bakhtiyar Khalji attacked in 631 of the Muhammadan era (1202 CE). [TN: Sharar dates by an era he calls *sanah-e-Muhammadi*; the Common Era years he gives in brackets are reproduced as printed.] So the Muslim families who first came and settled in this land were among those who came with these two invaders, and especially with Sayyid Salar Masud Ghazi.

Besides the Bhars and Pasis, Brahmins and Kayasthas were already here. All these people together founded a small town here and lived in peace and security. But it cannot be said when the name of this settlement changed from "Lachhmanpur" to "Lucknow". The present name cannot be traced before the Emperor Akbar; but it cannot be denied that a sizeable population of Hindus and Muslims was already here, as can be proved by an event recorded from long ago in the family traditions of the Shaikhs of Lucknow: in 969 of the Muhammadan era (1540 CE), when the Emperor Humayun was defeated by Sher Shah at Jaunpur, he fled from the field by way of Sultanpur, Lucknow and Pilibhit. In Lucknow he rested for only four hours; and though he came defeated, with no power or authority, the people of Lucknow, out of pure human sympathy and hospitality, presented him in those few hours with ten thousand rupees and fifty horses. From the fact that this was got together in so short a time it may be supposed that there was a considerable population here then, and that the Lucknow of those days was livelier and more prosperous than most country towns (*qasba*s) of today.

Among those who came in that early time was the family of Shah Mina, whose radiant tomb is visited by the people to this day. Probably among those who came at the same period was Shah Pir Muhammad too, who settled on Lachhman Tila itself and was buried there. Because of his residence the old mound changed from Lachhman Tila to "Shah Pir Muhammad's Tila", and with the passage of time the deep cave was filled in. Later the Emperor Aurangzeb, who came here in person, built a fine, strong, beautiful and splendid mosque on it, which still raises the cry of *Allahu Akbar* (God is Most Great) on Alamgir's behalf.

In 1019 of the Muhammadan era (1590 CE), when the Emperor Akbar divided all India into twelve provinces, Lucknow appears to have been made the seat of the governor or ruler of the province of Awadh. At that time a man of the Bijnor district called Shaikh Abdur-Rahim, an elder in poor and distressed circumstances, happened to reach Delhi in search of a living. There he gained influence among the nobles of the court and was admitted to the imperial presence. At last he was enrolled among the imperial *mansabdar*s (holders of official rank) and received a fief in Lucknow, and a few days later he came to live on his fief with great pomp and display. Settling here on Lachhman Tila, or Shah Pir Muhammad's Tila, he built his Panch Mahla (Five Palaces) and the Shaikhan Gate, and he was buried in Lucknow. His tomb is still known as the "Nadan Mahal", a building that the Government of India took under its protection only a few days ago, having found it worthy.

At this time Shaikh Abdur-Rahim built a small fort on another height near Lachhman Tila, stronger than the forts of the neighbourhood, which greatly impressed the people round about. Either because Shaikh Abdur-Rahim had been granted the fish standard (*mahi maratib*) by the imperial court, or because one building in the fort had twenty-six arches and the builder carved two fish on each arch, making fifty-two fish, this fort became known as "Machchhi Bhawan". The word *bhawan* either means a fort, or is a corruption of *bawan* (fifty-two). The builder who built this fort was an Ahir called "Lakhna", and they say the city was named Lucknow after him. Others think that Lachhmanpur itself was corrupted into Lucknow. Whichever is true, the settlement got this name after Shaikh Abdur-Rahim came.

Some time later, besides Shaikh Abdur-Rahim's family, that is, the Shaikhzadas, a group of Pathans arrived, who settled to the south and became known as "the Pathans of Ramnagar". They fixed the limit of their landholding at the place where the Gol Darwaza (Round Gate) now stands, because from there towards the river the Shaikhzadas' land began. After these Pathans a new group of Shaikhs came and settled to the east, who are called "the Shaikhs of Nahra". Their land was where the ruins of the Residency now lie.

These three groups each held their own territory and ruled their own circles, but the Shaikhzadas' influence prevailed over all and weighed on the neighbourhood, chiefly because they had influence at the Delhi court. Several of them had been appointed governor of the whole country of Awadh, and the strength of their fort, Machchhi Bhawan, was so famous that people used to say: "Whoever holds Machchhi Bhawan holds Lucknow."

Lucknow had begun to progress in Akbar's own time, and its population kept growing and spreading. It is true that some governors of Awadh were chosen from these Shaikhzadas, but the usual practice was for notables of Delhi to be appointed to this post. They sat at home year after year, made a sort of tour only at revenue-collecting time, and their deputies lived here. So no progress for the city could be expected from them. But when one or two of the local Shaikhzadas were appointed governor, Lucknow did benefit from their appointment.

It seems, however, that Akbar took a special interest in Lucknow. He granted a hundred thousand rupees to the Brahmins here for the *Vajpeyi* offering (a Vedic sacrifice), and from that time the Bajpai Brahmins of Lucknow became famous. From this we learn that the oldest Hindu quarters of Lucknow, which existed in Akbar's time, are Bajpai Tola, Kasari Tola, Sondhi Tola, Banjari Tola and Ahiri Tola, all of them round about the Chowk.

Mirza Salim, who on taking the throne became known by the title Nur-ud-Din Jahangir, founded Mirza Mandi, west of Machchhi Bhawan, in his father's lifetime while he was still heir apparent. At the end of Akbar's reign the governor here was Jawahar Khan. He himself lived in Delhi, but his deputy, Qazi Mahmud of Bilgram, founded Mahmud Nagar to the right and Shahganj to the left, adjoining the Chowk on its south, and between them and the Chowk built the Akbari Gate in the Emperor's name.

In Akbar's time, while these buildings were going up and these quarters were being settled, Lucknow had become a good centre of trade, and had reached such a degree of progress that a French merchant who dealt in horses tried to profit by staying here. He obtained from the imperial court a certificate of safe-conduct (*sanad-e-mustamini*) [2] for residence in Lucknow, set up his stables here, and in the very first year prospered so much that he built four splendid houses next to the Chowk. When at the end of the year he sought to renew his safe-conduct, he was not allowed to stay longer; and when he meant to stay on by force all the same, the city authorities, by imperial order, confiscated his houses for the government and expelled him. The four houses remained in government hands for a long time, until in the reign of the Emperor Aurangzeb Alamgir, when Mulla Nizam-ud-Din of Sihali, worn out by the feuds in his home town, decided to settle in Lucknow, the four houses were given to him as a government grant. He came with his whole family and settled in them, and together with many houses round about they are called "Farangi Mahal" (the Frenchman's Palace) to this day. Through the blessing of the Mulla's arrival Lucknow became a centre of learning and a resort and refuge for students; and this pre-eminence in learning grew so great that the syllabus compiled by Mulla Nizam-ud-Din, called the *Silsila-e-Nizamiya* (the Nizami course), has for a long time been the syllabus not of India alone but of all Asia, and it is believed to hold saintly blessings as well as learned excellence. From this one may well imagine from how many places, and from how far away, students must have gathered in Lucknow in that early age.

The European traveller de Laet, who was touring India in 1061 of the Muhammadan era (1631 CE), that is, at the beginning of the Emperor Shah Jahan's reign, writes of Lucknow: "It is a great market town." In Shah Jahan's time the governor here was Sultan Ali Shah Quli Khan. He had two sons, Mirza Fazil and Mirza Mansur. In their names he went on south of Mahmud Nagar and founded two new quarters, Fazil Nagar and Mansur Nagar.

At that time there was a cavalry captain here called Ashraf Ali Khan. He founded Ashrafabad in the same line, and his brother Musharraf Ali Khan built his house on the other side of the stream (*nala*) and set up another quarter called Musharrafabad, whose name has with time become Naubasta. In those same days there was another army officer called Pir Khan, who went far to the west of all these quarters and built his small fort (*garhi*), and the place is called "Pir Khan's Garhi" to this day.

The Emperor Aurangzeb Alamgir made a journey to Ayodhya for some purpose. On his way back he stopped at Lucknow and then went on to Delhi. On this occasion he built the mosque on Shah Pir Muhammad's Tila, which, being on Lachhman Tila itself, stands on a height than which no more fitting place for a mosque could be found in Lucknow. And it was probably on this occasion that he presented the houses of Farangi Mahal to Mulla Nizam-ud-Din, the most learned man of the age.

In the time of Muhammad Shah "Rangile" (the Pleasure-Loving), the governor of Lucknow was a brave Hindu cavalry commander called Girdha Naga. His uncle Chhabile Ram held the government of Allahabad on behalf of the Delhi court. When Chhabile Ram died, Girdha Naga rebelled and meant to make himself ruler of Allahabad in his uncle's place by force; but then, on second thoughts, he declared his obedience and submission, and the court granted him the robe of office (*khil'at*) of the governorship of Awadh. He settled here, and his wife, who was called the Rani, founded Rani Katra.

But whoever was ruler and governor here, the Shaikhzadas were so powerful that no governor, however mighty and with whatever charter of authority he came, dared set foot in their circle. Although Machchhi Bhawan had the standing of the seat of government, the Shaikhzadas had made it their hereditary property, and no governor who came from Delhi was allowed near it. They had built two more buildings near Machchhi Bhawan, one called the "Mubarak Mahla" and the other the "Panch Mahla". Of the Panch Mahla some say it was a five-storeyed building, and others that five palaces stood next to one another. To their south was a great, imposing gateway called the "Shaikhan Darwaza" (the Shaikhs' Gate). People from the city who wished to go to the Shaikhzadas' buildings had to pass through this gate.

In the arch of this gate the swaggering Shaikhzadas had hung a naked sword, and the rule was that whoever wished to come in, whoever he might be and however great, must first bow and salute this sword and only then step forward. Who dared refuse to obey this order? Even the governors and officials who came appointed from Delhi, when they went to meet the Shaikhs, had willy-nilly to bow their heads before this sword.

Such was the state of Lucknow when, in 1162 of the Muhammadan era (1732 CE), Nawab Saadat Khan Burhan-ul-Mulk came from the Delhi court as governor of Awadh, and with him was laid the foundation of this last Eastern court in India, whose rise I wish to describe as the last example of Eastern civilisation. In the first number I showed the state of Faizabad, which was the first sketch of this civilisation and an appendage of this same Eastern court of Lucknow. In this number I have shown the picture of Lucknow before this court was established, and set before my readers the board on which this court laid out its game of chess. In the next few numbers I shall describe the history of the rule of this Nishapuri dynasty, and after that show what this civilisation was, and what it was like.

# Notes

[1] Sharar's note: In Hindu mythology Shesh Nag is the name of the thousand-headed serpent that holds up the earth on its hood, and is a revered manifestation of divine power and majesty.

[2] Sharar's note: *Mustamin* means one who seeks safety. Since Europeans saw danger for themselves among Muslims and Hindus, wherever they wished to stay they would obtain from the Delhi court a certificate of safe-conduct for that place, so that officials, authorities and subjects alike would not trouble them. Because such a certificate laid responsibilities on the state, it was seldom granted for more than a year.
