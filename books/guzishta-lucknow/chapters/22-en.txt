# (21)

Now we wish to show how the art of music, and with it the people connected with this art, fared in Lucknow.

Singing is among the things that human nature invented first of all. When people felt the urge to show emotion in uttering words they began to sing, and in the movements and actions to which their feelings moved them they began to dance. And since the greatest ardour, rapture and absorption are found in worship, and in worldly matters the most uncontrollable and unbearable ardour is in the expression of love and passion, singing too began at first in worship and love. In India singing certainly began with worship, for the first singers here were Brahmins, who in the beginning, while performing worship and leading it, used to sing *bhajan*s (hymns) in praise of their deities. The birth of Kanhaiya-ji made love for him and passion for him a form of worship and gave rise to amorous music.

And this is why in India both poetry and music are expressed through a woman's voice. At first the Brahmins used to sing only *git* and *sangit*, that is, plain simple songs in which there was not the slightest trace of artistic development. But afterwards, through the mingling of the tastes of the Egyptians or the Babylonians and of Iranian researchers, the foundation of an art was laid, and first of all the seven notes (*sur*) were invented, for every sound, as it spreads, changes on reaching a certain point. By calculating these changes exactly, the researchers invented the seven notes.

After this, music in India was divided on this basis: the *rag*s sung in worship were either in praise of Brahma (the divine creative power), or in praise of Bishan (Vishnu, the divine power that sustains and nourishes), or in praise of Mahesh, that is, Mahadev (the divine power that destroys). Accordingly three kinds of *rag* arose. Of the *rag*s of the first kind it is said that the Brahmins told them to no one and took them with them to the grave. The *rag*s concerned with all the stages of life, the lying-in chamber, marriage and all the affairs of the world, became the second kind. The last kind of *rag*s were connected with the state after death and with reward and punishment. They were often awesome and fearsome and impressed on the heart the transience of the world. Amorous *rag*s too were included in this kind, simply because the lover longs for death, and especially because Kanhaiya or Shri Krishna-ji was himself an incarnation of Mahadev. [TN: in Hindu tradition Krishna is an incarnation of Vishnu, not of Mahadev (Shiva); the statement is Sharar's.] *Rag*s of this kind were generally called *parag*. Their *rag*s are *Bhairon* and *Saras Rag*, and their *ragini*s *Bhairvin*, *Paraj*, *Kalangra*, *Sohni*, *Sindh*, *Pilu* and so on.

After this, when the Brahmins had to sing odes in praise of the rajas at their courts, *rag*s of awe, majesty and grandeur suited to them were invented, such as *Malkaus*, *Darbari*, *Shahana* (*Adana*) and so on.

The Muslims had brought their own music with them. Their music had first been codified and perfected by Ibn Misjah. After this, when the Abbasid court was established in Iraq, a new and most perfect art of song arose from the mingling of Arabic and Persian music, which spread through the whole world, and this in the end was Persian music. The Muslims brought this art to India, and the *qawwal*s of today are the memorial of the singers who came here with them. Their instruments of pleasure are the *sarod*, the *chang*, the *shahnai* (*surnai*), the *barbat* and the *rabab*.

In India the Muslims left their mark on everything. They changed all the sciences and arts and all the ways of social life. But they could have very little effect on the music here, the reason for which is generally thought to be that the music here was itself so regular and of so high an order that, because of its strength and regularity, it simply could not be affected by outside influence. But the truth of the matter and its real cause is that a man turns his attention to the music of a country and a language only when he has become a native of that country, and its language and social life have coloured him. So after coming here, the invading Muslims paid no attention to the music of this place as long as they remained Arabs or Persians. And when they did pay attention to it, Indianness had already entered their veins and sinews. They had forgotten their national *rag*s and were devoted to the melodies of this place. By then they were no longer capable of making any alteration in the music here, or of criticising it at all.

Even so, the melodies of the Persian *qawwal*s did have some little effect on the music of India. So several of their *rag*s were included in Indian music. *Zangula* (*Jangla*), *Zilaf*, *Shahana*, *Darbari*, *Fala'* (*Khamach*) and others are thought to be Persian *rag*s that have entered the Indian art of song.

Amir Khusrau acquired both arts and made great efforts to combine them. It is said that he invented the *sitar*, and certainly many tunes are of his invention. But it is very difficult to discover what particular things Amir Khusrau added to the music of this country.

Among the Muslims it appears that the Sufi elders turned to music before the kings did, and the gatherings of *hal-o-qal* (ecstasy and recitation) that had been held as a form of worship among the early ascetics of Iraq and Persia were established in India too. And the singers who had formerly sung *bhajan*s in the temples began to sit in the circles of Muslim ascetics and Sufis and sing *ghazal*s of mystical knowledge.

At the courts of the kings too the singers of this country and singing and dancing courtesans were always present, but their chief officer was usually some Persian singer, who certainly left some mark of his own on their music. In the time of Muhammad Tughluq the greatest singer of the court was Amir Shams-ud-Din Tabrizi, and all the entertainers (*arbab-e-nishat*), women and men, were under his command. In those days, next to Deogarh, that is, Daulatabad, there was a whole settlement of entertainers, called "Tarababad" (Abode of Delight). In the middle of its crossroads bazaar there was a tower in which every day after the afternoon prayer the headman (*chaudhri*) of the entertainers would come and sit, and before him all the troupes of singers and courtesans would come in turn and sing. Most of them were Muslims who kept the fast and the prayer. There were mosques here and there in this settlement in which the *tarawih* prayers were said in the blessed month of Ramazan. Great rajas came here to hear singing. Several Muslim crowned heads too had come here and heard singing. Since the leaders and headmen of the musicians were generally Muslims, it is plain how quickly the Arab, Persian and Indian arts of song must have mingled.

The centres of Hindu music in northern India were Mathura, Ajodhya and Benares, where, because the religious element was paramount, the art of music was always nurtured. Of the Sharqi Sultans of Jaunpur, Sultan Husain Sharqi was very fond of music. He was himself acknowledged as a great singer. And since both Ajodhya and Benares were within his realm, he must certainly have done great service to this noble art of India.

Akbar valued this art so highly that the greatest and most famous singer of his age, Tansen, was included among his "Nine Jewels" (*Nau Ratan*). Seeing this attention of a Muslim emperor to song, he himself, or his son Bilas Khan, became a Muslim. Through court patronage Indian music advanced day by day in this family. Singers of this line went on being honoured at later courts. So to this day the people of this family think of themselves as attached to the Mughal court. It is generally believed that this art of the Hindus came to the Muslims through this line. But the events we have described make it plain that the Muslims had acquired this Indian accomplishment long before this family. So at present all the masters of Indian music and all the famous singers are Muslims.

In Delhi the first book on this art, *Shams-ul-Aswat*, was written in the reign of the Emperor Shah Jahan; it is now nowhere to be found. Then, in the time of Akbar the Second, a gentleman named Mirza Khan composed with the help of pandits and Sanskrit scholars the book *Tuhfat-ul-Hind*, of which only one or two copies remain with a few people. In it he has gathered many Indian arts. Where it discusses various arts such as *jotish* (astrology), *sarodha*, *samudrik* (palmistry), *kok* (erotics), *nayika-bhed* (the classification of heroines), *indrajal* (magic) and others, it also explains Indian music. [TN: *Tuhfat-ul-Hind* is usually dated to Aurangzeb's reign; Sharar's dating is followed here.]

Things had advanced only so far in Delhi when this delightful art moved to the court of Lucknow, and the patronage and generosity of Nawab Shuja-ud-Daula brought together the musicians of all India on the soil of Awadh. Here the old schools of music of Ajodhya and Benares were already established. Some memorials of the patronage of the Sharqi Sultans of Jaunpur also survived. When the accomplished singers of Delhi and the masters of music of Tansen Khan's authoritative school came and joined them, a special glory arose, and in fact a new era of music began.

Of Shuja-ud-Daula the author of the history of Faizabad writes that he was very fond of entertainers. Thousands of singing courtesans, generally from Delhi, and from other cities far and near, had come and gathered here. It had become the general custom that when the Nawab Wazir's servants and all the nobles and army commanders marched anywhere, the tents of the entertainers and courtesans went along with them.

The result of this was that in the time of Nawab Asaf-ud-Daula Bahadur the book *Usul-un-Naghmat-ul-Asafiya* was written in Persian. No better book on the art of Indian music has been composed to this day. Although very few copies of this book are available either, I have one and have read it. The author is mature in mind, a man of learning and scholarship, and appears to have full command of all three languages, Arabic, Persian and Sanskrit; he has made a very successful effort to impress Indian music on everyone's mind with great clarity. The late Asadullah Khan Kaukab, who died only a few days ago, was a master of music of the highest learning and was well known in Calcutta as a professor of Indian music. He writes to me of this book in one of his letters: "I have this Persian treatise on music. It has been written with great research and precision, drawing its contents from the authoritative books that are the ancient stock-in-trade of this science." Alas, this unrivalled book has not been printed to this day, and its copies are so rare that there is a danger of its perishing. If some noble would attend to it, he would do a great service to the country and to its ancient history.

This treatise itself shows how far music had advanced in the Lucknow of Asaf-ud-Daula's time. Its author appears to have been a great scholar, who has also explained clearly the principles of Arabic and Persian music, beginning from Ibn Sina's book the *Shifa*. To complete this article in *Dilgudaz* we had asked the late Professor Kaukab for help. We publish as it stands what he wrote to us in reply. It will show clearly what the state of the art of music was after it came to Lucknow. Alas, he is no longer in the world, or we should have had much more help from him, especially as he wished to have his new book, which is unrivalled in the art of music, printed by us. After acknowledging the advance of music in Asaf-ud-Daula's time, he writes:

"In the time of Nawab Saadat Ali Khan, music was blighted. In the time of Ghazi-ud-Din Haidar there was in Lucknow a very great and consummate master of this art named Haidari Khan. Because of his eccentric temperament this gentleman was known as 'Crackpot Haidari Khan' (*Sare Haidari Khan*), and he lived in Golaganj. Ghazi-ud-Din Haidar was very eager to hear him sing, but had never had the chance. One afternoon Ghazi-ud-Din Haidar went out for a ride along the river bank in his *havadar* (open sedan). Under the Rumi Darwaza people saw 'Crackpot Haidari Khan' walking along. They said to the King, 'Refuge of the World! This is Haidari Khan.' The King was eager in any case, and ordered, 'Call him.' People caught him, brought him and stood him before the King. The King said, 'Well, Mian Haidari Khan! Why do you never let us hear you sing?' He said, 'Yes, sir, why shouldn't I? But I don't know where your house is.' The King burst out laughing and said, 'Very well, come with us. We shall take you to our house ourselves.' Saying 'Very good', he went along without ceremony. They had come near the Chhattar Manzil when Haidari Khan flew off the handle and said, 'I'll come, all right, but I'll sing only if you give me *puri*s and cream to eat.' The King promised, and sitting in the palace began to hear him sing. After listening only a little while he was greatly delighted. A state of ecstasy came over him, and he was beside himself and overcome. Seeing this state, Haidari Khan fell silent. When the King asked him to sing again, he said, 'Your Highness! This tobacco that is filled in your *pechvan* (hookah) smells very good. From whose shop do you get it?' Ghazi-ud-Din Haidar was himself of an unsettled temperament and was known as a crackpot. When he was put out by this question, his companions said, 'Refuge of the World! This fellow is a crackpot, sure enough. He hasn't even understood yet whom he is talking to.'

"Now, at the King's bidding, people took Haidari Khan into another room. They gave him *puri*s and cream to eat and a hookah to smoke. He sent for a quarter-seer of *puri*s, an eighth of a seer of cream and a pice's worth of sugar and had them sent to his wife (which was his custom everywhere). While he was busy with these things the King drank cups of pure wine. And when the drink had taken hold, he remembered Haidari Khan again. He had him called at once and ordered him to sing. But as soon as he began his song the King stopped him and said, 'Haidari Khan! Are you listening? If you merely please me and do not make me weep, remember that I shall have you drowned in the Gomti.' Now Haidari Khan's wits were set spinning. He understood that this was the King. He said, 'Your Highness! God is the master,' and began to sing with all his heart. By God's power, or say it was Haidari Khan's luck to live, in a little while the King was moved. He began to weep uncontrollably, and said in delight, 'Haidari Khan! Ask, what do you ask?' He said, 'Will you give me what I ask?' The King promised, and Haidari Khan, having made him give his word three times, said, 'Your Highness! I ask that you never send for me again or hear me sing.' The King asked in astonishment, 'Why?' He said, 'What is it to you? You will have me killed. Then there will never be another Haidari Khan like me. And when you die, another king will come at once.' At this answer Ghazi-ud-Din Haidar turned his face away in anger. Seizing this chance, Haidari Khan ran for his life and came home.

"In short, in the time of Ghazi-ud-Din Haidar this was the one accomplished musician in Lucknow. In the time of Nasir-ud-Din Haidar there were thousands of singers, but no singer of this rank. The times of Muhammad Ali Shah and Amjad Ali Shah were periods of sobriety, for Muhammad Ali Shah had the insensibility of old age, and Amjad Ali Shah did nothing without asking His Reverence the *Qibla-o-Ka'ba* [TN: the chief *mujtahid*]. So in their time, even if some pleasure-loving nobles of the city were patrons of the entertainers, they listened to singing in secret. So whatever appreciation this art now received came with the accession of Wajid Ali Shah in the prime of his youth, when Lucknow's cup of pleasure was about to brim over, and the lamp that was about to go out flared up and burned bright for the last time."
