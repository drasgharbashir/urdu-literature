We have described the garment for the middle part of the body, so now we turn to that part of dress which is reserved for the head, and it is this garment that is held in greater honour and respect in India, because just as the head is distinguished above the whole body, so its covering too should be more distinguished. From ancient times the custom of tying the turban (*pagri*) has come down in India. Although the Arabs and Persians also came here wearing turbans (*'amama*), and owing to the establishment of their rule a great change took place in the turbans here, it cannot be said that there was no turban here before the coming of the Muslims.

The turbans of the Muslim rulers of the early period were very large, and accordingly in those days the turbans of all men of honour, nobles and the wealthy were probably also large, with pointed conical caps of old Turkish style beneath them, which are current and to be seen in Afghanistan to this day, and have been taken from there into the uniforms of our Indian army.

In the period of the Mughal empire turbans began to grow smaller day by day, and the reason is that just as in cold countries, to escape the harm of cold, dress grows heavier and thicker as time passes, so in hot countries it keeps growing lighter, thinner and briefer. How heavy and thick were the clothes in which the earlier Muslim conquerors came here we can estimate only by conjecture, but we are seeing with our own eyes how much lighter, thinner and briefer the dress of the English and of their women is growing day by day.

According to this same principle turbans here grew lighter and smaller day by day, and this tendency of the country went on affecting the style of the court too. In the last period of the Mughal court the turbans of the nobles and *mansabdar*s (ranked officers) had become very light, and this same love of brevity brought it about that turbans took on hundreds of shapes, and many nobles devised for themselves special ways of tying and small turbans of a special style.

The shrinking of turbans caused the Turkish cap (*kulah*) to be given up, and things came to such a pass that no one had a cap under his turban at all; and if some did wear one, it was a tiny cap of some very fine cloth that would blow away at a breath. We do not know with certainty what style these caps were. Probably their cut resembled that of the caps now on the heads of *shaikh*s (spiritual masters) and *faqir*s: that is, a band six or seven fingers wide is made into a ring the size of the head, and gathered at the top with pleats.

But in a few days the need was felt to take off the turban and lay it aside at home and in informal company. But since it is improper to go bareheaded, some kind of cap should always remain on the head. To meet this need a *kamarkhi* cap [TN: apparently "star-fruit-shaped", from *kamrakh*, the star fruit] was invented in Delhi, taken from the style of a crown, in which four corners stood out above the round band that was worn on the head. A cap of this style is still to be seen on the heads of some nobles and princes of Delhi. In the true sense of the word this cap was called the *chaugoshiya* (four-cornered). Within a few days the process of modification and abrogation began in this cap too, and in Delhi itself a round dome-shaped cap was invented by taking out those *kamarkhi* corners, in which four panels (*pan*) were cut and joined in such a shape that an elongated dome appeared on the head. Wearing this cap people came to Lucknow, and from that time the influence of the Lucknow court began to act upon it. Here the first modification was that long *surahi*s (flask shapes) were made on the joins of the panels, and handsome moons were set between these *surahi*s. These moons and *surahi*s were made in this way: in panels of fine *tanzeb*, *surahi*s and moons cut from *nainsukh* (fine cotton cloth) were stitched on the inside, which, showing through on top, gave the cap a fine refinement, neatness and simplicity. This cap was very much liked here. The common people suddenly gave up tying turbans, and this cap began to be seen on the head of every cultivated and polished man.

General popularity improved its cut further. The elongation was stopped and a most suitable roundness was produced, and moulds of wood and copper were invented so that these *chaugoshiya* caps (which had brought with them the name of the old *kamarkhi* caps of Delhi) could be stretched on them and made well domed and round.

Meanwhile came the time of Nasir-ud-Din Haidar, when the Shi'a faith was flourishing greatly in Lucknow and was making reforms according to its own taste in everything: religion, politics, civilisation and social life. Opposition to the four Caliphs and love of the *Panjtan* (the Five Holy Persons) had made the number four disliked and the number five beloved in the court society of Lucknow. Its effect on the cap was that, according to some reliable accounts, by the direction of His Majesty himself five panels were put into this *chaugoshiya* cap instead of four, so that it came to have five *surahi*s and five panels, and its name too was fixed as *panjgoshiya* (five-cornered) instead of *chaugoshiya*. But the modification made in the cap itself became so permanent that four-panelled caps vanished entirely, and no one even remembered that they had ever had only four panels; yet the name *chaugoshiya* could not be wiped out. It survives to this day and is the one on people's tongues. Although some people do say *panjgoshiya*, more people are such as still call this five-panelled cap *chaugoshiya* to this day.

Nasir-ud-Din Haidar, King of Awadh, originally devised this five-panelled cap specially for himself, and in his lifetime none of his subjects dared wear it. But the people of the city liked this style so much that as soon as he closed his eyes, high and low alike adopted it, and this same round dome-shaped cap was to be seen on the heads of all the cultivated and polished people of Lucknow.

A few days later, out of the needs of winter, very fine caps of the same kind with gold work were invented, in which in all five panels, on a ground of *zarbaft* (gold brocade) or gold-sprigged cloth, a silk ground of another colour was set and moons and *surahi*s were made with gold cord (*qait*), and in the winter season there was no cap but these on the heads of all men of style. After this, when *chikan* (white embroidery) came into use, *chaugoshiya* caps of the same work for the hot season began to be made of such high refinement and beauty that they were prepared by a whole year's labour, and their price reached ten or twelve rupees.

At this same time a prince of Delhi arrived in Lucknow, whom the court and society greatly honoured. He used to wear a *dupalli* cap (two-piece cap), in which two long thin pieces cut in an oval shape to suit the size of the head were joined together. This simple cap of his pleased many people, because it was comparatively simpler and easier to make. Many people adopted this cap, and it became so current among the common people that today this same *dupalli* is the national cap of India. That prince became famous among the people here as "the prince of the *dupalli* cap", and the heads of millions of people are to this day under obligation to his invention and cut. Indeed in the last period of the monarchy, taken from this same *dupalli*, a very small *teli* cap was invented here, from which two points stood out, before and behind. This was called the *nuke-dar* (pointed) cap, and caps of this kind with heavy work were reserved for princes, wealthy and influential royal kinsmen and Nawab-zadas of high rank.

In short, up to the time of the Mutiny two kinds of cap were current among the people of Lucknow: first, the *chaugoshiya*, which was reserved for cultivated and serious people; and second, the *dupalli*, which was current from princes down to the lower classes with slight variations of style, and is the general wear today.

Probably from the time of Ghazi-ud-Din Haidar or Nasir-ud-Din Haidar, a round cap also came into use among particular people, which was called the *mandil*. Its shape was like a *dafli* (tambourine), and it was mostly preferred in *karchob* work. The wealthy and some Nawab-zadas adopted it, considering it more dignified and handsome, and it was given the distinction that no one could go before the King and the princes without tying a turban or wearing a *karchob* *mandil*. In short, the *mandil* was given a place at court. Derived from this *mandil* was the round cap whose upper corners were slightly rounded, and which was called the *jarneli* (general's) cap. It was generally of black velvet, with genuine work of real gold *kalabattun* (gold thread) on it. Originally this cap had been given to the white soldiers in the army of the English Government, and outwardly it had the air of a uniform; but probably the first instance of imitating the English was just this, that this military and general's cap entered the dress of princes and nobles of family.

The last King of Awadh, Wajid Ali Shah, devised a new and strange kind of court cap for the titled dignitaries of his court. In it, stiffened with pasteboard, a round band of plain satin or *karchob* work was made, which was higher at the forehead. To its top a large bag (*jholi*) of *tanzeb*, *grant* or net was made and joined, and when it was worn the bag hung down behind to the nape of the neck and lay on the back of the head. The King had named this court cap "*Alam-pasand*" (pleasing to the world), and the common people mostly called it the "*jhola*" (bag). But it was so unpopular and disliked a style that even in Wajid Ali Shah's lifetime it could not be seen outside his court on the heads even of those to whom it had been granted, and after him it so completely vanished that the people of today have perhaps never even seen it.

After the Mutiny a great revolution suddenly began in the world of caps in Lucknow. For some days there was no headwear except the *chaugoshiya*, the *dupalli*, and *mandil*s or turbans. After this the use of the *chaugoshiya* cap suddenly began to be given up, until now only a few old heads of style remain for it. Of the heads left empty of these caps, most adopted the *dupalli*, but some began to look for novelties. For some days there was the era of the needle-worked *mandil*-like caps of Meerut. After this the English nightcap or the long tasselled woollen Kashmiri caps came into use. Then, derived from their style, thin caps of *grant* or satin were adopted, which, growing briefer and briefer, had come close to the *dupalli*. Now the fashions of the English period began, and headwear resembling theirs began to be sought. Some gentlemen, shutting their eyes on every side, began without hesitation to wear the hat or the English nightcap; but now the era of the Turkish cap (*Turki topi*, the fez) had begun. This cap had been adopted by the late Sayyid Ahmad Khan, and he had matched it with coat and trousers for the Muslim gentleman. For this reason at first this cap was looked upon with the greatest aversion. It got the name of "the *Necharis*' cap" [TN: *nechari*, "naturalist", a hostile name for Sayyid Ahmad Khan's followers]. Thousands of gibes were made at it in the newspapers, but Sir Sayyid's steadfastness did not let up until it had made it current. In his own lifetime hundreds of thousands of men began to wear it, until it reached Lucknow as well. Here too many began to wear it in defiance of its opponents, and inwardly people's inclination towards it grew so much that now throughout India most educated and cultivated Muslims are using this cap.

In Lucknow honoured, educated and polished Shi'as are perhaps more numerous than in all the cities of India, and among them the impulse, as compared with the Sunnis, to distinguish themselves in everything and to fix their own distinct emblems and styles is stronger. With this there is also the fact that just as the Sunnis are partisans of the Ottoman Empire, the Shi'as are followers and partisans of the Qajar dynasty of Iran. So when the use of the Turkish cap, which is the cap of the Turks, began to grow in Lucknow, Shi'as of style thought that instead of the Turkish cap they should adopt for themselves the *papakh* (lambskin cap) of the Persian court. This movement did its work completely, and now the situation is that those Muslims who leave their old caps and adopt a new cap, if they are Sunnis, begin to wear the Turkish cap, and if they are Shi'as, adopt the Persian cap of Iran. Although in both parties there are also some enlightened people who wish to wipe out this internal doctrinal division among Muslims, and who, though Sunni, wear the Iranian cap or, though Shi'a, the Turkish cap, such people are few. The general style of the newly-minded Muslims of the city is that Shi'as wear the Iranian and Sunnis the Turkish cap.

Seeing this mutual division among the Muslims, educated Hindus generally adopted the round *mandil*-like felt cap, which some Muslims also wear, but owing to its having entered so widely into the style of English-knowing Hindus, the English have named it the "*Babu* cap". But the common people, whether Hindu or Muslim, Shi'a or Sunni, wear only the *dupalli*.

The period that passed after the Mutiny was for the society of Lucknow a time of tremendous coming-into-being and passing-away. Along with social life, morals and habits, change began in people's dress and style too, and in the educated class there arose in great numbers people who, along with their social life, gave up their style entirely. There were no longer trousers (*paijama*) on their legs, nor *angarkha* on their bodies, nor the upturned shoe (*charhwan juta*) on their feet, nor cap or turban on their heads; rather, in a single leap they jumped over the seven seas and landed from India in England, and coat, trousers, boots and hat became their dress. But the predominant group of the population wished to keep its style; nonetheless, without their feeling it, change took place in them too, and instead of the *angarkha* the *sherwani* became their national dress. But for the head it seems that no cap has yet been chosen which everyone will adopt without hesitation.

In this time of coming-into-being and passing-away and of change, scores of caps arose in Lucknow, which were either inventions of this very place or taken from some other people or place. Of them, those that could last a few days the genuine taste of Lucknow also altered a great deal, but in the end they were given up. The natural inclination of the people of Lucknow is that everything should be as delicate, refined, small, close-fitting and light as possible. They applied this taste to every style and garment, and caps too mostly underwent alteration of this kind. But in the Turkish cap, the Iranian cap and the hat these people could make no alteration at all, the reason being that these caps are taken ready-made from other peoples and come from outside. And because of this impossibility of alteration, it is our view that not one of these caps, though they have become widely current, will be able to become the national dress here, being foreign to the taste of Lucknow. And the question of the cap is still under consideration and proposal in the assembly of the inventors of dress.
