# (10)

Persian poetry really took its rise from the *masnavi* (long narrative poem in rhymed couplets), and this form of poetry has always been thought the most important and weighty. It began with Firdausi's epic *masnavi*, the *Shahnama*. Then Nizami, Sa'di, Maulana Rum, Khusrau, Jami, Hatifi and others won the highest fame and renown in it. In Urdu, Mir Taqi Mir wrote many little *masnavi*s during his stays in Delhi and Lucknow, but they are so short and ordinary that even to mention them in an account of *masnavi*s seems out of place.

The writing of *masnavi*s in Urdu began with Mir Ghulam Hasan Hasan, son of Mir Zahik, who had come to Lucknow with his worthy father as a child. It was in the society of this place that he grew up, here that he was brought up, and in the lap of this place's air that his poetry was nurtured; for the education and the society that made him write his *masnavi* "Benazir and Badr-e-Munir" were purely those of Lucknow. At the same time Mirza Muhammad Taqi Khan Hawas wrote the *masnavi* *Laila Majnun*, and the taste for the *masnavi* began to grow in Lucknow. In the time of Atish and Nasikh there was something of a lull, but when this taste rose again, Pandit Daya Shankar Nasim wrote *Gulzar-e-Nasim*, Aftab-ud-Daula Qalaq wrote *Tilism-e-Ulfat*, and Nawab Mirza Shauq wrote *Bahar-e-Ishq*, *Zahr-e-Ishq* and *Fareb-e-Ishq*; and these won such wide fame and such universal popularity that verses from these *masnavi*s were on the lips of high and low alike. Earlier than this, someone had written a *masnavi* called *Lazzat-e-Ishq* in answer to Mir Hasan's *masnavi*; because it was published with Nawab Mirza Shauq's *masnavi*s it came to be attributed to him, but in truth it is neither his nor of his time.

Compared with all these *masnavi*s, *Gulzar-e-Nasim*, for all its general popularity, is full of hundreds of mistakes. Reading it, one sees a delicate-minded novice who wants to put every kind of poetic beauty into his verse, but, lacking mastery of expression, stumbles at every step and nowhere achieves his aim. In answer to it Agha Ali Shams, a very practised poet, wrote a *masnavi* in the same metre in which, while keeping free of mistakes, he displayed great skill in similes, metaphors and wordplay. But alas, that *masnavi* has perished and could not overcome the fame that *Gulzar-e-Nasim* had already won. In Delhi in those days Momin Khan wrote a few short, matchless *masnavi*s, which became very popular and well known.

In Momin Khan's poetic taste, subtlety of thought was carried far. He built the structure of his poetic invention on imaginary similes and metaphors. In his *masnavi*s he mostly personified imaginary feelings and qualities, and so gave his verse a special charm. A pupil of Momin Khan, Nasim of Delhi, came to Lucknow and made such an impression at the *mushaira*s here that many people became his pupils. Nasim of Delhi brought his master's manner to its full brilliance in Lucknow, and his pupil Taslim of Lucknow showed in the Urdu *masnavi* the flights of fancy of Naziri, Urfi and Sa'ib, and set up in Urdu verse living, breathing Faizis and Ghanimats. More recently, Maulvi Sayyid Ali Haidar Tabatabai Nazm of Lucknow has presented to the Urdu-reading public, under the name *Saqi-nama-e-Shafaqatiya*, a moral poem in condemnation of wine so matchless that it cannot be answered. In short, if one leaves aside a few short *masnavi*s of Momin Khan, the writing of the Urdu *masnavi* both began in Lucknow and advanced here.

Some people compare and weigh the language of Delhi and Lucknow by means of Mir Hasan's *masnavi* and *Gulzar-e-Nasim*, an idea to which Maulvi Muhammad Husain Azad gave still more force. But in the first place, even if *Gulzar-e-Nasim* did become famous, like Nazir Akbarabadi's *Banjara-nama*, to set it beside Mir Hasan's *masnavi* is a grave insult and humiliation to Urdu poetry. If a true comparison can be made, it is between Mir Hasan's *masnavi* and the *masnavi* *Tilism-e-Ulfat*. And even if the language of *Gulzar-e-Nasim* is by force accepted as the language of Lucknow, a comparison of Mir Hasan's *masnavi* with *Gulzar-e-Nasim* is not a comparison of the poetry of Delhi and Lucknow, but a comparison between the earlier and later languages of Lucknow itself; for Mir Hasan's *masnavi* is a specimen of Lucknow's first language, and this of its last.

An important and very ancient form of poetry is the *marsiya* (elegy). In ancient Arabic poetry it was mostly elegies and *rajaz* (martial verse) that served as the means of showing excellence in poetry. In Persian the elegy had grown weak, but when the Shia faith flourished in Iran under the Safavid kings, poets turned to the elegy to keep fresh the memory of the sufferings of the Prophet's family (*Ahl-e-Bait*). Maulana Muhtasham of Kashan wrote a matchless elegy of a few stanzas, which was generally admired. After that it became the custom for poets now and then to compose an elegy or two in mourning for Husain. But the standing of elegy-writing in the world of poetry was so low that there was a saying, "A failed poet becomes an elegist." Then, when, by the fortune of religion, the kingdom of Awadh became the successor of the late Safavid state, the advance of mourning assemblies (*majlis*) and the fervour of mourning (*azadari*) in Lucknow gave elegy-writing such patronage that this art began to rise to extraordinary heights; and indeed the whole secret of Lucknow's rise lies hidden in this historical fact. India was ruled by the Mughals, who made Persian the court language, and Persian culture was the centre of their aristocratic life and of all their accomplishments. The result was that every Iranian, as soon as he arrived in India, was treated with the greatest honour, and his every action and manner was looked upon with approval. Because the religion of the kings of the Delhi empire was Sunni, the Iranians concealed many of their ways and could not blossom in the gatherings there as fully as they really were. The court of Awadh was Shia, and its ruling family had come from Khurasan itself. So here the Iranians opened out completely; and as they blossomed all the more for appearing in their true colours, so much the more, because of their shared faith, did the people of the court here begin to adopt their manners and customs; and Iranian-ness, which had in truth been nurtured in the lap of Sasanian and Abbasid splendour, within a few days permeated the society of Lucknow.

In short, in the time of Sauda and Mir, Mian Sikandar, Gada, Miskin and Afsurda were elegists, who used to compose short poems on the martyrdom of Imam Husain and recite them in the assemblies. After them Mir Khaliq and Mir Zamir advanced elegy-writing greatly, and the present form of the elegy was invented in their time. At last time brought Mir Zamir's pupil Mirza Dabir and Mir Khaliq's son Mir Anis to the high seat of renown, and these two masters displayed such poetic excellences in elegy that they shone like sun and moon in the sky of poetry. The same rivalry that had existed between Mir and Sauda, and between Atish and Nasikh, now arose between Mir Anis and Mirza Dabir. Mirza Dabir had grandeur of diction, loftiness of thought and the force of learning. Mir Anis had those qualities of a simple, unaffected language that rules over human feelings, which cannot be learned and come only by the grace of the Bountiful Source. These two masters raised the art of the elegy above all the other forms of poetry, and created in Urdu literature those new things that minds, under the influence of English education, had begun to seek.

Anis and Dabir had brought elegy-writing to such a degree of perfection that, instead of being a thing to be ashamed of, it had now become the greatest poetic accomplishment. All the people of Lucknow became such admirers and devotees of these two masters that the whole city was divided into two parties: every judge of poetry was either an Anisiya or a Dabiriya, and there was always rivalry between the two parties.

Along with elegy-writing, Mir Anis also made the recitation of the elegy (*marsiya-khwani*) an art. It is said of certain Greek orators and speakers that they made particular efforts to give their speeches effect, and produced effect in their speech through the rise and fall of the voice and changes of manner and gesture. In the long life of Islam it was Mir Anis alone who revived this most necessary art on principle. The art of changing the voice to suit the words, of making the face to match the subject, and of strengthening speech with fitting movements of the limbs and hints of the features, is the invention of Lucknow alone, and of Mir Anis's family in particular, and efforts to advance it continue to this day. And if our speakers, to give effect to their eloquence, were to become the pupils of these masters, they would prove most successful speakers.

Arabic and Persian literature was entirely devoid of drama, the art of speech that is the life of Western poetry, and because Urdu was the pupil of Persian, no attention was ever paid to it in Urdu either. There were dramas of the highest order in Sanskrit, but the later society of India had become wholly unacquainted with them. The deeds of Ramchandra-ji and Shri Krishna-ji were indeed shown among the Hindus with religious ceremony, but Urdu poetry had no connection with them. The story of Ramchandra-ji was shown in open fields, like the Olympia in England, in the style of epic mimes, and the story of Shri Krishna-ji appeared on religious stages in the form of dance, song and music, exactly in the manner of opera; these were called *rahas*. Wajid Ali Shah developed a particular interest in the *rahas*, and taking its plot as his model he prepared a drama of his own, in which he would become Kanhaiya-ji, or, as a *jogi* (ascetic) tormented by love, would light his ascetic's fire, while many women, dressed as fairies and lovelorn *gopi*s (milkmaids), went about searching for him. Then, when the gates of the Qaisar Bagh fairs were opened to the common people too, the art of drama began to advance of its own accord among the enthusiasts of the whole city, and within a few days this taste had advanced so far that some well-known poets too, following the taste of the time, began to try their hand at writing drama. So, alongside Wajid Ali Shah's enthusiasm, Mian Amanat, a practised poet, wrote the *Indar Sabha*, and, like the companies of the present day, different troupes began here and there in the city to stage his *Indar Sabha*, in which in some places women and in others boys acted. Charming tunes were set for this *Indar Sabha* according to the principles of music, and the whole city was eager to see performances of it. Seeing the success of Mian Amanat's *Indar Sabha*, others too were inspired, and many dramas of this kind were invented, and all of them came to be called *sabha*. So many *sabha*s were set up in the city, such as those of Madari Lal and others, with changed plots.

The new style of the *sabha* brought such liveliness to the city that, apart from the *Indar Sabha*, people did not care for any other kind of dance and song. There was a clamour of *sabha*s on every side, and the foundation was laid for presenting the old love stories to the public, in imitation and according to the taste of society, in good verse and with an appealing treatment. There is no doubt that the Parsi theatres, by their skill in management and the allure of their spectacle, made the colour of the *sabha*s fade; but do not think that the old taste for drama that was invented and became current in Lucknow has died out. In the first place, the Parsis too took this thing from Lucknow: their first public play was Amanat's *Indar Sabha*. And in spite of this, at all the community gatherings of Lucknow scores of performances like *Sehre*, *Harish Chandra* and so on are given to this day, and a regular body of actors of this taste has arisen which entertains the common people even though the national taste has died out among the gentry. In any case there can be no doubt that the foundation of Urdu drama was laid in Lucknow itself, and from here it spread throughout India.

One kind of Urdu poetry is the *wasokht* (poem of a lover's estrangement). These are amorous six-line poems (*musaddas*) of a particular kind, whose subject is usually this: first the declaration of one's love, then a head-to-toe description (*sarapa*) of the beloved, her faithlessness; then, quarrelling with her, making her believe that one has fallen in love with another beloved; praising the beauty and charm of this imaginary beloved to make the beloved jealous, teasing her, taunting her, and so breaking her pride before making up again. This kind of Urdu verse began in Lucknow itself. Nearly all the poets of the middle period wrote *wasokht*s and created great charm in them. In Delhi too various *wasokht*s were written in the later period, especially by Momin Khan, who wrote several very good ones; but the beginning was in Lucknow.

The pleasure-loving temperaments of the nobility also brought into being several other forms of poetry whose beginning had been in Delhi itself. Of these the most worthless is *hazl* (obscene comic verse), and the somewhat more charming is *rekhti* (verse in women's idiom). *Hazl* began in Delhi with Jafar Zatalli, who probably lived in the time of Muhammad Shah. I have looked through his verse from beginning to end. Apart from obscenity and shamelessness beyond all bounds, one sees neither any poetic merit nor any charm of language. After him a writer of *hazl* of Bilgram, of the soil of Delhi itself, with the pen name Sahib-Qiran, came to Lucknow and shone here. His name was Sayyid Agha Ali, and he arrived in Lucknow in the time of Asaf-ud-Daula. It appears that he grew up among the young nobles of Lucknow of vulgar taste. His *diwan* is to be found, and although the verse is obscene and miles away from decency, there is still something in it: along with poetic merits it has the full charm of the language and its idioms. But in the last period of Lucknow this art was brought to perfection by Mian Mushir, who was a pupil of Mirza Dabir.

I must say here, without regard to the partisan feelings of Shias and Sunnis, that when a Shia kingdom was established in Lucknow, Shi'ism, keeping its true colour, began with complete freedom to develop every one of its principles. The Shia faith rests on two things: one is *tawalla*, that is, declaring love for the noble Family of the Prophet and the house of prophethood; and the other is *tabarra*, that is, declaring one's dissociation from the enemies of this revered family, which, as mutual rivalry and partisanship grew, took the form of abuse and execration. In principle the Sunnis too share this belief with them, but the difference arose that the Sunnis hold the first three successors of the Prophet to be the best of mankind after the prophets and messengers and the true successors of the Prophet, while the Shias call them usurpers and oppressors. And when, in their beliefs, these elders too were counted among the enemies of the Prophet's family, *tabarra* against them also became obligatory. If cultivated and learned people kept this within the limits of the true meaning of the word "dissociation", the common Shias, following their own taste, began to let loose their tongues in abuse and execration of them, and this became the root of the partisanship between Sunnis and Shias.

These two religious things had a most excellent and fitting influence on the poetry of Lucknow. If *tawalla* took the art of the elegy into its embrace and raised it above all the forms of poetry, the zeal for *tabarra* against the enemies of the Prophet's family took up the old art of satire (*hajv*) and developed it under the name of *harziya-goi* (scurrilous verse). Several masters of this art became famous in Lucknow, but alas, this thing was bound to be offensive to the Sunnis in particular. In the royal period swords would be drawn over it, and even under English rule criminal cases and lawsuits arise from it now and then to this day. The result was that the composing and reciting of *harziya* never dared to leave the four walls of the house. If the whole subject of *harziya* had not been so narrow and contentious, the world would have seen what feats the *harziya* poets of Lucknow displayed even in their absurdities and obscenities.

In this art the greatest fame was won [illegible] by Mian Mushir, the pupil of Mirza Dabir. The merits of the language and the style of speech he adopted to make a gathering roll about with laughter and give the listeners stitches are beyond description. To create charm even in vulgarity and make it fit to present before refined people was his special gift, which no one before or after him was granted.

In connection with *hazl* the name of Mian Chirkin too must be mentioned. In the middle period of Lucknow there was a lively, very able noble of fine taste named Ashur Ali Khan. The company at his house was one of the most perfect examples of the society of that time. It was he who produced Jan Sahib and Chirkin, and some say that Sahib-Qiran too grew up in his company. Chirkin brought urine and excrement into every verse, and such a stench comes from his verses that our readers' brains will have rotted at merely hearing his name. But because he had a kind of distinction, we have mentioned him. His verse also has some poetic merits and good similes, but his taste has made even these merits filthy and foul.

But the art of *rekhti*, for all its want of refinement, is entertaining, and not offensive like Chirkin's poetry. In every language there is some little difference between the idioms and tone of men and of women, but not as much as is seen in our language. This distinction exists in Persian, Arabic and every language, but Urdu goes further in this peculiarity. The old taste of Persian and Arabic was that when women wrote poetry they wrote it in their own language, and when men sometimes expressed an idea through the mouth of women they adopted women's language to give charm to the diction. The same is the case with English. Urdu poetry has always remained solely in the language of men, so that even when women write in it they write as men. They adopt the very language of men and use even masculine forms for themselves. If the poet's name is not known, no one can tell whether the verse is a man's or a woman's.

It was only the third or fourth period of Urdu poetry when the idea arose among playful young men that, on the model of *rekhta* (Urdu verse), a *rekhti* should be invented. Mir Hasan had composed in this language in his *masnavi* where it was needed, and up to that point there was no harm. Mian Rangin, who was a native of Delhi and used to take part in the gatherings of Lucknow, adopted this manner permanently. At first the company of cultivated people thought this manner shameless and contrary to good breeding. So in the conversation we have recorded, in Sayyid Insha's words, between a cultivated elderly gentleman of Delhi and a courtesan of the same city, Nura, in Lucknow, the gentleman says: "And above all, hear one more thing: Saadat Yar Khan's son thinks himself the Anvari of *rekhta*; his pen name is Rangin. He has composed a tale and named the *masnavi* *Dilpazir*. He has put the speech of courtesans into it. He is consumed with envy of Mir Hasan, though that departed man too had no sense at all. He did not compose the *masnavi* of Badr-e-Munir, he was as good as a peddler of *sande*-oil [TN: oil of the spiny-tailed lizard, sold by street hawkers as an aphrodisiac; proverbial for quackery]. How can one call that poetry? All the people of Delhi and Lucknow, from courtesans to men, recite:

She went off from there, lifting her skirts, / clinking bangle against bangle.

So poor Rangin too has composed a tale in the same way. Someone should ask him: 'Brother, granted your father was a cavalry officer, but the poor man was one who wielded spear and lance and swung the sword. So where did you get such ability?' And the coarseness in his nature comes from whoring. So, leaving *rekhta*, he has invented a *rekhti*, so that the wives and daughters of decent people may read it and be smitten, and he may blacken his face with them. Now what sort of verse is this?

Just find out where Rangin's house is; / from here, what is the fare, palanquin-bearers?

He says this being a man. A line: 'Let it not happen that I, wretched woman, am killed.' And he has made a book in which he has written down the slang of courtesans, in which there are *upar-valiyan chilen*, *upar-vala chand*, *ujli dhoban*, and so on and so forth." [TN: these are examples of courtesans' cant expressions, left in transliteration; their precise meanings are uncertain.]

But the cultivated old men died complaining, the gaiety of the young men did not stop until it had advanced Rangin's taste, and *rekhti* became an art of Urdu, which, although invented by a poet of Delhi, was invented in Lucknow and flourished here. To what high degree of perfection Nawab Mirza Shauq, after Mir Hasan, brought this language in the course of narrative cannot be praised enough. One may read on page after page and not discover whether the needs of versification have anywhere taken any liberty with the spoken language. But in the *ghazal* (lyric poem) Rangin's successor was Jan Sahib, an ordinary man of Lucknow, who had been turned on Ashur Ali Khan's lathe. Although other *rekhti* poets too arose in Lucknow after Jan Sahib, excellence and fame came to an end with him. He wrote *ghazal*s, *wasokht*s and several other poems as well.

If *rekhti* had avoided the taste for obscenity and vice and adopted the feelings of chastity, this art would to some extent have been capable of advancement; but the trouble was that its very foundation lay in the emotions of vice and the thoughts of unchastity. So the *rekhti* poets always stepped off the path of decency and moderation, and though the language may have gained to some extent from it, morals suffered.
