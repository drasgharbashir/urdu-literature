The ceremonies of rejoicing which we have mentioned, and of whose women's gatherings we showed a general picture on the last occasion, are explained in detail as follows. The *chhathi* is the name of the ceremony when, after childbirth, the mother and child are bathed for the first time. Bathing the woman in childbed with very hot water is a medical treatment, but since this bath after birth takes place on an occasion of joy, great importance is given to it. And since this first bath generally takes place on the sixth day after childbirth, it got the very name *chhathi* (the sixth). In it the mother is bathed with great care, then the child is bathed, and after them all the women who are to bathe bathe one after another. New heavy suits are prepared for the mother and child according to one's means, and at the same time all the women change their clothes. The various customs observed at this bathing are without limit or number, and probably in every city and village, indeed in every family, they are the same in general and different and new in detail.

From the young mother's parents' home or other relatives, on this occasion, suits for the mother and child, a *tauq* (neck ring), a *hansli* (collar ring) and bangles, toys suitable for a little baby, rattles, *chatwe* (small toy cooking pots), and with them chickens and God knows what other things come with great pomp, procession and bands. In the women's quarters gatherings of dance and song are in full swing, and if there are not the means for this, the women of the house themselves set a drum (*dholak*) before them and sing and play.

The same splendour attends the two later bathings, that is, the bathing of the twentieth day and of the fortieth day (*chilla*). If God has given ease, there is a gathering of pleasure and gaiety on both occasions; otherwise there is more pomp only at the *chilla* bathing, and the ceremony of the twentieth-day bathing is ordinary.

The *'aqiqa* is a purely religious rite of the Muslims, whose beginning comes down among the family of Abraham from the time of the Children of Israel. The Jews, on the eighth day after birth, used to take the child to the Masjid-e Aqsa (the Temple in Jerusalem), shave its head and make a sacrifice, and their religious leader would pray for a blessing on it in particular ways [TN: the author's account of Jewish practice is not accurate; the eighth-day rite in Judaism is circumcision]. This same practice has continued among the Muslims to this day in the character of the rite of Abraham and the *sunnat* of Muhammad. Although now the restriction of the *'aqiqa* to the eighth day after birth has been lifted, it mostly takes place within the first year of the child's life. In it the child is bathed and dressed in new clothes, and after this the barber (*na'i*) shaves its head in a gathering of relatives and friends. And as soon as he puts the razor to the head, two goats are sacrificed if the child is a boy, and one if it is a girl. After the shaving, sandal paste is put on the head. Relatives and kinsfolk give the child some *ru-numa'i* (gift for seeing the face) according to their means. The meat of the sacrifice is distributed among the poor and the relatives, and there is a gathering of rejoicing in the house, and the same kind of gathering is arranged as at other ceremonies.

*Khir-chata'i*. With this ceremony begins the giving of foods other than milk to the child, which usually takes place when the child is four or five months old. In most houses the feeding begins with *khir* (rice pudding), which is cooked with special care and given to the child to lick, especially in the presence of the ladies of the family, while it is wearing new clothes. And all the ladies put money in its hand with prayers for long life, and the same gathering of merriment is held that is seen at every ceremony.

*Dudh-barha'i*. This ceremony takes place on the occasion when the child is weaned. In it *khajur*s (date-shaped fried sweets) are generally cooked, so that if the child is obstinate for milk they may be given into its hand to distract it. But it is generally the custom to cook so many that they can also be distributed among all the houses with which there is exchange of portions. The general way of weaning is that aloes or some other bitter thing dissolved in water is applied to the breasts of the mother or wet-nurse, and the child, alarmed by its bitterness, gives up the milk. And when it is obstinate to drink and will not be distracted, the same procedure is repeated. And in one or two times it comes to dislike the milk. The time of *dudh-barha'i* is generally when the child has become two years old. Among the Hanafis the period of suckling is two and a half years, that is, after two and a half years weaning is obligatory, but the custom is for a shorter time than this. It is another matter that some women go on suckling for three or four years, but this is generally looked on with disfavour, because it is against the *shari'a*. At this ceremony too, in houses to which God has given the means, there is a very good bustle, and a gathering of dance and song is in full swing.

*Bismillah*. This ceremony takes place on the day when the boy is first seated to read. And by current custom its time is considered to be when the child has become four years, four months and four days old. And this number four has acquired such special importance in this ceremony that after four years, four months and four days, four hours and four minutes are also observed. At the appointed time some respected Maulvi Sahib or some elder of the family sits down to teach the boy, who has been bathed, washed and dressed in new clothes and made a bridegroom (*dulha*). They set the alphabet book before him, and making him say *Bismillah* (In the name of God), make him say the Arabic words of prayer "*Rabbi yassir wa la tu'assir wa tammim bi'l-khair*", the meaning of which is: "O Lord, make it easy and do not make it hard, and bring it to a good end." Then, making him say *alif*, *be*, sweets are distributed. Relatives and kinsfolk give the boy according to their means, and from that day his education begins.

*Khatna* (circumcision). This too is the *sunnat* of Abraham and an old and necessary rite of the family of Abraham, and since in India it is peculiar to the Muslims alone, and it is thought that after this procedure the boy becomes a Muslim, the general name of this rite has become "*Musalmani*". In it the skin at the tip of the child's private member is cut off, the cutting of which is also very beneficial by medical and doctors' principles for preventing certain diseases and complaints. It is a kind of operation which our old surgeons (*jarrah*), who are generally barbers, perform with great skill and extraordinary quickness. They are given a good fee and reward. And when performing this rite, many relatives and friends are invited and seated in the men's quarters, and in the women's quarters there is a gathering of lady guests. As soon as the circumcision is done, sweets are distributed. Those who have the means give a feast. And then there is a ceremony of rejoicing on the day when, after the wound has healed, the boy takes the bath of recovery. In many families, and in households that have made a vow, on that day the boy is made a bridegroom and mounted on a horse, and a wedding procession (*barat*) goes with great procession and pomp to some shrine (*dargah*), where, after offering a sheet (*chadar*) and sweets, the boy comes back home in the same state, where chirrupings of joy and gatherings of pleasure and gladness are seen. The time of performing this rite varies. Some people have the child circumcised at the *chhathi* or *chilla* itself, but the general custom is when the boy has become six or seven years old.

There is another ceremony too, the *roza-kusha'i* (opening of the first fast). It takes place when a boy or girl reaches the age of nine or ten and is made to keep a fast for the first time. In it generally many fasting people are invited, for whom an abundance of *iftari*s (fast-breaking foods) are prepared, and the boy sits with them and breaks his fast. And if it is a girl, she opens her fast in the women's quarters with fasting lady guests. In this there is little singing and playing, but for fashionable and romantic people even this pretext is enough to set a gathering of dance and song going.

Proceedings of the same kind take place at ceremonies of the bath of recovery and on occasions of the fulfilment of a vow. And apart from the particular things connected with that ceremony, all the other things in them too are the same as were described for the other ceremonies.

The greatest and most important ceremony is marriage, or the marriage contract (*'aqd-e nikah*). This is the necessary ceremony through whose excesses hundreds of families go on being ruined and destroyed. And the reason is that in the ardour of joy and the absorption of embracing the beloved of their desire, no one keeps in mind either his condition and means or his end and final outcome. The result is that by borrowing, selling property, begging from friends and relatives, or getting together a sum in whatever way it can be had, the longings are fulfilled; and as soon as the wedding is over, the state of things is that in many houses it comes to starvation.

Since marriage and *nikah* are the most important event of human life, we wish to describe it in somewhat more detail and explanation. A marriage engagement is mostly settled through *mashshata*s. In all the great cities of India, especially in those where the former civilisation had advanced, there is a particular profession of women, *mashshata-giri*. In the language of the poets and in the dictionary, *mashshata* means the woman who combs and plaits the hair of high-ranking ladies, dresses them in clothes and jewellery, and adorns and decks out brides. But in society *mashshata* is the name for those women who carry proposals of marriage, settle engagements and arrange marriages. Probably this profession had its beginning with those women who adorned and dressed beauties, and in the end the women who settle marriages got the name of *mashshata*. These are very clever and cunning women. When they take any boy's proposal to a house, they praise his wealth, education, dutifulness, good manners and beauty so much that they do not rest until they have proved him, in the eyes of the girl's family, to be Prince Benazir of Mir Hasan's *masnavi*. In the same way, when they take some girl's proposal to the boy's family, they spin such tales in describing her beauty and loveliness, her airs and graces, and her excellence and charm, that it seems the girl they are speaking of is not a human being but a fairy of Mount Qaf or Princess Badr-e Munir.

After the *mashshata*'s carrying of messages, although it is the men who make enquiries and investigation, in settling the engagement it is mostly the women of both houses who have the greater say; they satisfy themselves and obtain the consent of the men, and the engagement is settled. In both families, as soon as children are born, mothers full of longing settle engagements; for them there is no need of a *mashshata*, but the bridegroom gets a betrothed bride without any courtship or love's troubles, and the customs before marriage that relate to settling the engagement do not arise. It is as if the betrothal takes place at birth.

When a proposal goes to new houses, the boy is often invited with a few of his relatives and particular friends to the bride's family's house under the name of "*bar-dikhawa*" (showing the groom), and seated in such a place that the women too can peep at him and see him. The men of the house gather, meet him and entertain him according to their means. In the same way the boy's mother and sisters go to the bride's house on an appointed date, and on the pretext of feeding her sweets or some other pretext see the bride's face, which is generally hidden from them and kept in *purdah*. But in some respectable houses the bridegroom is not invited; rather, the men of the family see the boy by some means or other without his knowledge and find out about him. And in the same way the girl's condition too is found out.

When by these means the boy's family approve the girl and the girl's family the boy, in which, besides looks, condition and standing, the respectability of the family also has a great deal of say, the rite of betrothal (*mangni*) is performed. In it sweets go from the bridegroom's side, ornaments of flowers go, and a gold ring goes, which in some households the bridegroom's female relatives go themselves to put on her.

After the rite of betrothal has been performed, it is understood that the engagement is settled, and from that time it becomes the custom on both sides that whenever there is any ceremony, portions are sent with special care to the *samdhiyana* (the other family by marriage), and the portion that is for the boy or girl is larger and is made especially substantial and dignified. If in the meantime Muharram comes, then from both sides braid (*gota*), cardamoms, *chikni dali*s (boiled betel nut) and high-class *karchob* and silk purses (*batwa*) are sent to the *samdhiyana* with care and trouble.

A few days before the *barat*, that is, the day of the *nikah*, the bride is seated in *manjha*, when she is dressed in the yellow suit of the *manjha*. From that time *ubtan* (cosmetic paste) is applied to her every day, and except for particular needs she does not come out from *purdah*. On the day she sits in *manjha* it is the custom that her leftover *ubtan*, her leftover henna, a *kuza* (pot) of sugar candy and many *handi*s (pots of sweets) are sent to the bridegroom's house with a splendid procession and band. The *handi*s that are specially for the bridegroom are distinguished and set apart on separate trays. With them is the heavy yellow *manjha* suit for the bridegroom, a painted and decorated *chauki* (low stool), and a *lota* and *katora* (water pot and bowl). The *lota* and *katora* are tied tight to the *chauki* with a *nara* (cord). And in the procession these things are in this order: after the musicians and the procession, the *chauki* is ahead of everything. After it come the bridegroom's special things on trays, which are generally put in stone dishes. And after them come ordinary *handi*s on many trays. The bride's younger sisters and the *domni*s go riding in *pinas*es and *doli*s (litters), who on reaching the bridegroom's house cut a *pindi* (ball of sweet) and the sugar candy into seven pieces each, and feed all those pieces to the bridegroom, keeping them covered.

It is conjectured of this rite that it is a purely Indian rite, connected neither with Arabia nor with Persia, because the origin of the *manjha*, and with it the *kangna* (wrist thread), is not established anywhere but in India.

When ten or twelve days or more have passed after the *manjha*, the *sachaq* goes from the bridegroom's house to the bride's with the same pomp and procession. *Sachaq* is a Turkish word and a Turkish rite, and it appears that the Turks and Mughals brought this rite with them to India. In it the *charhawa* (bridal) suit for the bride goes from the bridegroom's house, which is generally very heavy and *karchob*-worked. With it there is for the bride a *sehra* (wedding veil of strands) of gilt and painted work, a silver *chhalla* (plain ring), a gold ring, one or two other things, and the jewellery in which the bride will be sent away. And there are ornaments of flowers. With the suit go sugar-coated sweets (*nuql*), sugar cakes and dried fruit. For the *sachaq* painted and coloured pots (*ghara*) are specially made. Then four pots each are set in multicoloured frames of bamboo and paper to make *chaughare*. And according to the dignity of wealth and nobility the number of these *chaughare* goes on increasing, and often reaches a hundred or two hundred. But inside them there is nothing except a counted few *nuql*s or a quarter or half seer of sugar. On the mouths of the pots a cloth of *suha* (bright red) is generally tied with a *nara*. And in the procession in front of all these pots there is a silver curd-pot (*matki*) filled with curds, on whose mouth too a *suha* cloth is tied with a *nara*, and round its neck one or two fishes are also tied for good omen. When these things reach the bride's house, they are distributed among relatives and kinsfolk.
