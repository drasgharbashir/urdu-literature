Of the history of this court's rulers, all that now remains to be told is this. When Mirza Birjis Qadr Bahadur fled from Lucknow, he did not stop until he reached the frontier of Nepal. About a hundred thousand people went with him. They meant to take refuge in the Himalayan valleys and, when they had the chance, to come out and attack the English: if they won, they would return home; if they lost, they would flee to the mountains again. But this was not a plan that could last. The state of Nepal could neither shelter so many people nor fight the English for them. It had not the strength to stand against the English. So the government of Nepal gave refuge only to Mirza Birjis Qadr and his mother, but ordered the reckless multitude with them, in no uncertain terms, to go back at once, and if they would not go, to be driven out by force; the territory of Nepal was to be cleared of them immediately. The result was that they all came out and fled. Many were killed. Many slipped away in disguise in one direction or another. And Mirza Birjis Qadr and his mother went to live in Nepal itself. The court of Nepal fixed some small stipend for them, and it is said that all the jewels they had with them passed to the state of Nepal. At last Hazrat Mahal was buried there, and after her death, on the occasion of Queen Victoria's Jubilee, the British government pardoned Mirza Birjis Qadr's offence. When he was allowed to come home, he slipped away from Nepal without telling anyone and reached Calcutta. Here Wajid Ali Shah had died, and Mirza Qamar Qadr, as the eldest son, was drawing the largest allowance. Birjis Qadr claimed that he was more honoured and more deserving than all the King's sons; that, according to the law of pensions, a third should be deducted from the King's pension and the rest paid to him, and the care of all the King's heirs and dependants be made his responsibility. He was preparing to go to England to pursue this when someone of his own family invited him to a feast. When he came back from the feast, vomiting and purging began. His condition worsened in an instant, and in a single day his life, his wife's, and the lives of several of his sons all came to an end. And the world was left empty of all the members of this family who had ever seen the face of a crown and throne.

Even so, the bustle of Matiya Burj and the splendour and population of that new settlement had taken such a form that, if it had escaped the evil eye of misfortune, it would have reminded people for a long time of the style and manners of this ill-starred King's court and its dependants, and of their taste. But the justice of the British government, in dividing Wajid Ali Shah's estate and doing right by his heirs, displayed its majesty in this way: all the property and the whole household were to be sold and divided among them all in proportion, and everything there was turned into cash. The inevitable result was that Matiya Burj was razed brick from brick. Goods worth hundreds of thousands were sold for cowries, and the very spot that had become a Garden of Iram in a few days was reduced to a hell at the lowest depth of misfortune. Go there now and kick up the dust; you will see nothing. If your eyes are looking for the old splendour and bustle, call some Imru-ul-Qais [1], who would shed tears as he went and tell them: here was the Murassa Manzil, here the Nur Manzil, here the Sultan Khana, and here the Asad Manzil. There *mushaira*s (poetry gatherings) were held. There was the assembly of accomplished scholars. There were the witticisms of pure-hearted friends, and there the spells of eloquent, magic-tongued speakers. In this place was a cluster of the world's most wondrous beauties. In this place the party of dance and song was in full swing. In this place houri-like, moon-faced girls were taught to sing and dance, and in this place His Majesty would sit among his coquettish *mut'a* wives and celebrate. In this spot stories were told in the gathering of the opium-eaters. In this spot quail fights were held. In this spot pigeons flew, and in this spot kite-flying matches were wagered. At this gateway moon-faced beauties with bewitching eyes could be seen peeping out with their heads through the curtain. At this gateway the coming and going of the maids kept up a strange excitement at all hours. At this gateway particular poets were always in attendance, because the lady of these apartments took an interest in the art of poetry. And at this gateway there was always a search for young-hearted writers of ornate prose, because every second or third day a *to-do-nama* [2] of a new kind went from here to be laid before the King.

But even though Matiya Burj has been wiped out, thousands of memorials of this departed court remain. The city of Lucknow itself and its society recall this court, and every inch of the land of Awadh is a memorial of its greatness, for the marks of the past kingdom are set on it everywhere. Every movement and gesture of the people of Lucknow is a living history of the former pillars of the court, and seeing their bearing and manners one cannot help saying: *Ai gul, ba tu khursandam, tu bu-e-kase dari* ("O rose, I am content with you; you carry someone's fragrance"). So, to refresh the memory of these enduring traces of the kingdom, I now wish to show what the society was that arose in Lucknow from the founding of this court, what it was like, and in what ways it had influenced the social life of India.

In India at that time Persian was the court language, and the best social life of the people of India was derived from Iranian culture. Under the Safavid dynasty the general religion of the Iranians had become Twelver Shi'ism, while the ruling dynasty of India, the Chaghatai Mughals, followed the Sunni creed. But such was the effect of the stamp of Persian culture on social life that, despite the difference of religion, Persians who came here were received with open arms out of respect. It was this moral inclination that made Nur Jahan Begum mistress of Jahangir's crown and throne. Thanks to it most of the honoured officials of Delhi in the last period were Shia, and because of it Amin-ud-Din Khan of Nishapur, as soon as he arrived here, became Nawab Burhan-ul-Mulk and master of the whole wide region of the Ganges valley. The more Burhan-ul-Mulk's influence and authority grew and advanced, the more he became the resort and refuge of the accomplished men of Delhi. Even so, since his life and Nawab Safdar Jang's were spent in laying the foundations of a new state, they had very little time, apart from valuing brave soldiers, to turn their attention to national culture and social matters; for such things belong more to luxurious times of peace and order than to military campaigns and conquest.

But when Shuja-ud-Daula, having lost heart after the battle of Buxar, made a new treaty with the English and was forced to sit quietly at Faizabad, the foundation of a new culture was laid in the land of Awadh. At the beginning of this essay I described in what numbers the accomplished men of Delhi had begun to leave their homes and come here in Shuja-ud-Daula's time: what a continuous stream of people of every profession and class flowed from Delhi to Faizabad, and how, in only nine years, Faizabad had become something else entirely. When after Shuja-ud-Daula Nawab Asaf-ud-Daula settled in Lucknow, the well-established arena of Faizabad was uprooted all at once and moved to Lucknow, and the flood of Delhi's leading families and masters of every art that was heading for Faizabad was stopped in Lucknow, which lay right on the way. And at last the few gentlemen and men of skill who had been left entangled in the begums' establishments at Faizabad gradually came to Lucknow too, for Asaf-ud-Daula had set flowing here such a Ganges of wealth that no one could hear of it without running helplessly to drink his fill.

There were many Hindu states in those days, but only the courts of Muslim rulers were thought cultivated and refined, and the Hindu rajas themselves admitted that in culture and social life they could not compete with the Muslim courts; for English education had not yet given them the idea of reviving their ancient civilisation and creating a new culture and a new literature for themselves. The result was that if an accomplished scholar, poet or soldier, disgusted with the Muslim nobles, reached the territory of Hindu nobles, he was received with open arms and honoured and esteemed like a god.

Muslim courts in those days were few, and could be counted. First of all there was the exalted court of Delhi, and because of its antiquity and past glory Delhi had become a mine of accomplished men of every kind and of gentlefolk of established family; and it was the scattered stones of this land that had gone to distant provinces and set up new courts. Of these, in the Deccan was the court of Asaf Jah. Beyond it were the courts of Tipu Sultan and the Nawab of Arcot. Going north from Delhi, one came first to the territory of the brave men of Rohilkhand, then to this court of Awadh, and beyond it to the court of the Nawab Nazim of Bengal at Murshidabad. Of these Islamic courts, those of the Deccan were very far away. First, the road to them was very hard going because of jungles and mountains; and even if someone dared to set out, the Thugs and dacoits who were spread over the whole country would end his life on the road. To say nothing of reaching the territory of Tipu Sultan and the Nawab of the Carnatic, it was hard for anyone to reach even the dominions of the Nizam of Hyderabad. So when Delhi began to decline, and the market for patronage there went slack as the condition of the Mughal emperors worsened, people generally turned towards northern India. There is no doubt that Rohilkhand was very near. If the Khans there had been patrons, no one had a better opportunity than they. But although they had piety, courage and many virtues, they were entirely without learned taste and social refinement. If you judge their condition correctly, you find they were people of purely military taste, with no interest in anything but gathering their countrymen and increasing the number of their clans to build up their fighting strength. Judged by the savour of social life and the manners and morals of civilised living, their condition was just like that of wild rustics. What could such people value in poets, men of letters and other masters of the arts? So whoever entered their land pressed on and went beyond it. Covering four or five stages, he reached Lucknow, and saw that everyone from the ruler to the lowest class was spreading his eyes like a carpet to welcome him and was ready to serve him in every way. Having reached such a place, who could come back? Whoever went there became of that place, and every ruined exile from Delhi, as soon as he came here, broke his legs and sat down. Neither did he remember his homeland, nor was any desire left in his heart to see any other court. Some people went on from here to the Nawab Nazim of Bengal too, but they were those whom Lucknow could not value. Such people were few, though; otherwise all the masters who came from Delhi were absorbed into Lucknow. In a short time things reached such a point that all the famous and celebrated elders of the most cultivated society of that age were gathered in Lucknow.

Only one thing existed in Lucknow before this court was founded, and that was Arabic learning, the foundation of which had been laid when the Emperor Aurangzeb granted the houses of Farangi Mahal to Mulla Nizam-ud-Din of Sihali. The residence of the Mulla and his family made Farangi Mahal within a few days so great a university of India that this little quarter of Lucknow became the centre of the scholars and learned men of all India. After Shaikh Abdul-Haqq of Delhi, no scholar of note had arisen even in Delhi. Towards the end the family of Shah Waliullah did indeed rise very high, but their fame was confined to the science of *hadith* (the traditions of the Prophet). For all the sciences other than *hadith*, Lucknow was the university. Lucknow was an obscure city in those days, and that such an obscure place should become so great a university that, never mind India, Bukhara, Khwarazm, Herat and Kabul bowed their heads before it is truly astonishing. The whole Islamic world was proud to be its pupil and followed the syllabus chosen here, the *Silsila-e-Nizamiya*. In short, thanks to the scholars of Farangi Mahal, Lucknow had already become the mine and resort of philosophy and wisdom, logic and dialectical theology (*kalam*), jurisprudence (*fiqh*) and its principles, and various other sciences before this new court was founded. So in this one thing Lucknow owes no debt to the new court; all the other advances came only with the founding of this kingdom.

Now I wish to describe one by one what things came from Delhi to Lucknow, and what colour they took on when they came here. First of all is the Urdu language, which was the language of those gentlemen and army chiefs of Delhi who came to Lucknow with Nawab Burhan-ul-Mulk Bahadur. This language was born in Delhi, and its poetry began in the Deccan. Wali of Gujarat came to Delhi and presented his collected poems (*diwan*), and with his charming song woke the native speakers from the sleep of neglect. There was such magic in that song that as soon as they heard it, it was on everyone's lips, and Urdu poetry began in Delhi.

At first there were only a few elders who began to give poetic judgement in Delhi with the authority of masters; but that time should be called, if not the infancy of the Urdu language, at least the childhood of Urdu poetry. The most learned and accomplished of these earliest pioneers of the world of Urdu was Khan-e-Arzu, whom the late Maulana Azad has placed in the second period of poetry. The great masters of the following age, among them Sauda, Mir, Mirza Mazhar Jan-e-Janan and Khwaja Mir Dard, were all his pupils. It was with this first master, Khan-e-Arzu, that the coming of poetry and mastery of the language to Lucknow began. Nawab Shuja-ud-Daula's maternal uncle, Salar Jang, invited him to Lucknow out of the highest regard, and having lived in Awadh for some time, he died in Lucknow itself in 1169 AH (1184 of the Muhammadan era, that is, 1756 CE), two years after Shuja-ud-Daula's accession. He was the first master of Urdu poetry, and with him began the coming of Urdu verse to Lucknow. But alas, his bones were snatched from the loving lap of the soil of Lucknow and handed over to the dust of Delhi.

After this another famous master of verse of the same period, Ashraf Ali Khan Fughan, who was the foster-brother of the Emperor Ahmad Shah, set out for Lucknow in search of patronage. Shuja-ud-Daula honoured and esteemed him greatly, received him with open arms and kept him at his court for a long time. But poets are more delicate in temper than in fancy. Taking offence at some slight matter, he went off to Azimabad, and was buried there two years before Shuja-ud-Daula's death.

Now began the third period of poetry as fixed by Maulana Azad, when Khan-e-Arzu's pupils ruled over Urdu verse. A look at the conditions of that time shows that Delhi could not keep its masters in its embrace. Accomplished men of every kind keep leaving its precincts, and whoever goes does not come back. Lucknow, by contrast, is such that whatever master of his art comes, wherever he is from, becomes a man of this place. Mirza Rafi Sauda, Mir Taqi Mir and Sayyid Muhammad Mir Soz, who are the prophets of verse of this third period, all left Delhi and came to Lucknow, and were buried here.

Besides them, the masters of verse who came to Lucknow at this time and made it their home were Mirza Jafar Ali Hasrat, Mir Haidar Ali Hairan, Khwaja Hasan Hasan, Mirza Fakhir Makin, Mir Zahik, Baqaullah Khan Baqa, Mir Hasan of Delhi, son of Mir Zahik (the author of the *masnavi*), and scores of poets like them. Mir Qamar-ud-Din Minnat, Mir Ziya-ud-Din Ziya and Ashraf Ali Khan Fughan came from Delhi to Lucknow, stayed a long time and shone here, but in the end, drawn by patrons elsewhere, went to Calcutta and Azimabad and fell prey to death there. Although Shaikh Muhammad Qaim Qaim died in his home town of Tanda, he too was for a long time an Akbar of this court of Lucknow. Only a few elders like Mirza Mazhar Jan-e-Janan and Khwaja Mir Dard were left in Delhi, who, through their dervish contentment and their following, had been able to keep their footing in Delhi, and who as heads of Sufi orders (*sajjada-nashin*) could not leave their seat of spiritual authority. In short, this third period of poetry is the time when the court of Delhi was being uprooted from there and planted in Lucknow, and when Lucknow had a fervour of patronage the like of which the history of India does not know.

Now the fourth period began. Its pillars too were born of the dust of Delhi, Akbarabad and so on, but the poetry of all of them shone in Lucknow. It was from here that their names became famous. They presided over the *mushaira*s here. These men in general came up from here, lived here, rose to eminence here, and died and were buried here. The chief pillars of this period were Jurat, Sayyid Insha, Mushafi, Qatil, Rangin and others. These men ruled the language in their time, and the fame of their poetry rang so loud that no Urdu poet's name could shine beside them. Where are the bones of them all? In the dust of Lucknow.

How many men of taste were coming from Delhi to Lucknow at that time can be judged from a story of Sayyid Insha's, in which he reports a conversation between a gentlemanly old man of good family of that age and a woman of pleasure called Nura. The old gentleman and the woman are both from Delhi, but both are talking in Lucknow. Bi Nura says: "Oh, come in, Mir Sahib! You've become as rare as the Eid moon. In Delhi you used to come and sit till late into the night. What has happened to you in Lucknow, that you never even show your face? How I looked for you in your *karbala*, but found no trace of you anywhere. Don't fail to come to the *Athon* (the Eighth Day) fair. I swear you by Ali, do come to the *Athon*." Mir Sahib's reply, though most entertaining, I shall leave out to avoid length. He criticises the present manners of Delhi and Lucknow and finds fault with contemporary poets, which does not concern us. All I need to show is that at that time, not to speak of gentlemen and masters of the arts, even courtesans were coming to settle in Lucknow, and people who had been devotees of the Phulwalon ki Sair (the Flower-Sellers' Fair) in Delhi now amused themselves at the *karbala* and the *Athon* fair.

The late Shams-ul-Ulama Maulana Azad has gathered all the later poets of Delhi and Lucknow together without regard to distinction or period and, stretching the ropes of time, made them into a fifth period; but this is unjust. The true fifth period was that of Nasikh and Atish alone, in which the language took on a new form. Many old idioms were dropped, new turns of phrase arose, and the foundation was laid of the language that became equally accepted among the later poets of Delhi and Lucknow, and became almost the language that is now standard in India. And this was the time when the coin of Lucknow first became current in the realm of poetry.

After this the sixth period was the time when, in Lucknow, the poetry of Wazir, Saba, Rind, Goya, Ashk, Nasim of Delhi, Asir, and the *masnavi* writers Nawab Mirza Shauq and Pandit Daya Shankar Nasim rang loud, and in Delhi Momin, Zauq and Ghalib were singing their poet's song. The truth is that this period raised the language to the highest level of advancement in thought.

After this the seventh period was that of Amir, Dagh, Munir, Taslim, Majruh, Jalal, Latafat, Afzal, Halim and others.

A close look at these last periods shows clearly how strong a hold eloquence of language and poetry had taken in Lucknow. In a few days writing verse became a mark of good breeding in Lucknow, and poets grew so numerous here as perhaps never anywhere in any language. Poetry was cultivated even among women, and even in the speech of the unlettered a gleam of poetic fancy, simile and metaphor began to appear.

[2] Sharar's note: *To-do-nama* was the name for the letters that the begums and ladies of the palace sent to His Majesty, which were usually amorous in tone.
