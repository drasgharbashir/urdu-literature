# (41)

(5) The fifth thing in social life is deportment (*nishast-o-barkhast*, sitting and rising). Every civilised people has various laws and established principles of deportment, and by them the degree of that people's advance and culture is established. If you go to the civilised Christian cities of Paris, London and Berlin, or to the cultivated Muslim cities of Constantinople, Tehran and Shiraz, and join the company of the cultivated people there, you will see how strict are the moral laws of deportment among them. But if you go to the great trading cities of India and meet the nobles and dignitaries there, you will find no trace at all of the moral laws of culture. But in those cities where some particular court is established or has been, for example Hyderabad in the Deccan, Bhopal, Rampur and so on, you will find, by the blessing of honoured native courts having been established, the rules of observing rank among high and low alike; unlike the trading cities, where there will not be a trace or sign of good manners, courtesy and the observance of rank.

In Delhi in earlier days these moral principles must certainly have been more advanced than anywhere else, because its court was the greatest and had been established for centuries. But there, owing to the trading castes gaining the upper hand over society, all the former culture was laid in the dust. The foundation of deportment is laid by nobility, rank and government. Government and rank teach how the small should meet the great and the great the small, and how one should behave towards equals. But trade is hostile to these airs of nobility and moral refinements. It is nurtured in the lap of business dealings and self-interest, and declares "self-sacrifice" [TN: written in English transliteration], that is, sacrificing one's time and money, one's skill and one's wealth on anyone without reason, to be foolishness and absurdity. In contrast, the essence of rank is that consideration should be shown without self-interest to one's adherents or to able people. And the inevitable result of this is that where trade flourishes and the social life of merchants prevails over the social life of well-to-do nobles and gentry, no moral law can survive. Thus this thing wiped out all the pomp and splendour of the former magnificent courts of Delhi, and nothing remained that was worthy of the history of its fame.

When the crowd of merchants began to destroy the culture of Delhi, it fled from its ancient home and took refuge in the small court of Lucknow, which, although small, once one had entered its precincts no one could see that there was any other court in the world greater than this. Then, when the gentry of Delhi, sitting here in freedom, began to practise their laws of deportment, in a few days things came to such a pass that Lucknow alone was the centre of culture, polish and the manners of deportment in all India, and the cultivated people of all cities were imitating and following the people of Lucknow. Establishing these gradations, whose reception one should go out to the door for, for whom one need only stand up, for whom one should half rise, and for whom it is enough to say "Come, pray be seated" while remaining seated in one's place, depends mostly on one's own inward judgement and discretion (*ijtihad*), and no one has such a faculty for this discretion as the cultivated gentry of Lucknow.

Here, if an equal comes, they will stand up to show him respect, vacate the best place for him, and not sit down themselves until he has sat down. They will sit before him with courtesy and good manners, and keep a cheerful face so that he should not feel any kind of displeasure. When he gives anything they will take it with a respectful salutation (*taslim*). They will take full care that no movement of theirs should be disagreeable to him. And if in his company they turn their attention to some other necessary task, they will do so after making excuses and begging his pardon. If there is need to get up and go somewhere, they will go after taking his leave. If it happens that they go with him, they will keep behind him on the way and let him go ahead. In keeping with the principles of culture he too will insist: "Pray go first." But from this side it will be said again and again: "Sir, pray go ahead; what am I worth?" And if he will by no means agree and absolutely compels them, then, performing an *adab* (salutation) in gratitude, they will step forward, but even then in such a manner that their back is not towards him.

Many people make fun of these manners, and it has become a proverb that two people of Lucknow kept saying "After you", "After you", and the train left, so both were left stranded at the station. It cannot be denied that anything carried beyond moderation becomes bad and harmful. But does this not prove that the observance of the manners of social life has reached such a degree in the morals of the people of Lucknow that in practising it they do not even think of coming to harm? Those who are devoid of culture and polish may make what objections they like, but a cultivated and polished man will acknowledge these things as a moral excellence rather than a fault.

Now, as in all cities, tables, chairs and English furniture have come into use here too, but formerly the seating was on the floor-covering, which was costly and elaborate according to one's standing and wealth. If some stranger of equal rank, or an elder and a person deserving respect, came, he was seated in front of the *ga'o* (bolster), and everyone sat respectfully on their knees in a small or large circle according to the number of those present. Whomever he spoke to answered with joined hands in the greatest humility, and considered it a moral offence to talk much before him or to raise his voice above his.

But if all were equals, companions and friends of like taste, there was informality in the sitting, and despite being of equal rank and age, everyone showed respect to each other even in informality. Care was taken that no one's back should be towards anyone, and that nothing should happen to show anyone slighted or indifference to honouring him. Servants and attendants could not sit nearby or on the floor-covering on which the friends of the company sat. To carry out orders they stood respectfully in front, or waited out of sight in some nearby place within call. And for them to be standing all the time or talking much was considered bad manners.

When they brought and set down the *khasdan* (*pan* box) or the *huqqa*, the master of the house offered it with his own hand to his friends, and they rose and took it with a *taslim*. In informal gatherings the coming in of juniors without need was improper. If they ever came in from need, they performed an *adab* to their father's friends, bowing with the greatest respect, and as soon as they came the elders' company changed from informal to polite. And just as the junior respected the seniority of all, so the elders, out of regard for his youth, gave up their informalities.

In the society here there was no custom of handshaking (*musafaha*) or embracing (*mu'anaqa*) with those one met daily. Handshaking was limited to kissing the hands of the leaders of the community, and embracing was only for those friends who came back from some journey or met after a long time.

When men went into the women's quarters they showed respect to the women. Before them it was impossible for them to behave too informally or to sit long among them. Informality between husband and wife was necessary, but before the elder women of the house even they were never informal. Among the gentry of the villages it was the custom that until a new bride became the mother of four or five children she kept *purdah* from her husband before all the women of the house, and no relative, man or woman, dared see her going to her husband or her husband going to her. This strictness did not exist among the gentry of the city. In the families of the city husband and wife ate at one dining cloth from the very beginning, but it was improper for them to be informal with each other even before the *mama*s and personal maids.

The company of women among themselves, except in the households of the great nobles, remained comparatively informal. Among them a moderate degree of ceremony was kept with ladies who came as guests, but along with this ceremony there was a greater display of sincerity and fellow feeling.
