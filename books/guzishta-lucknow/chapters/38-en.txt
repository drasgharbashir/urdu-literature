In matters of dress in Lucknow, the cut and the kinds of cloth kept advancing day by day. Because it is a hot country, the people of the lower classes in India keep their whole body bare except for covering their private parts. This is not only because of their poverty and the country's lack of means, but because of the demands of the season and climate. Its effect in Delhi too was that light and delicate cloths were adopted instead of thick and heavy ones. Here there was even greater advance. And since there was now very little need left for soldiering and warfare, while love of pleasure and the company of women kept increasing greatly, the style of women began to influence the men, beyond all moderation, and men began to adopt in their style and dress the kind of adornment and ornament that is suitable for women.

Especially from the time when the rulers here gave up the word Nawab for themselves and adopted the word King, the people of the Nishapuri and Salar-Jangi families, who received considerable *wasiqa*s (stipends) and pensions, were entirely confined to their homes, so that they had no company but that of women. The inevitable result was that not only did effeminacy appear in their style and dress, but their speech too became like that of women. And since they were regarded as the gentlemen and men of style of the city, most of the common people also began to follow them. And unlike the gentlemen of other places, here in Lucknow this became the general style: a parting on the head, over it a cap worked with trimmings, hair down to the ears, in combing which flat bands (*patti*) were set on both sides of the forehead, *pan* in the mouth, *lakha* (lip colour) on the lips, on the body a close-fitting *angarkha* with three *kurti*s each, under it a drawn silk *ghutna* of *gulbadan*, *mehndi* (henna) on the hands, and *tat-bafi*, that is, gold-worked boots on the feet. In winter, instead of the *angarkha*, a cotton-padded *dagla* (quilted coat) of blue, yellow, or green and red satin or *grant*.

In winter some honoured people here generally wore *qaba*s of shawl cloth, but everyone liked the *doshala* and the shawl *rumal*. It was the result of this that such shawls as still turn up here and there among the people of Lucknow, let alone India, could perhaps not now be had even in Kashmir itself.

The fondness for shawls grew so much that many shawl-weavers and thousands of Kashmiri darners (*rafugar*) and shawl-washers left their homeland and settled in Lucknow, of whom in the past fifty years not a name or trace now remains. If any of them did survive, he took up some other trade.

Since Muharram was a very important thing in Lucknow and a time of mourning (*azadari*), special dress and special jewellery were invented here for Muharram, keeping in view both mourning and refinement and delicacy. Black and blue were considered colours of grief and mourning. And green, because in the time of the Banu Abbas, as against their black colour, the colour of the Banu Fatima was green. Thus even today some Fatimids of Iran and India give proof of this ancient style of the *sayyid*s by their green turbans. In any case, red was forbidden in Muharram. Green, blue and black, and with them yellow too, were considered suitable for this season. Thus here in Muharram the whole dress of women was chosen from these said colours, matched suitably. All jewellery was taken off, even the bangles were removed, in place of which black and green silk *pahunchi*s (wrist ornaments) for the wrists and *karan-phul*s (ear studs) of black and yellow silk for the ears were invented, which with greater refinement even than gold and silver jewellery enhance their beauty and grace.

Muharram was indeed a very important month. Here, for every season and every time, such inventions were made daily in women's dress as all India regarded with astonishment. And the truth is that anyone who saw the cut of women's dress in Lucknow fifty years ago and its fresh novelties day by day would forget the changing fashions of France and London. And on this ground it became current on many tongues that Lucknow is the Paris of the East. Today many lovers of simplicity, and those who are deprived of an advanced social life, object to these refinements, and do not see that at the courts and cities where civilisation advances such things arise in every branch of social life and company, which, however absurd and superfluous they may be in a philosopher's eyes, the company of men of style and the gatherings of polished people regard as most important and necessary.

If the effect of women's style prevailing over men had been limited to the delicacy of cloths and bright and flashy colours, that would have been something. But here many people came to such a state that in the *dagla*s, *dupatta*s, *dula'i*s (light quilts), [illegible] and trousers of husband and wife there remained no difference of any kind, except that braid and lace (*gota-patha*) and jewellery were reserved for women. Men wore delicate silk cloths of gaudy colours without braid and lace. But this taste began to decline after the Mutiny under English influence, and now it does not survive in anyone except a handful of people.

Like the male attendants and their various classes, particular styles were also fixed here for the different classes of women. The *khansama*s, coachmen and grooms (*sa'is*) of the English go about in various liveries, but those liveries could not become their real dress, so that they would wear them in their own homes too. In contrast, in Lucknow the particular dress fixed for the female and male servants and all the employees within and without became their real style. For example, just as the sentries at the gateways (*deorhi*), the mace-bearers, the runners and so on had particular and distinct styles, so in the women's palaces the styles of the *mahaldar*s (palace superintendents), *Mughlani*s (seamstresses and tirewomen) and *kahari*s (women bearers) were so distinct that on seeing them from afar one would understand that this woman is a *mahaldar*, this a *khawass* (lady's maid), this a *Mughlani* and this a *kahari*. And the charm of it was that no air of livery was allowed to enter their dress.

The attendants, however, and the personal servants like them, had the same dress as that of the master and mistress themselves, the reason being that both these groups wear their master's or mistress's *utran*, that is, their cast-off clothes.

After dress, the most important thing for women is jewellery, and women mostly regard their jewellery as their own special wealth and property, the inevitable result of which is that in most provinces of India clumsy and heavy jewellery is most in use, so that it should be of greater value. The fondness for heavy jewellery is growing day by day in the villages of Awadh and generally in all the cities of India. But when the honoured ladies of the respectable families of Delhi came to Lucknow, they were at first wearing the same jewellery that was current throughout India and in Delhi itself; but a few days after coming here, when the special modified social life of this place was established, only the idea of adornment and ornament remained in jewellery, and every kind of jewellery became day by day lighter, slighter, more delicate and more handsome. Until in the last period the style of the wives of nobles and wealthy households became this, that they wore plain clothes without trimmings and braid, and contented themselves with only one or two items of jewellery, which were very delicate, light and costly. And if they did wear several things on the neck and nose and ears, they were very light. The result of this was that such light and slight jewellery began to be made in Lucknow as could be made nowhere else.

The *nath* (nose ring) in the nose had since the time of the Hindus been considered a most necessary ornament and the sign of wifehood (*suhag*), a notion that through mutual mingling also arose among the Muslims. Thus village women even today exaggerate its heaviness so much that they wear *nath*s of four or five *tola*s each, from which the nostrils are often torn; but the nose is pierced again so that it should not remain without a *nath*. The ladies of Lucknow did away with the *nath* altogether, and in its place began to wear jewel-set gold nose studs (*kil*), which proved a very refined and beautiful ornament. And from love of delicacy they made even these studs so small and light that craftsmen anywhere but the goldsmiths and *sadakar*s (setters of plain gold) of Lucknow cannot make such light nose studs.

Now for the last twenty-five or thirty years the use of the *bulaq* (pendant from the nasal septum) has increased very much. Although this is no admirable taste, the trend towards less jewellery and its general popularity have advanced it so much that there are now very few women who do not wear the *bulaq*.

At present, through the mutual mingling of different cities, the art of making jewellery is advancing everywhere, and particular cities have become famous for particular ornaments. But before the Mutiny, when the railway had not produced this mutual familiarity and uniformity in the cities of India, better goldsmiths and craftsmen than those of Lucknow could be found nowhere. But now many cities are getting ahead of Lucknow in this art. In particular the city of Delhi has outstripped all the cities of India in making light jewellery of alloyed silver. But even so the refined households of most places prefer jewellery made in Lucknow and the silver vessels of this place. We shall have to touch on this subject again and again among the crafts of Lucknow, so here we content ourselves with this.
