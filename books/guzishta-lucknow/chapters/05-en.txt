# (4)

After the death of Safdar Jang Mansur Ali Khan, his son Nawab Shuja-ud-Daula took his seat as ruler in 1182 of the Muhammadan era (1753 CE); something of his history has been told in the first part of this essay. He was an ambitious ruler of restless, unquiet temperament, but unluckily his reign was full of great upheavals and revolutions that the age would remember. The fate of two great historical peoples and powers of the world was decided before his eyes. First came the apocalyptic battle of Panipat, in which Ahmad Shah Durrani, Shuja-ud-Daula and Najib-ud-Daula, with all the strong armies of the Khans of Rohilkhand, stood on one side, and the locust swarm of the Marathas on the other. In a single day in 1190 of the Muhammadan era (1761 CE) this battle decided that, whether or not India remained the Muslims', it could not be the Marathas'. After this the cataclysmic field of Buxar was joined, with the regular army of the English on one side and Shuja-ud-Daula's huge host on the other. Four years after Panipat, in 1193 of the Muhammadan era (1764 CE), this battle settled within twenty-four hours that India now belonged not to the Muslims but to the English.

Before these battles, although Shuja-ud-Daula lived in Lucknow, his great political preoccupations and his military reforms left him no time to attend to the progress and adornment of the city. He built forts, set up strongholds, gathered military stores and weapons of war; he had no leisure to put his house in order and adorn his city. After the battle of Buxar, as I have said, he went and settled in Faizabad, so Lucknow was deprived of his blessings. In 1204 of the Muhammadan era (1775 CE) he died, and Nawab Asaf-ud-Daula succeeded him.

As soon as he took his seat as ruler, Asaf-ud-Daula fell out with his mother and set off for Lucknow, and it is from this time that the ruling power of the Awadh court began to wane and the outward splendour of Lucknow to grow. After winning the field of Buxar the English had gained many rights of interference in the Awadh court, on the strength of which military growth here was checked, and a close watch was always kept to see that the government of Awadh never regained such power that its armies could again be drawn up against the English forces. Still, as long as Shuja-ud-Daula lived at Faizabad he was busy with military reform, and night and day his one passion was to increase his strength by whatever means he could. So Munshi Faiz Bakhsh, in his *Tarikh-e-Farah Bakhsh*, gives an eyewitness account of that time: "In speed of loading and firing, the English army's muskets were nothing compared with those of Shuja-ud-Daula's army."

But as soon as Asaf-ud-Daula's reign began, all this was gone. The English very shrewdly began to extend their rights of interference, and very wisely persuaded Asaf-ud-Daula to stop worrying about military reform and amuse himself with other pursuits. Asaf-ud-Daula himself had no great taste for the army. He needed money to scatter and enjoy himself, which could not be had without disbanding troops; so he kept a small force, dismissed all the rest, and gave himself up to pleasure and luxury. He was the obedient friend of his Western friends, following their every hint and listening to no one against their advice.

As a reward for this sincere devotion the English got Rohilkhand into his hands. When he asked the English for help in harassing and plundering his mother, Bahu Begum Sahiba, he was given moral support most generously and they took his side. Even so, in his time neither he nor the people of Lucknow could feel much that any outside power was interfering in their administration. The chief reason was that Asaf-ud-Daula's general generosity and love of pleasure had made all his subjects pleasure-loving and luxury-seeking too, and in the face of present comfort and ease no one felt the need to think about the end.

The result of this love of pleasure was that, to outward appearance, the court of Lucknow acquired in those days a splendour and magnificence found in no other court anywhere, and such a store of luxuries was gathered as could be seen nowhere else. The city of Lucknow was then at such a height of splendour that no city, not in India alone but perhaps in the world, could have matched Lucknow at its zenith. The money that Shuja-ud-Daula spent on the army and on preparations for war, Asaf-ud-Daula began to spend on his taste for pleasure and on the adornment and prosperity of the city, and in a few days he had gathered the pomp of the whole world about him. His one ambition was that no court, whether the Nizam's of Hyderabad or Tipu Sultan's, should outdo his own in display, glory and grandeur.

At the wedding of his son Wazir Ali Khan he showed such magnificence that the pomp and splendour of the wedding procession surpassed every extravagance in the history of the earth. There were twelve hundred elephants in the procession. The royal robe worn by the bridegroom was set with jewels worth two million rupees. Two vast, sumptuous tents were made for the festivities, each sixty feet wide, twelve hundred feet long and sixty feet high, and such fine, exquisite and costly cloth was used that the two of them cost the state a million rupees.

On the riverbank, west of Machchhi Bhawan, he built the Daulat Khana (his palace), the Rumi Darwaza (Turkish Gate) and his unique *imambara* (hall for the Muharram mourning assemblies). In 1213 of the Muhammadan era (1784 CE) there was famine in Awadh, and even the gentlefolk of the city were starving. At this critical time the building of the *imambara* was begun in order to relieve the people. Since men of good family thought it a disgrace to work as labourers by day, the building went on by night as well as by day, and the poor, starving gentlefolk of the city would come in the dark of night, join the labourers and work by the light of torches. As sincerely and devoutly as the Nawab had commissioned this building, just as purely and truly did the people build it. The result was that a building was completed so fine and splendid that it is unequalled and unique of its kind. Great and famous engineers and architects were called to draw its plan, and each tried to make his plan surpass the others', but the plan of a matchless architect named Kifayatullah was chosen, and the building was begun according to it. It is 167 feet long and 52 feet wide. It is built of brick and lime of the very finest quality, and there is not a piece of wood in it from floor to roof. The building has nothing to do with the stone buildings of the Mughal emperors. Marble could not be had in such quantity in Lucknow; yet look at the *imambara* and Asaf-ud-Daula's other buildings, and they have a new beauty and a singular grandeur and dignity. The vaulted roof of the *imambara*, made without beams, is so large that there is no vaulted roof as large anywhere in the world, and for this reason too it is counted among the wonders of the world.

Asaf-ud-Daula's buildings show not the least influence of European buildings. They are purely Asian in kind, and their grandeur and magnificence are real and genuine, not for show. After Nawab Asaf-ud-Daula these buildings lay neglected. After the Mutiny the English took them over, pulled down the houses round about, cleared the ground on three sides (all except the river side), and made the *imambara* a fort and the Rumi Darwaza its gate. At that time British soldiers lived in the *imambara*. Its great hall was an arsenal, and huge cannon were trundled about its floor, yet neither was the ground ever dug up nor did a single flake of plaster come off the walls. Now the British government has given up the *imambara* and returned it to the Muslims. A *mujtahid* (senior Shia jurist) leads the prayers in its mosque, and the *ta'ziya* rites (of Muharram mourning) are held in the *imambara*.

The strength of Nawab Asaf-ud-Daula's buildings can be judged from the fact that although more than a hundred and twenty-five years have passed since they were built, they still stand in their places with the same grandeur and dignity and the same strength and endurance. Not a brick has moved from its place, nor has the lime let go of the bricks anywhere. The buildings that the later kings of Awadh had built at a cost of tens of millions of rupees, on the other hand, besides having lost the national and native style, are very weak, and if they had not been repaired from time to time they would have fallen down long ago.

Asaf-ud-Daula lived in his palace, the "Daulat Khana", next to the *imambara* and Machchhi Bhawan. To take his pleasure outside the city and across the river, far from the throng of people and apart from worldly quarrels, he built the palace of Bibiapur. He often stayed in this house when he went out hunting and on pleasure trips. In the same way he built an airy, delightful house at Chinhat, and mansions in the Char Bagh and the Aish Bagh. At this time too the stables were set up in and next to Tikaitganj, and then the quarter of Wazirganj was founded, which, being the residence of Asaf-ud-Daula's son Wazir Ali Khan, came to be named after him and is his memorial.

Now that the ruler and sovereign was living permanently in Lucknow, people at large turned towards Lucknow. Those who had settled in Faizabad in Shuja-ud-Daula's time began to leave Faizabad and come and settle in Lucknow. On the other side, people from Delhi bade farewell to their homeland and came straight to Lucknow, and never had the chance to go back. This throng of people began to settle new quarters, for each newcomer settled wherever he found room, and hundreds of new quarters kept springing up.

So Agha Mir's Deorhi, Fatehganj, Rakabganj, Nakhas, Daulatganj, Begumganj, Nawabganj, the Khansaman's Enclosure (founded by one of Nawab Asaf-ud-Daula's household stewards, who invited the Nawab himself to the opening ceremony), Tikaitganj and Tikait Rai's Bazar (named after the chief minister Maharaja Tikait Rai), Tirmaniganj, the cantonment of Tikri or Tiki Husain-ud-Din Khan, Hasanganj, Azli, Bhawaniganj, Balakganj, the Kashmiri Mohalla, Surat Singh's Enclosure, Nawazganj, Tahsinganj, Khudaganj, Nagaria (founded by Nawab Asaf-ud-Daula's mother, Bahu Begum Sahiba, on the same day that he himself laid the foundation of Aliganj across the river), Amberganj, Mahbubganj, Top Darwaza, Khayaliganj, and Jhau Lal's Bridge (the founder of these last two quarters, Raja Jhau Lal, was finance minister of the Awadh state): all these are quarters that were settled and built in Asaf-ud-Daula's time. [TN: several of these names are read from the print and are uncertain.] In those days too Hasan Raza Khan founded Hasanganj across the river.

Nawab Asaf-ud-Daula's generosity was famous among high and low, and his bounty was talked of in distant cities. People spoke his name with respect and love at every turn; all his personal faults were hidden in the skirt of his generosity and vanished from sight, and to ordinary people the Nawab seemed not a pleasure-loving ruler but a selfless saint with the qualities of a dervish. To this day Hindu shopkeepers say with fervent devotion as soon as they open their eyes in the morning: "O Asaf-ud-Daula, saint!"

At this time a very wealthy French merchant called General Claude Martin had come to live in Lucknow. He drew up the plan of a most splendid mansion and showed it to Nawab Asaf-ud-Daula. The Nawab liked it so much that he was ready to pay a million gold *mohur*s for it. The contract of sale had not been completed when Nawab Asaf-ud-Daula died, and the building was not yet finished when Monsieur Martin himself departed this world. Since he left boundless wealth and had no heir, he directed in his will that his body be buried inside this mansion, so that after him the rulers of Awadh could not confiscate it. He had named the building Constantia (Constantinople), but people now know it as "Martin Sahib's Kothi", and it is well worth seeing. After his death he was buried in this mansion. That school is still running today, and many pupils get food and clothing from it. [TN: Sharar mentions "that school" without introducing it; it is La Martinière, founded under Martin's will.] But I hear that Martin Sahib did not restrict this school and its scholarships to any religion or people; he directed that Christians, Hindus and Muslims alike might benefit from it. Now, however, the school is reserved for European children only. Far from any Indian receiving a scholarship, none is even admitted to study there. Perhaps this is because during the Mutiny ignorant, fanatical rioters dug up the grave, took out Mr Martin's bones and scattered them. After the English regained control they happened to find one bone, which was buried again in the same earth. But ordinary Indians cannot be held responsible for what those rioters did.

In 1226 of the Muhammadan era (1797 CE) Nawab Asaf-ud-Daula died, and in his place Nawab Wazir Ali Khan took his seat, the splendour of whose wedding I have described. But within four months he showed such absurd and hateful behaviour that most people were displeased with him. Bahu Begum Sahiba herself preferred her stepson, Yamin-ud-Daula Nawab Saadat Ali Khan, to him. Meanwhile the report spread that Wazir Ali Khan was not Asaf-ud-Daula's son at all, for many believed that Asaf-ud-Daula had been impotent from birth.

Because of Asaf-ud-Daula's hostility, Nawab Saadat Ali Khan had lived for a long time outside his dominions and far away. He lived for years in Calcutta, and for a long time in Benares. Once this view of Wazir Ali Khan had taken hold, the choice fell on Nawab Saadat Ali Khan. He was brought from Benares, and in the Bibiapur house the Governor-General himself held a court and decided on Wazir Ali Khan's deposition and Nawab Saadat Ali Khan's accession. Wazir Ali Khan was at once arrested and sent to Benares, where in a fit of rage he killed Mr Cherry; for this he was arrested and sent to Chunar Garh, and he died there. A long story of his misfortunes and wanderings is well known, for which this short essay has no room.
