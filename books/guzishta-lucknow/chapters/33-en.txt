# (32)

Now we wish to discuss dress at this court and in Lucknow, which is in fact a most interesting subject. The history of dress in India lies in great darkness. Before the coming of the Muslims, so far as can be traced in India, and if one considers ancient images and the pictures on monuments and the like, it is proved that before the Muslims came there was no custom here of sewn cloth. Both women and men covered their bodies with unsewn sheets, *sari*s and *dhoti*s. The Arab travellers who had reached here even before the conquerors of Islam found the people here in this same style in every coastal city from Sind to Bengal and in the nearby inland regions.

Although the first Arab Muslims who arrived here wore *kurta*s, shirts and cloaks (*'aba*), in dress and style they had no great superiority over the people here. Advance in dress began from the time when the Abbasid court of Baghdad, adopting Sasanian manners, devised for the Arab gentry trousers (*paijama*), the *'aba* and *qaba* (robe), and well-shaped turbans, a costume taken wholly or mostly from the style of the nobles and grandees of the Sasanian court. In a few days this same costume became that of all the Muslims spread from Egypt to the banks of the Indus, and in the end they came to India bringing this costume with them. The costume of the Muslim crowned heads of India of the earliest period, as seen in pictures, is almost the same as that of the Persian and Abbasid nobles and rulers. The only difference was that the sultans here, in imitation of the Indian Rajas, wore a great many more jewels.

The last costume of the Mughal court at Delhi that we have been able to learn of was this: a turban (*pagri*) on the head, the *nima* and *jama* on the body, on the legs narrow-cuffed trousers ending above the ankle, on the feet high-heeled shoes in the form of slippers (*kafsh*), and at the waist a sash (*patka*) over the *jama*. This was the style of the old gentry of Delhi, in which no alteration of any kind had taken place up to the time of Muhammad Shah Rangile, and if any did take place, it was not so much that we can see it.

By the *nima* in this costume was meant a *shaluka* (short jacket) with half sleeves to the elbows, with loop buttons (*ghundi*) fastened in front on the chest. This was worn underneath, and over it was worn the *jama*, which had been made by modifying the Persian *qaba*. It had no opening at the neck (*gireban*); rather, the edges on both sides, which were called the *parda*, came slantwise over each other and covered the chest. The upper part of the chest, below the throat, remained open in the same way as it remains open in English coats today, and just as at present the shirt hides the upper part of the chest, so in those days the *nima* covered it. Of the *jama*'s *parda* on the chest, the one that came from the left lay underneath and was tied with ties on the right side, and over it lay the right-hand *parda*, which was tied above on the left side. Then from the waist, in place of skirts, a sort of skirt was joined to it which hung down to above the ankles. Many pleats were given to it, and its flare was very large. The sleeves of the *jama* were unsewn and open to half-way down the forearm, and hung down on both sides. Under it were plain narrow-cuffed trousers, which among the nobles were of *mashru'* (silk-and-cotton cloth) and *gulbadan* (striped silk). Then over the *jama* a *patka* was tied at the waist.

Two or three centuries ago this was the costume of our forefathers and of the nobles and all the gentry of India. The changes that took place in caps, turbans and trousers we shall describe in full detail later. For the present we speak of the garment of the middle part of the body, which, truth to tell, is the real garment, and by which a man's style and cut is distinguished and determined. This was the court dress of that period, and wearing this very dress Nawab Burhan-ul-Mulk, Mansur Jang and Shuja'-ud-Daula came from Delhi to Awadh. The *jama* was generally of fine muslin, which was made exceedingly fine, thin and light in different cities of India and was famous throughout the world. The muslin and *jamdani* (figured muslin) of Dacca were reserved for nobles of high rank and kings.

After this the *balabar* was invented, derived from the Iranian *qaba*, in which the round neck-opening remained entirely open, because the *nima*, which was worn under it too, sufficed to cover the chest. The pleats and flare were taken out of it, and so that the skirts should not open in front, a broad gusset (*kali*) was fixed to the right skirt. This gusset is the first form of the gusset that at present in *sherwani*s is taken down on the left side and tied with a tie or fastened with a hook. The *balabar* too is an invention of Delhi.

Improving on this same *balabar*, the *angarkha* was invented in Delhi itself, in which in fact the *jama* and *balabar* were both combined and a new cut was produced. In it a bodice (*choli*) was sewn on the chest like that of the *qaba*, but in place of keeping the chest open a round and elongated neck-opening was extended, over which, below the throat, a crescent-shaped *kanktha* (collar-piece) was fixed, and it was fastened by a loop button and loop near the neck on the left side. The bodice lay low, and in it first the right-hand *parda* was tied below at the armpit with ties, and then above were ties by which the *parda*s of both sides were brought to the middle below the chest and tied. In it a little of the chest remained open on the left side. The bodice lay low, and below, although the skirts were like those of the *qaba*, yet in memory of the old *jama* pleats were always kept on both sides under the armpits.

This was the old *angarkha*, which had become current in the last period of Delhi and spread from there over all India. After coming to Lucknow more smartness and cut were given to this *angarkha*. The bodice became well rounded, high and tightly drawn. The pleats under the arms went out entirely. On the skirts, instead of a turned hem, a *sanjafi* edging (*got*) stitched on was applied. Then after this the young Nawabs and fashionable men of style, instead of the one *kamartoi* (waist band) that was at the place of the ties below the bodice, put on three *kamartoi*s each in the style of pleats, and made cut-work (*kata'o*) here and there on the edging and near the *kamartoi*s.

In Delhi, after the invention of the *angarkha*, the *nima* had been given up, and the chest remaining open on the left side was not considered improper, but rather thought stylish. In Lucknow, instead of the *nima*, the *shaluka* was invented to go under it, with buttons (*botam*) fastened in front, because by now the buttons of Europe had reached here. Special stylishness was shown in *shaluka*s. Delicate-tempered people wore close-fitting *shaluka*s of net (*jali*) or *barlet*, on which designs were embroidered with raw thread. Some people wore coloured *shaluka*s, because its floral patterns and its colour, showing their gleam through a white *angarkha* of *tanzeb* (fine muslin), produced a special delicacy and a special refinement.

The second modification of the *balabar* after the court came to Lucknow was that a close-fitting *qaba* was invented under the name of *chapkan*. It was given the same round neck-opening, and like the *angarkha* a *parda* was also put on the chest, but that *parda* was fastened on the right side with buttons in an arc-shaped form. In it, on the right side, a handsome arc of buttons came curving from near the throat to the *kauri* (pit of the stomach), and the arc on the opposite side was sewn into the real *qaba*. In it too, as in the *balabar*, a broad gusset was put on above, which was fastened under the armpit on the left side with a button or loop button. This *chapkan*, which was of shawl cloth or some other heavy cloth and was more suited to the winter season, was at one time the honoured dress here of the people of the court, and especially of officials and those admitted to the court. The English too liked it very much, and for a long time made their servants wear it.

Last of all, in the very last period of Lucknow, the *achkan* was invented by combining the *chapkan* and the *angarkha*. In it the neck-opening like that of the *angarkha* and *chapkan* was kept, which was cut straight down the middle and sewn half on each side, and at the place of the seam the roundness and cut of the neck-opening was maintained by means of the *sanjafi* edging. Buttons were fixed in the middle slit, which came straight from the throat to the *kauri*. The gusset of the *balabar*, which used to be fixed above, was put lower in it, so that the skirts should not open either, and so that the bad taste shown by fixing the *balabar*'s gusset on the upper side should be removed. The lower part of the *achkan* was exactly like that of the *chapkan* and the *angarkha*. Fashionable people put on it too the same edging on the skirts and the same three *kamartoi*s each, and made cut-work.

This last invention, the *achkan*, pleased people very much. Its use passed beyond the city and began in the villages too, and spread in no time over all India.

This same *achkan*, reaching Hyderabad, became the *sherwani* after a slight modification. There its sleeves were made like those of the English coat. The neck-opening, which used to be made prominent on the chest by putting edging on it, was taken out. In cut and shape the style of the English coat was adopted in the skirts and so on too, and that garment was invented which today deserves to be called the national dress of all the people of India, Hindu and Muslim. The people of Lucknow too, a few days later, when they saw Hyderabad's suitable improvement on their old invention, liked it very much, and in a short time the *sherwani* came into use in Lucknow as in every city and village.

Instead of the *shaluka* that used to be worn under the *angarkha*, first a loose and high *kurta* was adopted, and a few days later Western influence made the *kurta* give way and brought into use the English shirt, which has cuffs and a collar. The use of the shirt and collar increased the refinements of the *sherwani*; that is, it became obligatory that the white collar should stand out above, and that the upper edge of the *sherwani*, fastened at the throat with a hook, should stay below that top button of the shirt to which the collar is attached. The sleeves were kept such that some part of the cuffs stood out. The dress of educated people and of the middle class in Lucknow at present, as in other cities, is this same *sherwani*, but it has no special connection with Lucknow. Lucknow's invention and innovation came to an end with the *achkan*, which has now almost entirely fallen out of use.
