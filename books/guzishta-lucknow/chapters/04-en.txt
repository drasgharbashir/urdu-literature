# (3)

About the family of Nawab Saadat Khan Burhan-ul-Mulk it is enough to say this. Mir Muhammad Nasir, a Sayyid of Nishapur whose lineage goes back to Imam Musa Kazim, came to India in 1135 of the Muhammadan era (1706 CE), in the reign of Bahadur Shah. His elder son, Mir Muhammad Baqir, came with him and married here, and father and son settled at Azimabad (Patna) under the patronage of the governor (*nazim*) of Bengal. God gave Muhammad Baqir a son by his Indian wife, who later became known by the honoured title of Sher Jang.

Two years after Mir Muhammad Nasir's arrival his younger son, Mir Muhammad Amin, also came to India from Nishapur. When he reached Azimabad he heard that his father had died. The two brothers, Mir Muhammad Baqir and Mir Muhammad Amin, now set out for Delhi, where Mir Muhammad Amin obtained the contract for managing the princes' fiefs. He showed such ability, energy and efficiency in it that his fame spread everywhere. Fortune was on his side. In a short time he was counted among the honoured nobles and *mansabdar*s of the imperial court; then he married the daughter of the governor of Akbarabad (Agra), and came to be reckoned among that highest class of nobles on whom the eye fell when men were chosen for the responsible offices of the state.

In those days the Sayyids of Barha were all-powerful in Delhi, so that not only the subjects but His Majesty the Emperor himself feared them. Muhammad Amin had them killed and broke the Sayyids' power for ever, and showed such courage in the fighting that the imperial court granted him the rank of seven thousand (*haft-hazari*), the command of seven thousand horse, and the title "Burhan-ul-Mulk Bahadur Jang"; and at the same time he was made governor of Akbarabad. After this he was given the superintendence of the royal attendants (*khawas*), a highly honoured post. A little later he was appointed governor of the province of Awadh and, at the same time, superintendent of the imperial artillery. He was shrewd and extremely alert, and with it very brave and valiant. Taking the imperial artillery into his own hands, he built up such formidable power as no one else in all India had in those days. At that time Bhagwant Singh, the landholder (*zamindar*) of Kora, had rebelled against the empire and grown very strong, and several officers who had gone to crush him had been killed by him. At last Burhan-ul-Mulk was put in charge of this campaign and arrived by forced marches. Balwant Singh [TN: so printed; the same rebel, called Bhagwant Singh above and below] cunningly surrounded him, and the battle seemed to go so badly that the bravest lost their nerve. But Burhan-ul-Mulk fought with such gallantry that for a long time his long, white, radiant beard shone and struck awe in the midst of the encircling enemy. Before long Bhagwant Singh fell to his arrow, and the enemy fled.

Burhan-ul-Mulk's second campaign was even greater. In those days the Marathas were very powerful in India. They had made the crowned head of Delhi agree to pay them the *chauth* (a quarter of the revenue), and great champions trembled at their name. Burhan-ul-Mulk went against the Marathas with a strong army and defeated them so severely that they lost their wits and fled headlong, and Burhan-ul-Mulk began the pursuit. A study of the history shows that if Burhan-ul-Mulk had not been forcibly stopped at this point, he would have pressed on and destroyed the Marathas, and the Mughal empire, as in its youth, would have become master of everything in all India. But this unfortunate, declining empire was bound to perish. The intrigues of the courtiers and the envy of the Emperor's favourites stopped Burhan-ul-Mulk's advance.

This convinced Burhan-ul-Mulk that the Emperor had no capacity to judge his own good and ill, and that the courtiers were dishonest and self-seeking. He at once made peace with the Marathas, then resolved to go and live in his own province and, keeping apart from everyone, make his territory strong and well ordered. In short, Burhan-ul-Mulk understood in his heart that the Mughal empire would not recover now, that it was best to take his province and stand apart, and that the Delhi court should be left to its fate.

In Lucknow, as I have said, the Shaikhzadas were all-powerful. They tried to stop him too, as was their habit, but Burhan-ul-Mulk entered by a stratagem, without so much as a nosebleed. Two stories are told of how Burhan-ul-Mulk entered Lucknow. One is that he kept advancing until he was stopped at the Akbari Gate. Being, unlike all the previous governors, an experienced, steady and serious man, he halted and camped in Mahmud Nagar. A day or two later he invited the Shaikhzadas to a feast and received them with great courtesy and hospitality; but while the unsuspecting Shaikhzadas were busy enjoying the many dishes, the imperial troops were quietly entering the Chowk, and they went on advancing until they reached Machchhi Bhawan.

The other story is that Muhammad Khan Bangash had told Burhan-ul-Mulk that the Shaikhzadas of Lucknow were very turbulent and not easy to get the better of, but that the other Shaikhs of the neighbourhood were against them: "Get help from those people, and with their help subdue the people of Lucknow." So Burhan-ul-Mulk halted at Kakori and won the Shaikhs of Kakori over to his side. With their help and guidance he advanced, and hearing that preparations for resistance had been made at Mahmud Nagar and the Akbari Gate, he turned aside from the main road and cut away to the west. He crossed the river near Gau Ghat, came slowly down the far bank, and fell suddenly on Machchhi Bhawan. Whichever way it was, he took the fort without anyone opposing him.

Once Machchhi Bhawan was taken, who could say a word? All the notable Shaikhzadas came and humbly bowed their heads. Burhan-ul-Mulk rode into the Shaikhan Gate on an elephant, and with his own sword cut down the sword that had taken salutes from so many brave men. Then he said to the Shaikhzadas: "Vacate Machchhi Bhawan for our residence." They tried to delay and make excuses, but it was no use. At last they were given a week's grace, and within that time the Shaikhs carried away whatever goods they could, and Burhan-ul-Mulk's soldiers took possession of what was left. Before going to live in the fort, he had a drum-house (*naubat-khana*) built near the spot where he had camped in tents, in which the *naubat* was sounded five times a day until the last days of the Awadh court.

After this Burhan-ul-Mulk went to Ayodhya and built by the river the Bungalow I have already described. But from time to time he came to Lucknow and stayed, since this city was the seat of the province. In his time several new quarters were settled here. All of them were really the camping grounds of his Mughal army chiefs, where people began to build houses to live in permanently: the Katra (walled market quarter) of Sayyid Husain Khan, the Katra of Abu Turab Khan, the Katra of Khudayar Khan, the Katra of Bazan Beg Khan, the Katra of Wafa Beg Khan, the Katra of Muhammad Ali Khan, Bagh Mahanarain, Sarai Maali Khan, and Ismailganj (which was east of Machchhi Bhawan and has now been dug away) are all quarters of that time, or the encampments of Burhan-ul-Mulk's army chiefs.

Nawab Burhan-ul-Mulk had lived only six years in Awadh and Lucknow when, in 1167 of the Muhammadan era (1738 CE), Nadir Shah invaded India and he was summoned to Delhi with the greatest urgency. What happened in that troubled time has nothing to do with Lucknow. He had left his nephew and son-in-law Safdar Jang in Lucknow as his deputy and representative. Nadir had already plundered Delhi and ordered the general massacre, and was still there, when Nawab Burhan-ul-Mulk died in Delhi. His nephew Sher Jang got Nadir Shah to put in a word for him, asking that after the late Nawab the governorship of Awadh should be given to him. But Raja Lachhmi Narain, one of Burhan-ul-Mulk's trusted officials, presented a petition to Nadir to this effect: "Nawab Burhan-ul-Mulk was not pleased with Sher Jang, and that is why he passed him over and gave his daughter to Safdar Jang, who acted as his deputy and is there on his behalf even now. The government is master of Burhan-ul-Mulk's property and may give it to whomever it pleases, since there is no heir. It is also submitted that Safdar Jang is patient, God-fearing, able and true to his word, and the army is pleased with him. Besides this, Burhan-ul-Mulk had promised Your Majesty the sum of twenty million rupees, and Nawab Safdar Jang has arranged to pay it. It will be presented whenever you command. For these reasons it is hoped that Your Majesty will recommend him." On seeing this petition Nadir Shah himself obtained from Muhammad Shah the robe of governorship for Safdar Jang, and sent it to Safdar Jang in Awadh with one of his companions and two hundred horsemen. So, putting on the robe of governorship, Safdar Jang sent the offering of twenty million to Nadir and began to govern his territory.

Safdar Jang's full name was Mirza Muqim Abul-Mansur Khan Safdar Jang. Although he did not have Burhan-ul-Mulk's true bravery, simplicity, uprightness and endurance, he was very generous, high-minded, kind-hearted, a cherisher of his subjects and a good administrator. Three miles from the city he built the fort of Jalalabad, and he also took from the Shaikhzadas the old building of the Panch Mahla inside Machchhi Bhawan, giving them in exchange 700 acres of land at Dogaon to live and settle on. Although this wronged the Shaikhzadas, the settlement of Lucknow gained in extent and progress by it. Safdar Jang rebuilt Machchhi Bhawan from the ground up and put it in good order.

But Safdar Jang had been in his province only five years when he was summoned to Delhi, and he went, leaving Raja Nawal Rai in Lucknow as his deputy. Nawal Rai was a friend of learning, punctual, hardworking, brave and a very great administrator, and God had also given him something of his master's high ambition and generosity. He resolved to build a bridge over the river in front of Machchhi Bhawan. He had deep wells dug for the foundations of the piers, but before work on the piers had begun he had to go, at his master's summons, to fight Ahmad Khan Bangash. He went on this campaign with a very strong army but was killed, and the work on the bridge, once begun, was left unfinished.

Ahmad Khan Bangash was the bravest man of the time. Burhan-ul-Mulk was needed to face him; Safdar Jang was no match for him. The result was that the power of Ahmad Khan, and with him of the Afghans, kept growing. Safdar Jang tried everything; he even brought the Emperor of Delhi himself to face him, but could do him no harm. At Ahmad Khan's signal Hafiz Rahmat Khan began plundering the towns and country towns of Awadh and seized Khairabad, and Ahmad Khan Bangash's own son, Mahmud Khan, set out with an army to take Lucknow. In 1179 of the Muhammadan era (1750 CE) the Pathans set up their post at Malihabad, and in 1180 (1751 CE) a kinsman of Mahmud Khan marched towards Lucknow with twenty thousand troops, camped outside the city, and sent in a police chief (*kotwal*) of his own. The city was empty of Safdar Jang's men. The few who were there fled on hearing that the Pathans were coming, and the Pathans' *kotwal* came into the city and began to behave outrageously.

At that time the most prominent of the Shaikhzadas of Lucknow was Shaikh Muizz-ud-Din. He went out of the city to meet the Afghan chief. Just then someone came and complained to the chief that the townspeople were insulting and humiliating his *kotwal* and that no one obeyed his orders. Shaikh Muizz-ud-Din said: "Who would dare be so insolent? I will go and punish the mischief-makers." With that he came back, called all his kinsmen together and said: "The Pathans' words and deeds cannot be trusted. It is better that we side with Nawab Safdar Jang, fight the Pathans and drive them out of here." Then Shaikh Muizz-ud-Din sold the jewellery of his household to raise a force, and with all the Shaikhzadas attacked the *kotwal*. He fled for his life, and the Shaikh dressed a Mughal in court costume, seated him in his own house, and had it proclaimed that Safdar Jang had sent this Mughal as his *kotwal*. With him he raised a green banner in the name of Ali, and people began to come and gather beneath it.

Hearing of this, the Pathans attacked. The Shaikhzadas fought with all their might and showed their old valour. The Pathans could not stand against them and fled with a force of fifteen thousand, and seizing the chance, the Shaikhzadas drove the Pathans out of the whole country of Awadh.

Two years later, when peace had been made with Ahmad Khan Bangash, Nawab Safdar Jang came to Lucknow again in 1182 of the Muhammadan era (1753 CE) and stayed at Mahdi Ghat. He had a special house built for himself to live in and set about putting the army in order, but he was not given the time. In the same year, while encamped at Papar Ghat near Sultanpur, he died. His body was first taken and buried in the Gulab Bari (Rose Garden) at Faizabad; then a little later his bones were taken to Delhi and buried there, under a most splendid tomb, which travellers from all over the world still look on with reflection and respect.
