We have already described many of the necessary articles of social life and the manners of deportment, but it still seems necessary for us to show the style and dignity of the gentry here in going out and about. As in all the cities of India, Englishness has prevailed here so much that the style that arose in the last civilisation of Asia has been entirely wiped out. But on this occasion we have to describe precisely that which has been wiped out or is near to being wiped out. So we go back to a time even earlier than sixty or seventy years ago, and set before the readers pictures of that time which can now be seen nowhere.

Owing to the absence of fine motor cars like those of today and of long broad phaetons and landaus, and also because the present principles of public health were not in view, there were in those days no long, broad, wide and open roads like those of today, but narrow thoroughfares, through which elephants, horses, camels, *hawadar*s, *bocha*s, *pinas*es, *miyana*s, *sukhpal*s, *doli*s, *rath*s and *bahli*s passed at all times through the crowd of people, crying "Make way! Look out!" However frequented the bazaar and however favoured the promenade, the state of all, without exception, was the same.

Apart from the camel, which was reserved for military needs, letter-carrying couriers or the carrying of loads, all the other conveyances were current among the gentry and gentlemen according to their condition and standing. Princes and Nawabs of the upper class, or other nobles of their degree, went out riding on *hawadar*s and *bocha*s. The *hawadar* was an open litter in the style of a throne, with a leather hood (*tap*) behind, which could be opened or closed by means of iron springs. In cool weather, when the hood was let down, the air was open on every side. Front and back it had poles like those of a *pinas*. Four *kahar*s carried it on their shoulders, and whoever rode in it went through the bazaar with great dignity and gravity, looking at everything and exchanging greetings with acquaintances as he went. From the shape of the *hawadar* it appears that it was a thing invented by the English. On coming to India they devised it according to their taste and by their own ingenuity, and for its refinement, handsomeness and neatness it greatly pleased the gentlemen of India. Now its use has entirely died out. Although a few *hawadar*s still lie about at the houses of some old gentlemen, which are not seen in the ordinary going and coming of gentlemen, they are occasionally seen in the wedding processions of wealthy Hindus.

The *bocha* was a more dignified and grave conveyance. Its shape was like the brougham or *rath* carriages of today, with legs instead of wheels, and two poles each, like those of a *pinas*, before and behind; and at least eight, and often sixteen, *kahar*s carried it, because it was heavier than all the conveyances carried by *kahar*s. Other nobles too may perhaps have ridden in this conveyance sometimes, but I saw only Wajid Ali Shah riding in it in Calcutta. And apart from him I have never seen this conveyance with anyone anywhere else. The King used to go about his gardens, palaces and mansions riding in it, and besides the attendants of his retinue all round, honoured pillars of the state and His Majesty's companions walked alongside on foot. But this too was certainly an invention of the English, taken from the English carriages of that period and made fit to be carried by *kahar*s.

The *sukhpal* was in those days a most honoured conveyance of women, which was a purely Indian thing and a complete example of the refinements of Indian taste. It was a red domed litter. On a long broad frame (*khatola*) a splendid red tower-like structure was built, on which gold and silver finials were set. Curtains hung on all four sides. It too had two poles or one pole before and behind, and many *kahar*s carried it. This conveyance was reserved for high-ranking *begam*s and the ladies of the royal palace.

The *rath* was a wheeled carriage of the same style, to which bullocks were yoked. *Rath*s still exist at the houses of the *ta'alluqdar*s (landed magnates) and honoured *zamindar*s of the villages and in the native States, but they are becoming useless day by day and their use is dying out. In Lucknow in those days there were thousands of *rath*s for the needs of the royal palaces alone. When Shuja'-ud-Daula's wife, Bahu Begam Sahiba, in the reign of Nawab Asaf-ud-Daula, passed her widowhood at Faizabad in the style of a ruling queen, there were eight or nine hundred *rath*s in her establishment alone. And in olden times, when the Kings of Delhi made long journeys in their dominions, their exalted ladies went with them riding in these *rath*s.

The *bahli* was the ordinary bullock cart, in which a *khatola* was set on two wheels, then four poles were set upright on it and a canopy fixed, and over it a cover was thrown for *purdah*. Men and women often travelled in it. In those days this conveyance was the means of travel for both villagers and townsmen of the middle class. *Bahli*s still exist in great numbers in the villages, but the need for them diminishes day by day, and a time is soon coming when this conveyance will become as rare as the phoenix.

As for all the other conveyances, people know them themselves; there is no need for us to describe their shape and appearance.

In any case, all these conveyances were to be seen passing through all the roads and lanes of the city. Most people rode in *pinas*es. Scholars, physicians, nobles and the well-to-do, to whom God gave the means, kept four *kahar*s in service, who also acted as attendants and served for the conveyance. Those who had even a little *bankpan*, or who wished to show a soldierly air, which in those days was general among all the people of the city, went out riding on horseback, the horses being decked out as brides with silver ornaments and *karchob* trappings. Dignitaries of the highest rank went to and fro seated on elephants, which, despite their size, passed through all the lanes without difficulty. On the elephants were plain broadcloth or *karchob* housings (*jhul*), and on them were fastened open *howdah*s or shaded tower-like *'amari*s.

The women's conveyances, which were in *sukhpal*s and *pinas*es, went out with great elaboration and dignity. Over the *pinas* red *chhatle* (covers) were spread, on which braid and *lachka* were sometimes stitched. The *kahar*s wore *jhagga*s (loose coats) of red broadcloth, and on their heads were red turbans with *gul-gir* (plume-holders), on whose plumes silver fishes hung. The fish is regarded in India as the best omen. When bidding farewell, or when someone is going on some important business, even today the words "*Dahi machhli*" (curd and fish) slip from women's tongues. Probably it has to do with astrology, and it also seems to be a charm of the astrologers that silver fishes should be made and stitched into the turbans of the *kahar*s who go in front, so that when going anywhere the fishes should always be in view.

Alongside the women's *pinas* a *kahari* ran holding the corner of the *chhatla*. The style of these *kahari*s too was of a special kind. The chief mark was that their *lahnga* (full skirt) had so broad an edging that more than half of it was edging alone.

Of these conveyances, in the city now only the *pinas* remains, or occasionally some gentleman is seen on a horse or an elephant.

Now we must see what the style of the gentry was in going out. We have described dress, but to show their picture we again need to point out their style and appearance to some extent. In what I have described about the dignity of conveyances, everything except the *bocha* and *hawadar* is the same as came from Delhi. Lucknow has no special connection with them. In fact this was the dignity of Delhi itself, which, showing its last glimpse with passing pomp in Lucknow, disappeared.

But in dress Lucknow became distinct from Delhi. Now it has become improper to sit at home with *kurta* or shirt taken off, but in those days, truth to tell, the home dress here was a *gharqi*. The court here was Shi'a, and everything here was moulded in the mould of Shi'ism. According to Imami law there is no harm in the thighs remaining uncovered [TN: the author's statement of the Shi'a legal position], unlike the Hanafis, in whose school the part of the body from the navel to the knees is included in the *satr* (what must be covered) and must be hidden. And on this ground in Delhi a *lungi* in the style of the *tahmat* was generally tied, in which the body is covered to below the knees. In the civilisation here this need did not remain, and the *lungi* here became only a thin *gharqi* or *janghiya*, in which the body is covered from the navel to the groin and all the rest of the body remains bare. The idea of going out as a cultivated man and a proper man had indeed grown among people, but at home there was not a thread on the body except a *gharqi*. And this had become so common that no harm was seen in meeting someone at one's own house in this naked style. But when these same gentlemen went out, the dignity was quite different. The *chaugoshiya* cap stretched on its mould; a bright, clean, shining *angarkha* which looked as though it had just come from the washerman's house, with its edging and sleeves crimped that very moment; wide trousers of *gulbadan* or *nainsukh* [TN: printed *tansukh*, probably for *nainsukh*]; a triangular *rumal* on the shoulder, a hand *rumal* and a cane in the hand; and on the feet a *khurd-noka* velvet shoe, a slipper made in Lucknow. This was the style of every man, high and low, in going out.

Very many people had such regard for this style and dress in going out that their clothes never looked dirty. It seemed that they had come from the washerman's that very moment, although for months they never had occasion to be washed. And what happened was this: they left the house with two *ghari*s of the day remaining; strolling along, avoiding everything, avoiding even their own shadow, they took a turn of the Chowk; and they came back when two *ghari*s of the night had passed. And the first thing they did on arrival was to put the cap on its mould and cover it with a cloth. They carefully folded the *angarkha*, trousers and shoulder *rumal*, tied them like a bundle in the hand *rumal* and hung it on a peg. And tying on a *gharqi* and putting on some old shoe or slipper, they sat down. It was by the blessing of this care that costly and shawl clothes lasted for four or five generations with such care that they neither got dirty, nor tore, nor were attacked by moth, but always remained new. And at wedding ceremonies or gatherings of pomp and splendour they went wearing such royal dress that people who knew their condition and standing were astonished.

Although nobles of the upper class, especially princes, scholars and physicians, invariably went out in conveyances, walking about was not considered improper for the gentry as it is in the present age. People of every class and degree took the air outside on foot in the same way, and those who walked sat on equal terms with the greatest gentlemen and dignitaries, and no harm was seen in it.
