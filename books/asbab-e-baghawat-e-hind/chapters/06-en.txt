# The Third Cause

The Government's ignorance of the true conditions, ways and habits of the subjects, and of the hardships they suffered, by which the hearts of the subjects were turning away from our Government.

There is no doubt that our Government did not know the conditions and ways of the subjects or the sufferings they endured. And what was the reason it did not know? Knowledge of people's conditions and ways comes from mixing with them, from close ties, and from coming and going freely among one another. This happens when one people mixes with another and, building love and sincerity, settles among them as fellow countrymen, as the Muslims, who were of another religion and came from another country, did when they settled in Hindustan, forming brotherly ties with those who were foreign to them. But in truth our Government cannot have this, which is the real means of knowing the conditions of the subjects, nor can such shared settlement be imagined for it. That leaves the possibility that the subjects themselves should report their sufferings, but the subjects had no means to do so, because they had not the slightest share in the Government's measures. If anyone sent some petition irregularly, or laid it before the Governor-General, it was treated as a complaint, not as the exercise of a right to take part in the Government's measures, and so it did no good. It was therefore necessary that someone else should inform the Government of the subjects' conditions. That information depended on the reports of the district officers. They themselves did not know, and had no way of finding out. Their inattention in this matter, and their touchiness, are well known. Everyone was afraid of their awe. No one dared to speak the truth, least of all anything against the officers' temper and liking. Every servant and every chief at their court spoke flattery out of fear. And our Government, which is in truth a government by a body of men, took on by these means the form of a personal rule. So this way of learning the conditions of the subjects through the district officers was not merely insufficient; it did not exist at all. Therefore the condition of the subjects always remained hidden from our Government. Whatever harm a new law of the Government did to the condition, welfare and well-being of the subjects, there was no one to remove it or to report it. In matters of this kind the subjects had no one to grieve for them but their own blood, which burned and burned in their bodies, or their own helplessness, behind whose veil they wept and wept and kept silent.

Why should the subjects of Hindustan not have suffered poverty and hardship under our Government's rule? The chief livelihood of the subjects of Hindustan was service, and it was counted a profession. Though people of every kind complained of unemployment, the Muslims complained most of all. It should be considered that the Hindus, who are the original inhabitants of this country, did not in former times live by service at all, but were all engaged in the work of the land. The Brahmin had nothing to do with service. Those called the *Bais* (Vaishya castes) were always engaged in trade and moneylending. Old histories prove that the Chhattris (Kshatriyas), who at times also ruled this country, did not live by service either, but were bound to the land and to the lordship of some piece of land in brotherhood. Their soldiers were not hired men; in time of need they gathered as kinsmen and an army was formed, as can still be seen to some small extent in the Russian empire. The Kayasth caste, it is true, appear to have lived by service from old times. The Muslims are not natives of this country. They came to Hindustan with earlier kings in search of employment and settled here, so they all lived by service, and they complained of the lack of employment more than the original inhabitants of this country. Honourable service as soldiers, which suits the temper of the unlettered subjects here best, was very scarce under our Government. The Government's army, which consisted mostly of *tilangas* (sepoys), was a service that men of good family thought it beneath them to enter. In the cavalry, it is true, there was still service for men of good family, but it was so small in number that it bore no comparison with the cavalry of former times. Besides service under the Government, men used to serve the governors, chiefs and nobles of earlier times, and their numbers should not be thought small either. Now this does not exist under our Government. For this reason the subjects suffered an extreme lack of employment. The result was that when the rebels wished to hire men, thousands gathered to take service, and as a hungry man falls upon grain in time of famine, so these people fell upon service.

reason will not believe he fears the fast of Ramazan.

Many men took service for only an *anna* (a small coin) or an anna and a half a day, and many took a *seer* (about a kilogram) or a seer and a half of grain a day in place of wages. This shows plainly that the subjects of Hindustan were as eager for service as they were distressed and pinched by poverty and want.

There was another way by which the subjects prospered in earlier reigns, namely grants of land and gifts and rewards. When Shah Jahan came to the throne, on the day of his accession alone he gave four lakh bighas of land and a hundred and twenty villages in jagir, and lakhs of rupees in gifts. Under our Government this was shut off entirely; indeed even the earlier jagirs were resumed, and through this resumption thousands of men came to want for their next meal. We have already described the poverty of the zamindars and cultivators. The livelihood of craftsmen was wholly lost through the spread and use of goods imported from England, so that no one in Hindustan cared any more for a woman who made needles or a man who made matches. The thread of the weavers was utterly broken, and these wretches were the hottest of all in this upheaval. By God's grace, since Hindustan too was part of the empire of Great Britain, the Government was bound to attend to this distress of the subjects and to strive to remove their grief of spirit and their heartfelt grievances.

Company notes brought a new kind of burden on the country, for which there is no precedent in any earlier reign. However much money was borrowed, the means of collecting its interest, and indeed the interest, the expenses and the profit, was found from the country. In short, the country became poor and needy in every way. Old families who once had thousands were now pinched even for a livelihood, and this was a real cause of the subjects' displeasure with the Government. I tell you most truly that it was for this reason that people's hearts longed for a change of rule, welcomed a new rule and rejoiced in it from the heart. We tell the truth, and again we say that we tell the very truth: when the Government conquered Afghanistan, people grieved greatly. Why? Only because now, they thought, religion would be openly interfered with. When Gwalior was conquered, when the Punjab was conquered, when Oudh was taken, people grieved deeply. Why? Because the Hindustanis had great prosperity from these neighbouring Hindustani states. Employment was often to be had there. There was a great trade in Hindustani goods of every kind. With the ruin of these states, poverty and want grew ever greater. There were very many good and beneficial things in our Government's rule too, but I do not lay blame on everything, as someone has said:

[TN: Persian couplet.] You have told all its faults; tell its virtues too.
Do not deny wisdom to please a few common folk.

Peace, security and freedom; the roads made safe; the robbers, highwaymen and *thugs* (ritual stranglers of travellers) destroyed; the roads well made; the comfort of travellers; merchants sending their goods far and wide; the letters of rich and poor, high and low, reaching distant countries without fail; the end of bloodshed and civil war; the breaking of the power of the strong over the weak: these and many other such things are so good that they never were and never will be under any other rule. But consider that these things do not remove the misery we speak of. And see who chiefly benefited from these advantages of the rule we have mentioned. First the women, who lived in every comfort, safe from the killing of their children in civil war, from being robbed by thieves and thugs, and from tyrants laying hands on their husbands and children, and safe from a thousand other misfortunes. See then how loyal they were, and how full of praise for the Government's rule. The moneylenders and merchants lived in great comfort, and not one of them was disloyal. In short, of those who had suffered no harm from the Government's rule, not one became disloyal.
