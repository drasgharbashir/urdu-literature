For my part I hold that this disorder had one real cause, and that all the other causes are its branches. Nor is this view of mine fanciful or mere guesswork. Many wise men of earlier times have agreed on it, all the writers on the principles of government are on my side in this, and all the histories of Europe and Africa are most reliable witnesses to the truth of my opinion.

Everyone has long accepted that, for the good order, excellence and permanence of a government, it is necessary that the subjects have a share in the government of the country. Only through the people can the good or bad of the rulers' measures be known, before the harm reaches a stage where it can no longer be cured.

[TN: Persian couplet, Sa'di.] A spring may be stopped at its source with a spade;
once it is in flood, it cannot be crossed even on an elephant.

And this cannot be had until the subjects have a share in the government of the country. Above all it was necessary for our Government, whose men came from a foreign country and differed from this country in religion, custom, manners, temper and habits, to keep this in mind. The administration of a government, and its excellence, order and permanence, depend on knowing the ways and habits of the country and then respecting them. For a look at past histories, which are in truth a daily journal of the varied habits, ideas and ways of mankind, shows that their habits, ideas and ways did not arise according to any rational rule, but grew up in each country and nation as chance would have it. So the rules of a government depend on these customs and ways, not the customs, ways and habits on the government, and in this lies the permanence and stability of a government. For so long as those habits and morals have become firmly fixed in the hearts of the subjects and have become like a second human nature, to go against them is plainly to go against human nature and to keep everyone aggrieved. Shall we forget the state of misrule in Bengal that came about through this very ignorance in 1765, when the *diwani* (revenue administration) of Bengal was handed over to the English Company, when the history of John Clark Marshman reminds us of it? And shall we not remember the good that came to Bengal from Lord Hastings's knowledge of the language and of the customs of the country?

No doubt a share for the subjects of Hindustan in Parliament was neither possible nor of any use, but there was no reason not to give them a share in the Legislative Council. This, then, is the one thing that is the root of the whole disorder in Hindustan, and all the things that piled up are its branches.

We do not say that our Government made no effort to learn the conditions and ways of the country. On the contrary, we fully acknowledge it, and we take some of the Government's laws, the instructions of the Board of Revenue and the Honourable Mr Thomason's revenue manual as witness to it. But there is no doubt that it paid no attention to learning the conditions, habits, ideas, customs, ways, temper, nature and abilities of the subjects. Certainly our Government did not know how the day passed for our subjects and what misery the night brought, what grief and misery they were sinking into day by day, and what grievances were gathering in their hearts day after day. Little by little these had piled up in great numbers, and at the slightest touch they burst out all at once.

The absence of Hindustanis from the Legislative Council did not only do the harm that the Government could not properly learn the real harm of the laws and regulations it issued, and that the general public had no chance or means to remove that harm and to put forward its own wishes. The very great harm was that the subjects did not learn the purpose, the real meaning and the true intention of the Government. The subjects misunderstood every measure of the Government. Whatever measure the Government made, the Hindustanis, because they had no part in it and did not know its purpose and reasoning, did not see its basis, and always thought that this too was meant to ruin, destroy and disgrace them and their countrymen and to rob them of their *dharam* (religion). And those measures of the Government that were in fact against custom and against the temper and nature of the Hindustanis, whether good or bad in themselves, only strengthened their mistaken ideas further. Little by little it came to the point that the subjects of Hindustan used to compare our Government to sweet poison, a knife dipped in honey and a slow fire, and believed it in their hearts. They thought that if they were safe from the Government today they would not be tomorrow, and if tomorrow, not the day after. There was no one to ask after their condition, and no measure to remove this mistaken idea of theirs. When the subjects feel towards their Government as one should feel towards a mortal enemy, what loyalty can such a government expect from such subjects? And since our Government was not in fact like that, the settling of these mistaken ideas in the hearts of the Hindustanis, and the failure to cure the grievances in their hearts, came only from this: that Hindustanis had no part in the Legislative Council. Had they been in it, all these things would have been cleared up as they arose. If one looks closely now, it is this one thing alone that put out many branches and caused needless disorder throughout Hindustan.

Do not say that our Government allowed the presses to print everything except abuse, slander and whatever might lead to sedition or rebellion, and that before a law was passed there was consultation and everyone had the right to put forward objections. For these measures were entirely insufficient, indeed entirely useless, to cure the very great matters of which we speak.

And we do not wish to be drawn here into a discussion of how Hindustanis, who are very ignorant and untrained, could have taken part in the Legislative Council, what rule could be found for their participation, and, if the subjects of Hindustan had been given a share in the Legislative Council like Parliament, what the method of choosing them would have been, and how many difficulties would have arisen. For here we have only to prove that this was very good and very necessary for the Government, and that these disorders broke out because it was lacking. On the method of giving the subjects a share we have a separate opinion, which should be seen, and any discussion should be held there.

This defect in our Government spread through all the affairs of Hindustan. However many causes of rebellion gathered, though they all branch from this one matter, if they are considered and brought together they rest on five heads.

First: the misunderstanding of the subjects, that is, their taking the measures of the Government in the opposite sense.

Second: the introduction of laws, regulations and methods of rule that did not suit the government of Hindustan and the habits of Hindustanis, or that did harm.

Third: the Government's ignorance of the true conditions, ways and habits of the subjects, and of the hardships they suffered, by which the hearts of the subjects were turning away from the Government.

Fourth: the neglect by our Government of things that it was its duty and obligation to do for the government of Hindustan.

Fifth: the bad management and neglect of the army.

Now we shall set out each of these five heads in detail, and each of their branches separately, and success is from God alone.
