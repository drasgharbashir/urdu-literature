# The Fourth Cause

The neglect by our Government of things that it was its duty and obligation to do for the government of Hindustan.

The points we write down here may displease some of our officers, but here it is most necessary for us to write the truth and to speak openly from the heart. What we speak of is the thing by which wild beasts of the forest are caught in the net and savage animals are tamed; what then of man? Lord Bacon's *Essays* would not be enough for us to set out here the benefits of friendship, love, close ties and union. But this much must be said: the love between a government and its subjects is far greater than love between friends or friendship between neighbours. A friend has to make friends with one person; a government with all its subjects. Lover and beloved are only two people, who through the bond of the heart are counted as one. A government has to form such a bond with all its subjects that subjects and government together become one body.

[TN: Persian couplet, Sa'di.] The subjects are the root, the king the tree;
and the tree, my son, is strong from its root.

Could our Government not have done this in Hindustan? Why could it not? For we see every day that a heartfelt union arises between two men of different countries and religions when they wish for it. And we see too that the bitterest enmity and hostility arise between two men of the same nation, religion and country. This proves that sameness of religion, country and nation is not necessary for love, union and friendship. Is not this the wise counsel of the holy Paul: "As we love you, so may the Lord make your love increase and abound towards one another and towards all"? Its fruit is true love, not only for one's neighbours and countrymen but for everyone, even one's enemies, and that this love and kindness should grow day by day. And is not this saying of the holy Messiah a comfort to the heart: "Whatever you would that men should do to you, do you even so to them, for this is the law and the prophets"? The meaning of this counsel of the holy Messiah is love. In short, no wise man can deny that love and union are a very fine thing, that they bear very good fruit and keep away many evils. Until today our Government has not created this love with the subjects of Hindustan. [TN: The marginal notes of the edition cite 1 Thessalonians 3:12 for the words of Paul and Matthew 7:12 for the words of Christ.]

It is also a rule of love, placed by nature in human and even in animal instinct, that love flows from the higher to the lower. A father's love for his son begins before the son's love for his father. In the same way a man's love for his wife comes before the wife's love for her husband. On this basis it is that love which the lower begins towards the higher is counted flattery, not love. The conclusion is that our Government ought first to have taken the lead in love and union with the subjects. Then this rule of love, learned from a thousand experiences, that love cannot help working on another's heart and drawing it to itself, would have worked on the hearts of the subjects, and the subjects would have loved our Government even more, indeed would have been devoted to it.

[TN: Persian couplet.] Love is that wrecker of homes
that brought you to our house.

But alas, our Government did not do this.

If our Government claims that this is false, that it did not act so, but showed love and received evil in return for good, we leave the judgement of it to the Government itself. If that had really been so, the subjects would certainly have loved our Government more than it loved them. Truly, love is a thing of the heart that cannot be made by saying it or by pretending. Though its signs are seen outwardly too, the truth is that it can be neither described nor pointed out; but the heart knows it well, and indeed holds in its hand a true balance that weighs even the slightest more or less.

[TN: Persian couplet.] Under this dome of heaven there is a path from heart to heart:
from the side of malice, malice; from the side of love, love.

Until today our Government has kept itself as separate and as unmixed with Hindustanis as fire and dry grass. Our Government and the Hindustanis are two pieces of stone, white and black, that are known apart, and between them there is a gap that widens day by day. Yet our Government ought to be with the subjects of Hindustan like a piece of mica, which is one though it has two colours: black spots look very handsome on the white, and the white shows a wonderful beauty in the black.

We say nothing unjust. Our Government must certainly have a special religious love for Christians, but we ask of our Government that brotherly love towards the subjects of Hindustan, and that affection added to brotherly love, which the holy Peter counselled. Now consider: our officers and the Hindustanis were not of one blood, not of one religion, not of one custom; the subjects did not consent from the heart; there was no love or union between them. On what basis, then, did our officers expect loyalty from Hindustan? [TN: The marginal note cites 2 Peter 1:7.]

Look at the history of the earlier empires of Hindustan. First the Muslims conquered Hindustan. Under the rule of the Turks and the Pathans there was no love or close dealing with the subjects of Hindustan, so long as the empire did not take on the form of security and ease. Under the Mughal empire, from the reign of Akbar the First, this coming together began in earnest and continued until the time of Shah Jahan. Though in those days too the subjects suffered hardships from the disorder in the principles of the empire, the wound was healed by the brotherly love they had for one another. In 1669, that is, in the reign of Alamgir, this love was broken. Because of the opposition and rebellion of Hindus such as Shivaji the Maratha and others, Alamgir grew displeased with the whole Hindu community and sent orders to his governors that all Hindus be treated harshly and that *jizya* (the poll tax on non-Muslims) be taken from every one of them. The harm and displeasure this caused the subjects is well known. In short, our Government, even in a hundred years of rule, did not create love and affection with the subjects.

No one can deny that keeping the subjects in honour and winning them over, that is, holding their hearts in one's hand, is a very great cause of a government's permanence. A man is far happier to receive little with honour than to receive much with little honour. To dishonour anyone is so bad a thing that it wounds a man's heart. It is the very thing that breeds enmity without any outward harm being done, and its wound is so deep that it never heals.

[TN: Arabic couplet.] The wounds of spears can be healed,
but what the tongue has wounded does not heal.

Winning hearts has the opposite property. It is the thing by which an enemy becomes a friend, and the love of friends grows greater. The stranger becomes one's own. It is the thing by which the wild beasts of the forest, beasts and birds alike, become obedient. If it is shown to subjects, how obedient and submissive they will be! At the beginning of the rule it was this that had drawn everyone's heart towards our Government and created a heartfelt obedience. Our Government certainly forgot these things. Surely all the subjects of Hindustan will bear witness that our Government has made them utterly worthless and of no account. A gentleman of Hindustan has not even such standing before a petty European as a petty European has before a very great duke. It was as if it were believed that there was no gentleman in Hindustan.

All these things, that is, love, affection, honour and the winning over of the subjects, are shown on the part of the Government through the covenanted officers who carry on the Government's work in Hindustan and have dealings, contact and meetings with the subjects. However good the Government's intention, it will never be shown until these men resolve to show it. The habits, conduct and manners of the covenanted officers of earlier days were very different from those of the officers of today. Those earlier men greatly honoured Hindustanis, treated them with every courtesy, held their hearts in their hands, and shared in a friendly way their sorrows and joys, although they held very great authority and power in Hindustan and did not let go of the dignity, awe and majesty that befit rule. Even so, they showed such love and honour to Hindustanis that every person in the country was captivated by their manners and their love, and would say in wonder, "What good people these are, who for all their dignity, splendour and power remain without pride, and how courteously they meet us!" They treated those who were counted great men in Hindustan in the same way. Surely those men had followed the holy Peter, and added affection to brotherly love. Among the covenanted officers of the present day, most are of the opposite temper. Has not their pride and arrogance made all Hindustanis worthless in their eyes? Has not their ill temper and disdain struck a needless terror into the hearts of Hindustanis? Does our Government not know that even the most honoured Hindustani trembled before the officers and was afraid of being insulted? And is it hidden that a respectable clerk reads out the file before the *sahib* (the English officer), speaks with folded hands, and, at the sahib's ill temper, harsh words and even abuse, weeps in his heart and says, "Alas, there is no bread to be had anywhere else; better to cut grass than to serve like this"? I do not lay this charge on all officers. Surely there are also officers whose love, manners and virtues are known to everyone, whom all Hindustanis know like the moon and the sun, and whom they take for a pattern of the officers of old. In truth they follow the very counsel the holy Messiah gave to the holy Simon and Andrew when they were casting their nets into the sea for fish: "Follow me, and I will make you fishers of men." By their good nature they have drawn the subjects into the net of their love. These officers have kept the awe of their rule and yet have shown no needless pride to the subjects, and they have won the blessing the holy Messiah spoke of: "Blessed are the poor in spirit, for theirs is the kingdom of heaven." These officers have shown their just gentleness to all the subjects and have ruled the earth, as the holy Jesus said: "Blessed are the meek, for they shall inherit the earth." These officers have shown their light to the subjects according to the saying of the Messiah: "Let your light so shine before men, that they may see your good works and glorify your Father which is in heaven." Officers of this kind were few, but wherever they were, they were loved. [TN: The marginal notes cite Matthew 4:19 and Matthew 5:3, 5:5 and 5:16. Sir Syed renders "poor in spirit" as "those who are without pride in their hearts".]

There is no doubt either that these things were not distasteful to people of every community alike, but they weighed far more heavily on the Muslims. The reason is very clear. For hundreds of years the Muslims have lived in honour in Hindustan too. There is a sense of honour in their nature and instinct. There is very little greed for money in their hearts. They do not wish to lose honour for any gain. It will have been seen many times that what people of other communities bear without grief, Muslims found it very hard to bear even in a lesser degree. Let us grant that these traits in the Muslims are very bad; still, they cannot help it. The nature God has given cannot be changed. That may be the Muslims' misfortune, but it is not their fault. These were the grievances because of which they longed in their hearts for a change of rule, and were glad to hear news against the Government. But the pity is that our Government did not overlook the welfare of the Muslims. Their ability, learning and culture were all in its view, but these people did not know it, and the intention and heartfelt purpose of our Government were not made known through the officers.

A great cause of the displeasure of the people of Hindustan, above all of the Muslims, was that there was very little advancement to high office. It is only a very short time since these people were held in honour throughout Hindustan and held great offices. Their aim and their wish were still the same: they wanted their rank and standing to rise in the same way, and no way to it could be seen. At the beginning of the Government's rule, men of good family and standing were chosen and given offices of honour. Little by little this ceased. No doubt those men had little ability, so the rule of examinations is in our view in no way to blame, and in truth no one resents it. No doubt examinations produced good officials, but men were appointed to those offices of honour who were of no worth at all in the eyes of Hindustanis. In granting certificates, little account was taken of family and standing. The advancement of Hindustanis made by Lord Bentinck was never carried further. There is no doubt that, because of the small number of offices, that advancement was very insufficient. Very high officers admit that the advancement of Hindustanis has not been what it ought to have been.

The people of Hindustan had an old habit of attending their kings' *darbars* (royal courts), and rejoiced to see the king's splendour, majesty, pomp and grandeur. It is a rule set in human nature that the heart rejoices in its own king and the lord of its country, and knows: "This is our king and our lord; we are his followers and subjects." The people of Hindustan above all had been used to this from old times, and it had long been lost. The Governors-General did hold darbars on tour, but these did not fully satisfy the wish of Hindustanis. Lord Auckland and Lord Ellenborough did hold royal darbars. Perhaps this practice was disliked in England, but the truth is that it suited the conditions of Hindustan perfectly, and even then it was not all it should have been. May God ever protect our Queen, Her Majesty Victoria. May God ever protect the Governor-General of Hindustan, ruler of the realm of India and viceroy of Her Majesty. We hope that now no wish of the people of Hindustan will remain unfulfilled.

It is true that true kingship belongs to God Most High, who created the whole world, but God Most High has created kings in this world as a pattern of His true kingdom, so that His servants, from this pattern, may know their true King and give Him thanks. So great sages and wise men have held that, just as the qualities of that true King are generosity, giving, bounty and kindness, so their pattern must be found in these earthly kings too. It is for this reason that great wise men have called the king *zill-Allah* (the shadow of God). From this it follows that, just as the boundless bounty of God is upon all His servants, so the bounty and rewards of kings should be upon all their subjects. At first it may seem that to give gifts and rewards for every small thing is to empty the treasury to no purpose. But it is not so; on the contrary, the great benefit of gifts and rewards is that the subjects' love for their king grows. It is a general rule that *al-insan 'abid al-ihsan* (man is the slave of kindness). So all the subjects, seeing their king's gifts and rewards, cannot help loving him from the heart, and are eager to render good service and show loyalty. The books of history show that this was very common in earlier reigns. Gifts and rewards of every kind came to the subjects and the chiefs: very costly robes of honour, fine presents, cash, and land and jagirs were given as rewards. Men of good family received titles and won honour among their peers. They had great ambitions in their hearts, and the subjects of Hindustan liked this very much; indeed they had been used to it for hundreds of years. Our Government did away with this practice entirely. No one among the subjects could any longer hope for such outward gifts and rewards, and for this reason their hearts longed for a change of rule, so much so that whenever they heard news that the Honourable East India Company's lease was ending and Her Majesty's rule beginning, they rejoiced. In the reigns of earlier kings, gifts and rewards were of two kinds. One was what the king spent on his own pleasures and on indulging his unworthy habits. This was in truth unworthy, and Hindustanis disliked it too; indeed they were displeased by gifts to rogues and the undeserving. The other kind was what the king gave to his loyal servants and victorious chiefs, and to the scholars, the pious, the poor, the poets, the retired and those without means among his subjects. Everyone desires gifts of this kind and is displeased by their absence. Some say that these things make the subjects lacking in spirit and fond of ease, so that they no longer work hard and earn their bread by the strength of their arms, and that the king should therefore give up this kind of reward and give the other kind instead, namely freedom, so that people have room to earn their own bread. This is true, but such a reward can be given only when the subjects are prosperous and educated; not by pulling the nose-rope out of the nostrils of creatures with the habits of beasts and driving them into a forest without food or water, saying, "Find your own food and water." What will their end be, but that they either die or act like wild beasts? By this we mean this rebellion of Hindustan.

Anger is a thing that hides the truth of affairs from the eye. The temper turns to revenge and punishment. It is true that what happened in Hindustan in 1857 was such that whatever anger our officers feel, and whatever revenge and punishment they take, is justified. But the circumstances of Hindustan should be considered: how much rebellion in Hindustan was real, why it grew so great and why it appeared so great, and why the unfortunate Muslims appeared more rebellious in some districts. It should be considered that for hundreds of years rule in Hindustan had been unstable. The people of Hindustan had an inherited habit: when some noble, chief or prince gained power, thousands gathered round him. They did not think it any fault of theirs to serve him, to act as his revenue officer or to manage for him. In Hindustan there is a well-known saying: "What fault has a servant? He serves whoever hires him and pays his wages." But when a chief was overthrown and another chief set up in his place, they did count it a fault not to obey the new one. The nobles and chiefs of Hindustan, especially those who held sway over Hindustan before the Government's rule and through whom Hindustan was split among many petty rulers, had the habit of not troubling the servants of the sword and the pen in any way. All the people of Hindustan had the same habit. When the mischief-makers raised their heads in Hindustan and wished to hire men, thousands who were in want of bread and longing for service went and took service. They all said, "What fault is ours? We live by service." Many of the common subjects, from their old habit of thinking, "Whoever is chief now, him we obey; we are subjects, and follow whoever is strongest," became followers of the rebels. Many Government officials, thinking to save their lives by keeping up appearances with the rebels and to return to the Government's side when its power was restored, also became guilty, though there is no doubt that at heart they were loyal to the Government. Many people and officials did something on the spur of the moment, from compulsion, or ignorance, or human weakness. They thought that now the Government would not pardon this chance, forced or ignorant fault of theirs, but would punish them. From this fear and dread they had no choice but to join the rebels. Many men had in truth done nothing, but joined the rebels out of fear and for many other reasons. Many people at that time did things that they, in their own minds and understanding, did not think were crimes against the Government. If the circumstances of the revolt throughout Hindustan are looked into, we are sure that both the communities that live in Hindustan will be found equally rebellious in this disorder, indeed each worse than the other, and all the circumstances of Hindustan bear witness to this. But the reason why the Muslims appeared more rebellious in some districts should not be thought to be only that a Muslim king laid claim to the throne of Delhi, or that the Muslims really were as rebellious as they appeared. No. The officers' temper was suddenly roused against the Muslims by the things the Muslims seemed to have done. Their opponents had great room to put forward self-serving tales, and made a small thing very large. On one side the officers grew more displeased, and on the other the Muslims grew more afraid and despairing, and by their ill fate they appeared more rebellious than they were. There is no doubt that rebellion of the fifth kind was very common among the Muslims, and that they were very glad at the thought of a change of rule, the reason for which we have stated at every point. Yet our Government will know from whom the most devoted acts of self-sacrificing loyalty came in this upheaval. Before God, who has true kingship, and before the kings of this world, to whom God has given earthly rule, all are sinners. Truly said the holy David, peace be upon him: "O Lord, enter not into judgement with Thy servant, for in Thy sight shall no man living be justified. Have mercy upon me, O God, according to Thy lovingkindness; according to the multitude of Thy tender mercies blot out my transgressions. Wash me thoroughly from mine iniquity, and cleanse me from my sin." Amen. [TN: The marginal notes cite Psalm 143:2 and Psalm 51:1–2.]

May God ever protect our Queen, Her Majesty Victoria. I cannot describe the excellence of the merciful proclamation that Her Majesty has issued. Surely God's hand is upon Her Majesty's head. Surely this merciful proclamation was issued by inspiration.

It has been a very old rule in Hindustan that when a king, whether by right or without right, was established in the capital, all the chiefs of the provinces turned to him. In this upheaval too it happened that when the King of Delhi sat on the throne and the news reached the provinces that the King of Delhi had taken the throne, everyone turned to the King. When the King of Delhi was captured and the capital came into our Government's hands, everyone was sure that all the mischief-makers who had raised their heads would submit, except perhaps the men of the rebel army. Why this did not happen, we do not think it necessary to write in this opinion of ours.
