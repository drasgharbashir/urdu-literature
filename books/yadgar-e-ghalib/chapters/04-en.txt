# The Journey to Calcutta

Apparently the Mirza made no long journey except the one to Calcutta. On the way there and back he also stayed a few months in Lucknow and Benares. The reason for going to Calcutta was this. When the Mirza's uncle Nasrullah Beg Khan died, the Mirza was nine years old and his brother seven. After Nasrullah Beg Khan's death the government had charged on the State of Firozpur Jhirka a pension for his dependants and heirs, among whom the Mirza and his brother were included; and so long as the Mirza was a minor he received whatever came from there. When he came of age and was married as well, the needs of youth and housekeeping grew very great, and whatever property there was in the house was all spent within a few days; so he was forced to think about his livelihood. First the Mirza came to believe, rightly or wrongly, that we were not receiving the full amount of the pension which the government had had fixed for our family from Firozpur. Needs pressed him hard; on one side the demands of creditors made his life a misery; on the other his younger brother had gone mad; for a free-spirited man like the Mirza this was a very hard time. In this struggle nothing occurred to him except to go to Calcutta and lay a petition about the pension before the Supreme Government. Thus the Mirza writes of this state in a letter: "On one side the turmoil of my brother's madness, on the other the clamour of creditors: such a tumult arose that breath forgot the way to the lips, and sight the window of the eye; and the world, for all its brightness, grew dark and black in my sight. With lips sewn shut from speech and eyes closed to myself, I took with me worlds of brokenness and realms of weariness; and lamenting the injustice of the age, and rubbing my breast on the sword's edge, I reached Calcutta." [TN: Persian in the original.]

In short, the Mirza was somewhat under forty [TN: so printed; Ghalib was about thirty-one when he reached Calcutta in 1828] when he reached Calcutta by way of Lucknow. In Calcutta people received him with much kindness and attention and gave him hopes of success. Mr Stirling, Secretary to the Government of India, in whose praise a Persian *qasida* by the Mirza is found in his collected works, promised that you will certainly receive your due. Mr Colebrooke, who was then Resident at Delhi, had already promised the Mirza in Delhi itself to make a good report. Deceived by these hopes he stayed in Calcutta two full years; but in the end nothing came of it but failure. The government enquired about the Mirza's case of Sir John Malcolm, Governor of Bombay, who had been Lord Lake's secretary and in whose presence people had received the deeds of their *jagir*s and pensions. He declared the Mirza's claim to be wrong, and sent the government a detailed account of how and in what amount the pension from Firozpur had been settled, which was entirely contrary to the Mirza's claim. When the Mirza was disappointed here, he appealed to England, but nothing came of that either.

The Mirza had made five requests to the Government of India: first, that the amount of pension which in his view the government had fixed should be paid in full in future; second, that the arrears of the shortfall in the pension received until now should be paid by the State of Firozpur from the beginning to the present day. Since the first request was rejected (how could the second be granted?). The third request was that the share of the whole pension settled as his should be separated from the other sharers. The fourth, that the pension should be transferred from Firozpur to the government treasury, so that he should not have to ask the chief of Firozpur for it. (These two requests were granted, and they were acted upon to the end.) The fifth request was for a title and a robe of honour. So far as is known, the Mirza received no title from the government. But the Government of India and the local government used to address him as "Khan Sahib, most kind friend of friends"; and whenever a *darbar* (court assembly) of the Viceroy or the Lieutenant-Governor was held in Delhi, he too was invited like the other chiefs and notables of the city, and he regularly received a robe of honour of seven pieces with a plume (*jigha*), a turban ornament (*sarpech*) and a string of pearls, and all the local officials and officers treated him like a man of noble family.

# The Dispute with the People of Calcutta

During his stay in Calcutta some people had raised objections to the Mirza's verse and had cited the authority of Qatil in support of their objections. But among the Persian-writing poets of India the Mirza acknowledged no one except Khusrau. Thus he writes in a letter: "Among the people of India no one is of established authority except Khusrau of Delhi; Miyan Faizi too gets it right here and there." For this reason he thought nothing of Qatil, Waqif and the like. On hearing Qatil's name he turned up his nose and said that I do not accept the word of Diwali Singh[1], the Khatri of Faridabad, and consider no one's word authoritative except that of native speakers. And in support of his verse he cited the usage of native speakers. At this the objectors grew more heated and a shower of objections began to fall on the Mirza. Although the Mirza had many supporters in Calcutta too, since he was much vexed by objection and opposition, one objector was enough to unsettle him.

# The Masnavi "Bad-e Mukhalif"

Driven to the end of his patience, he wrote a *masnavi* (poem in rhyming couplets) named *Bad-e Mukhalif* ("The Contrary Wind"), in which he describes his exile, complains of the unkindness of the people of Calcutta, and sets out their objections and his own replies with great beauty, clarity and pathos. Some verses of this *masnavi* are quoted here from various places [TN: Persian verse; a few readings in the print are uncertain]:

O spectators of the assembly of poetry,
O rare masters with the breath of the Messiah!

O precious ones of the world of letters,
you who sit at ease on this wondrous carpet!

O cherishers of poetry of Calcutta,
O masters of language of Calcutta!

Each one the head of the assembly of a court,
the candle of the private chamber of some workshop;

each one the leader of a caravan,
each one the master of a stage on the road;

O you who are ready for the business of advocacy,
who have given the world its due of sympathy!

O wondrous ones of the world of justice,
come on embassies from every side!

O you who have given poetry the adornment of the soul,
and given the page the furnishing of a rose garden!

Scattering perfume on the brain of the world,
champions who know the Pahlavi tongue!

O precious masters who compose in *Rekhta*,
fine drinkers of oceans, seekers of brawls!

O chiefs of this great city,
gathered here from the seven climes!

Like me a resident of this city,
come to this city for some business,

Asadullah, of upturned fortune,
wandering in the coils and twists of helplessness,

though he is your uninvited guest,
gathers crumbs from your table without speech.

He has come here to seek redress;
he has rested here in hope.

Grant him rest for four days,
the weary one, in the shade of your wall.

To mend the affairs of friends is a virtue;
to cherish a guest is a virtue.

Where is that custom of helping on affairs?
Where is the way of hospitality?

Who am I? A broken-hearted, grief-stricken one,
a heartless, weary, oppressed one,

one whose soul the lightning of helplessness has struck,
whose house and home the fire of grief has burned,

in fever and torment from the melting of breath,
thirsty-lipped in the desert of despair,

a straw tossed in the storm on the ocean of calamity,
from head to foot the dust of the caravan of death,

a sufferer with a melted heart,
one who has lost his courage through the world's grief,

one whose awareness has been struck by annihilation,
who has kicked away his whole self.

What calamities I have endured at last,
that I have come to this place at last!

Look at me on the black day of my grief;
look at the dark nights of my desolation.

Look at the sorrow of distance from home;
look at the grief of separation from my company.

Not only lament and outcry together:
I and the Creator of the soul, for my soul is on my lips.

Weeping has made me like a hair;
grief has made me its habit.

Where have I a taste for verse and poetry?
When have I a tongue that sings poetry?

Yes, I have, from my idle babbling,
a dirge over myself and my own destitution.

I am my own turning of fortune;
I am the bewilderment of my own affairs.

This anger and hatred towards me! Alas, alas!
I like that, and you like this! Alas, alas!

How is oppression lawful upon strangers?
If there is no pity, why then oppression?

I am a servant, a servant of the kind,
of those who understand hints and know subtleties.

I do not fear the clash of argument;
I and my faith! what I fear is this:

that after me, for long years,
this story will remain on men's tongues:

that a fool had come here,
had rested here for a few days,

had begun a quarrel with the great,
had caused trouble and gone his way;

he was impudent and ill-natured,
shameless and a babbler;

his talk was foolish,
and he had the air of a tavern-haunter;

he had neither worldly goods nor the gear of religion;
he was a disgrace to Delhi and its land.

Ah for the moment when, after my going,
the blood of Delhi will be upon my neck!

As long as I am here I shall be a vexation to friends,
I shall be a burden on the heart of the assembly.

They will be glad when I leave their midst;
alas for me, that I should go thus!

I shall return weary and sorrowful;
I came gentle, I shall return dejected.

No one of you will come to bid me farewell;
no good news of faithfulness will reach my longing.

If my friends have a complaint against me,
that "your gait goes against the caravan;

all of us follow Qatil,
all of us have made the *fard* (single verse) our proof;

you, having broken out of this circle,
have set your foot upon another road":

O spectators of deep sight,
come, say, for the sake of God,

how shall I turn my head away from Hazin,
that prophet of magic with his enchanting breath?

Will my heart allow[2] me to turn away from Asir,
from that melodious song?

How shall I let go from my hand the skirt
of Talib and 'Urfi and Naziri,

and especially the very soul and spirit of meaning,
Zuhuri, that world of meaning?

He, by the loftiness of whose pen
the banner of his standard brushes the sky;

the manner of thought is his creation;
it is he who breathed a soul into the body of the word.

The back of meaning is strong through his side;
the pen gets its plumpness from his arm.

From him the manner of writing has its freshness;
from him the page has the fullness of Mani's gallery [TN: reading uncertain].

I am the captive of these men's speech;
I am drunk on the dregs of their wine jar.

One who has travelled through these stations,
what does he know of Qatil and Waqif?

But with all that I have this,
the treasure of meaning in my sleeve,

my heart and soul are a sacrifice for my friends;
my longing is devoted to my friends' pleasure.

I make myself a guide to peace;
I sing the song of Qatil's praise,

so that no complaint of me may remain,
and some reward may come from his followers [TN: reading uncertain].

To speak is not the way of prudence,
but knowing is not a matter of choice.

Though I will not call him an Iranian,
I will not call him a second Sa'di,

yet he is a thousand times better than I;
than I, and than a thousand like me, he is better.

I am a handful of dust, and he the lofty sky;
how can the dust's noose reach the heavens?

To praise him is not within the scope of one like me;
the sun does not fit a little window.

Bravo the instrument of his fine eloquence!
Splendid the passion of his subtlety!

His verse is like the water of life;
in its flow it is like the Euphrates.

His prose is the pattern on the peacock's wing,
a selection of the *Sihah* and the *Qamus* [TN: two celebrated Arabic dictionaries].

A king who in the realm of letters
has invented wondrous subtleties;

his pen is an Indian that knows Persian;
Indians bow their heads to his command.

These lines that the pen of imagination has poured out
were a line from the record of my deeds.

From me, helpless and knowing nothing,
it is a letter of apology to my friends.

Perhaps through our pleading for pardon
there may come pity on us and on our innocence.

A letter of reconciliation, a message of love:
it is ended. Peace, and all honour.

# Notes

[1] Hali's note: Mirza Qatil was a convert to Islam; before embracing Islam his name was Diwali Singh, and he was a Khatri of Faridabad in the district of Delhi. After becoming a Muslim he went to Lucknow, where he was greatly honoured.

[2] Hali's note: That is, "Does my heart permit me to turn away from Asir?" It is the idiom of native speakers to say *dil dihad* instead of *dil dasturi dihad*.
