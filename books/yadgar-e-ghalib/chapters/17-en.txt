# The Great Number of His Pupils

The Mirza's pupils throughout India were countless. His breadth of courtesy and his general wish to please everyone had made this circle very wide. It was impossible that whoever sent him a *ghazal* for correction should not receive a reply to his letter and his *ghazal* corrected. Although the Mirza's nature in poetry was so far above the people of his own class that he could not take any pupil or follower along with him, as he himself has hinted at this theme in a Persian verse; he says:

We are a *huma* (bird of fortune) of swift flight; seek no bounty from us:
the shadow, like smoke, goes up from our wings.

In spite of this, among the people of Delhi and its environs, some gentlemen who had profited most from the Mirza's company and his counsel on verse were considered his foremost pupils, such as Nayyar Rakhshan, 'Arif, Salik, Majruh, 'Ala'i, Tafta and others. Besides them there were also some people among the Delhi elite who were not commonly considered the Mirza's pupils, but were in truth his pupils in spirit: such as the late Nawab Mustafa Khan, who after the death of the late Momin Khan always showed his verse, Persian or Urdu, to the Mirza alone; or such as the late Sayyid Ghulam 'Ali Khan[1], whose pen name was Wahshat, who was the Mirza's devoted admirer and follower beyond all bounds and had profited from his company. The Mirza has alluded to these two gentlemen in the closing couplet (*maqta'*) of one of his Urdu *ghazal*s, and said:

Wahshat and Shefta will perhaps write an elegy now;
they say distracted-voiced Ghalib is dead.

# Anecdote

These two gentlemen had the deepest friendship with each other, so much so that their friendship had reached the degree of love. Once, when the present writer too was at Jahangirabad, the late Sayyid Ghulam 'Ali Khan had come to see the Nawab, and the Mirza had heard of his coming there. In those days a letter came from the Mirza addressed to the Nawab; in it he had also sent greetings to Khan Sahib, and at the end had written the first half-line of Khwaja Hafiz's famous verse altered thus:

"When you sit with your beloved and measure out tea[2],
remember the companions who measure out wine."

On hearing this witticism a friend said that there was not as much charm in Khwaja Hafiz's original verse as had come into it on this occasion through the Mirza's alteration.

It is not necessary to write here an account of those pupils of the Mirza's to whom many letters written by the Mirza are found in *Urdu-e Mu'alla* and *'Ud-e Hindi*; so we give here only a short account of two gentlemen, one the late Nawab Ziya-ud-Din Ahmad Khan and the other the late Nawab Mustafa Khan; for probably there is no letter addressed to either of these two gentlemen in the Mirza's Urdu correspondence from which their special relation with the Mirza would be known to the public.

# Nawab Ziya-ud-Din Ahmad Khan

Nawab Ziya-ud-Din Ahmad Khan, whose pen name in Persian was Nayyar and in Urdu Rakhshan, apart from his excellence in poetry and prose, had no equal in history, geography, genealogy, the biography of men (*asma'-ur-rijal*), the investigation of words, and "general information" (general knowledge). Although he did not leave any independent work under his own name in these arts, many authors sought his help and consulted him in whatever difficulty arose; in particular, in the composition and arrangement of the history of India in several volumes that Mr Elliot wrote [TN: Sir H. M. Elliot, *The History of India as Told by Its Own Historians*], the said Nawab gave very great help, which the author himself acknowledged in the preface to his book.

Since the said Nawab was a lover of men of accomplishment, and especially since his own first cousin was married to the Mirza, he had a special connection with the Mirza. He composed verse in both Persian and Urdu, but mostly wrote Persian verse and prose and followed in the Mirza's footsteps. Some verses, picked from various places, of a most eloquent and delicate *qasida* which the Mirza wrote in praise of the said Nawab, and in which he takes pride in being his teacher, are given here [TN: Persian verse; the print is calligraphic and several readings are moderately confident]:

A hundred suns could be made in play
from a mote that is in the light of my Nayyar (luminary).

This is not this sky nor this sun; it is another world:
I am the sky, and he is my light-spreading sun.

I am that sky which knows that, as the sun to the moon,
my shining Nayyar gives light to the sun.

I am that sky to whose greater fortune (Jupiter)
the gift of bounty comes every moment from my lesser fortune (Venus).

I am the treasury of secrets, and in the treasury [TN: reading uncertain]
is Ziya-ud-Din Muhammad, my younger brother;

in faith, knowledge and fortune unique in the world,
younger in years and, in rank, my elder.

With heart's love I give my heart to a brother; I am not Jacob,
for whom his own son was the heart-ravisher and beloved.

I take pride in the singer of new melodies [TN: reading moderately confident],
my companion in lament, my equal in passion.

In subtlety the manner of my pupil resembles me:
my Azar carves the idol in his own likeness [TN: Azar, the idol-maker, father of Abraham in Muslim tradition].

Though he is Aristotle and I am Plato,
in rank my Aristotle is my Alexander.

Every morning the rising of the bright Nayyar
from the side of my outlook makes the ground of my lane a sky.

If, for instance, I become a fire scattering sparks,
by the rule of companionship he becomes my salamander.

If my road falls into the sea, he is my ship;
if I have a mind for the throne, he becomes my crown [TN: reading moderately confident].

In love of a friend I give my heart: he is the joy of my mind;
in hatred of an enemy I set my face: he is the banner of my army.

If my affairs are ruined by grief, he is my comforter;
and if my hand fails in work, he is my helper.

Bravo! From your face the radiance of knowledge and justice is manifest;
by this radiance my star has become world-illuminating.

From you, who are the mirror of the bounty of his company,
the longing to see Ghalib has fallen into my head.

You praised me[3] and said, "I am yours";
may the least and the most of me be a sacrifice for you.

Happiness and honour for one like me would come from excellence;
is it not enough that one like you should be my praiser? [TN: this and the previous couplet are spoken in the Nawab's voice; reading moderately confident.]

# Nawab Muhammad Mustafa Khan

The late Nawab Muhammad Mustafa Khan, whose pen name in Persian was Hasrati and in Urdu Shefta, was not counted among the Mirza's pupils; rather, as long as the late Momin Khan lived, he consulted him about his verse. But after the death of the said Khan he regularly showed his verse in both *Rekhta* and Persian to the Mirza; and, if our judgement is not mistaken, after the Mirza no contemporary's Persian *ghazal* could stand beside his *ghazal*; and such true taste for poetry as had been placed in his nature has very seldom been seen. People regarded his taste as the standard of the beauty and ugliness of verse. At his silence a poet's verse would fall in the poet's own eyes, and at his praise its value would rise. This was the man of whom Mirza Ghalib says:

Ghalib prides himself on the art of speech with this worth, that he
did not write a *ghazal* in his *diwan* until Mustafa Khan approved it[4].

In the said Nawab's praise too there is a Persian *qasida* of the Mirza's in his *diwan*, in which he first wrote a boastful prelude; in the course of the boastful verses he says [TN: Persian verse; several readings are uncertain]:

I lay the hand of rejection on the crown of Caesar;
I kick at the throne of the Khaqan.

The angels find fault with me
if I breathe a word in praise of a sultan.

I am that swift-flying *huma* that beats its wings
in longing for Mustafa Khan.

'Urfi and Khaqani are under his command [TN: reading uncertain];
I strike my coin in Shiraz and Shirvan.

He strides along drunk, and I, like a herald,
shout at the stars and the elements.

The garden of his lane is my thoroughfare;
in passing, I jostle shoulders with Rizwan [TN: the keeper of Paradise].

His own beauty is my bad teacher;
I boast of friendship; yes, I boast.

See the kindness, that I should be his companion,
I who kneel before his doorkeeper!

Hear for a moment [TN: reading uncertain] what the wind carries off,
if I raise a lament in the corner of the prison;

see for a moment what the pen draws,
if I trace a figure on the page of the soul.

# Notes

[1] Hali's note: This gentleman was among the distinguished people of Delhi. His father's name was Sayyid Farhatullah Khan, and he was the son-in-law of the late Maulana Rashid-ud-Din Khan. Apart from learning and scholarship, he was very eloquent in both Urdu and Persian and had a taste for poetry of the highest order. At first he was in the service of the English government, then he became *faujdar* (police and military officer) at Alwar, then in Lucknow, through the late Nawab Sa'id-ud-Daula [TN: reading uncertain], the deputy minister, to whom he was closely related, he was appointed to an honourable post; from there he came back to Alwar and remained there until the Mutiny. After the Mutiny he was attached to the Education Department to the end of his life; and the late Nawab Mustafa Khan helped him in every circumstance.

[2] Hali's note: Since the Nawab abstained from wine and the Mirza drank, he used the expression "measuring out tea" of him and "measuring out wine" of himself.

[3] Hali's note: It appears that the Nawab had written some *qasida* in praise of the Mirza, to which these two couplets allude.

[4] Gloss printed above the word in the original: that is, did not approve.
