# Title and Office

In 1266 of the Hijra [TN: 1850] the late Abu Zafar Siraj-ud-Din Bahadur Shah conferred on the Mirza in open court (*darbar-e 'amm*) the title Najm-ud-Daula Dabir-ul-Mulk Nizam Jang, and a robe of honour of six pieces with three jewelled items, namely a plume, a turban ornament and a pearl sash, and appointed him to the office of writing the history of the house of Timur at a salary of fifty rupees a month. And it was settled that the late Ihtiram-ud-Daula Hakim Ahsanullah Khan should gather material from various histories and hand it over to the Mirza, and that the Mirza should set out all this matter in Persian prose in his own particular style. The book was to be divided into two parts. In the first part a short account was to be given from the beginning of creation to Sahib-qiran Timur Gurgan, and a somewhat detailed account from Timur to the last days of Nasir-ud-Din Humayun. In the second part all the events from the Emperor Jalal-ud-Din Akbar to the time of Siraj-ud-Din Bahadur Shah were to be recorded at full length. The Mirza had proposed the name *Partavistan* for the whole book, *Mihr-e Nimroz* ("The Midday Sun") for its first part, and *Mah-e Nim-mah* ("The Mid-month Moon") for its second part. He was proud of two expressions of his: one *Mah-e Nim-mah*, and the other *Rastkhez-e Beja*[1]. The Mirza used to say that earlier poets have often used *mah-e chardah* (the moon of the fourteenth) and *mah-e do hafta* (the moon of two weeks) for the moon of the fourteenth night; but, so far as I know, no one has used *mah-e nim-mah*. This expression is my own coinage. But it is a pity that the second part, that is *Mah-e Nim-mah*, was never written at all. After finishing *Mihr-e Nimroz* the Mirza had paused for a few days to take a little rest, and intended to begin the second part soon, when the Mutiny broke out, and of this part only the name remained.

A gentleman from Hyderabad had asked the Mirza for *Mah-e Nim-mah*; in reply he writes: "*Mah-e Nim-mah* is a name that has nothing named by it. Since one cannot turn one's neck from what is written on one's brow, I will tell the story again. When one half of *Partavistan* was completed and was named *Mihr-e Nimroz*, there was a little delay so that breath might be drawn. Suddenly the day of the one who had commissioned it set, and his time came to an end; and the ancient empire of the Qarachar Turkmans [TN: the Timurids] passed away. *Mah-e Nim-mah*, like the moon of the twenty-eighth night, became invisible, and its name remained manifest in *Mihr-e Nimroz* only as something without a trace." [TN: Persian in the original.]

# Correcting the King's Verses

In 1271 of the Hijra [TN: 1854 to 1855], when Shaikh Ibrahim Zauq died, the correction of the King's verses was also entrusted to the Mirza. But it appears that the Mirza did this work unwillingly. The late Nazir Husain Mirza used to say that one day the Mirza and I were sitting in the Diwan-e 'Amm (Hall of Public Audience) when a mace-bearer (*chobdar*) came and said that His Majesty has asked for the *ghazal*s. The Mirza said, "Wait a little," and told his servant, "There are some papers tied in a handkerchief in the palanquin; bring them." He brought them at once. When the Mirza opened it he took out eight or nine slips, on each of which one or two half-lines (*misra*s) were written. And having sent for ink and pen then and there, he began writing *ghazal*s on those half-lines; and sitting right there he wrote eight or nine *ghazal*s complete and handed them to the mace-bearer. The late Nazir used to say that writing all these *ghazal*s took him no longer than it would take a practised master to put a few *ghazal*s right by correcting them here and there. When the mace-bearer had gone with the *ghazal*s, he said to me: "Today, after a long time, I am relieved of His Majesty's occasional demands." Although whatever the Mirza wrote in his own particular style, verse or prose, he produced with great effort and toil, as he himself has stated plainly here and there, yet whenever there was no need to follow his own particular manner he did not have to put much strain on his thought.

# Improvisation

In 1861 [TN: year printed small and blotted; reading uncertain], when the late Nawab Ziya-ud-Din Ahmad Khan had gone to Calcutta, the late Maulvi Muhammad 'Alim, an aged scholar of Calcutta, told the Nawab that at the time when the Mirza had come here, at a gathering where the Mirza was present, and I too was there, poets were being discussed. In the course of the conversation a gentleman praised Faizi highly. The Mirza said, "Faizi is not what people take him to be." At this the talk grew warm. That man said that when Faizi first went before Akbar, he composed and recited on the spot an extempore *qasida* of two hundred and fifty couplets. The Mirza said, "Even now there are servants of God who can compose extempore, if not two or four hundred, then two or four couplets on any occasion." The man took a *chikni dali* (a boiled betel nut) from his pocket, put it on his palm, and asked the Mirza to say something on that nut. The Mirza at once composed and recited a *qit'a* of eleven couplets, which is in his Urdu *diwan*, and of which the first couplet is this:

This *chikni dali* that is on the palm of the Sahib's hand,
it suits it, however good a thing you call it.

# Children

The Mirza had no children. At first seven children were born one after another, but none lived. So for a long time he and his wife lived alone. But a few years before the Mutiny, when his wife's nephew Zain-ul-'Abidin Khan 'Arif died and his two children, Baqir 'Ali Khan and Husain 'Ali Khan, were left young, the Mirza and his wife took the younger boy, Husain 'Ali Khan, who was then very small, under the shelter of their affection. The Mirza held Husain 'Ali Khan dearer than even a child of his own would have been, never let him out of his sight, and indulged him beyond all bounds.

When Zain-ul-'Abidin Khan's mother died, the Mirza took Husain 'Ali Khan's elder brother Baqir 'Ali Khan too under his guardianship. Both of them were of fine poetic thought, capable [TN: reading uncertain], good-natured and of most gentle disposition.

Alas, after the Mirza's death both died young, one shortly after the other.

# 'Arif

The Mirza was attached in the highest degree to Zain-ul-'Abidin Khan 'Arif: partly because of kinship, and more because he had a nature of fine thought and quick to find meanings, and, though he wrote much, he wrote very well. He held him dear beyond all bounds. So when he died young it was a heavy blow to the Mirza and his wife. On his death the Mirza wrote a *ghazal* in the manner of a lament (*nauha*), which is most eloquent and moving; a few of its couplets we quote here:

You ought to have waited for me a few more days;
why did you go alone? Now stay alone a few more days.

You came yesterday, and today you say "I am going";
granted, not every day is as good as today, but a few more days.

Going, you say, "We shall meet on the Day of Resurrection";
how fine! as if Resurrection were some other day.

Yes, O aged heaven! 'Arif was still young;
what would you have lost if he had not died for a few more days?

You were the full moon of the fourteenth night of my house;
why then did the house not keep that look a few more days?

You were not so very honest in your give and take;
the Angel of Death would have pressed his claim a few more days.

Granted you hated me and quarrelled with Nayyar,
you did not even watch the children's play a few more days.

This span passed, in any case, in joy and sorrow;
you who died young, you should have gone on a few more days.

You are foolish to say, "Why do you live on, Ghalib?"
It is in my fate to long for death a few more days.
