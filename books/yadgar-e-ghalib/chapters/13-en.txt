# Certainty of Islam

The Mirza had a very firm belief in the truth of Islam, and held the unity of being (*tauhid-e wujudi*) to be the fundamental principle and chief pillar of Islam. Although outwardly he was not one of the people of spiritual states (*ahl-e hal*), yet, as it has been said, "Whoever loves a thing mentions it often," the unity of being had become an element of his poetry. This theme, in the abstraction [TN: reading uncertain] and the variety of forms of verse in which he expressed it, has probably not been expressed by anyone after Naziri and Bedil. If nothing else, this verse of 'Urfi's certainly holds true of the Mirza:

There is hope that the strangeness of 'Urfi
will be forgiven for the sake of his familiar words.

Of all the acts of worship, obligations and duties, he had taken only two things: one the unity of being, and the other love for the Prophet and the Prophet's Family (*Ahl-e Bait*); and these he considered his means of salvation.

Although a poet's beliefs cannot be inferred from his verse, what comes from the heart does not stay hidden. It appears that, just as many philosophers of Islam have denied the physical delights of Paradise, the Mirza too did not believe in them; for he expressed this idea in his poetic manner in several places. In one place he says:

We know the truth about Paradise, but
to keep the heart happy, Ghalib, it is a pleasant thought.

The same idea he expressed thus in a Persian *ruba'i*:

# Ruba'i

The ascetics' bold gazing about in Paradise [TN: reading uncertain],
and that stretching out of hands for fruit from branch to branch,

when you look closely, by way of comparison,
is like cattle in a wide pasture.

Although the Mirza observed the outward ordinances very little, whenever he heard of anything that brought humiliation on the Muslims he was deeply grieved. One day in my presence he was lamenting greatly over an incident of this kind, and said: "There is nothing of the Muslim in me; and yet I do not know why I feel such grief and sorrow at the humiliation of the Muslims." But since his nature was very playful, when a sharp remark occurred to him he could not keep from saying it, whether anyone took him for an infidel, called him a libertine, or thought him irreligious.

# Anecdote

After the Mutiny, when his pension was stopped and he had not been permitted to attend the *darbar*, Pandit Moti Lal, Mir Munshi to the Lieutenant-Governor of the Punjab, came to see the Mirza. There was some mention of the pension. The Mirza said: "If in my whole life there has been one day on which I did not drink wine, call me an infidel; and if I have once said the prayer, call me a sinner. Then I do not know how the government counted me among the rebel Muslims."

# Religion

Although the Mirza's real creed was peace with all (*sulh-e kull*), his natural inclination was found to lean mostly towards Shi'ism, and he held His Holiness the Amir [TN: 'Ali] to be superior to the whole community after the Messenger of God.

Once the late Bahadur Shah said in court, "We have heard that Mirza Asadullah Khan Ghalib is a Shi'a by creed." The Mirza came to know of it. He wrote some *ruba'i*s and recited them to His Majesty, in which he disclaimed Shi'ism and *rafz* (rejection of the first caliphs). One of them, which is very witty, has stayed in my memory, and is written here:

# Ruba'i

Those people who bear me deep enmity
call me a *Rafizi* and a materialist (*dahri*).

How can one be a materialist who is a Sufi?
How can a man from beyond the Oxus be a Shi'a?

The vast difference between materialism and Sufism is plain: the materialist does not believe in the existence of God at all, while the Sufi holds that God alone exists and considers everything else nothing; so how can a Sufi be a materialist? The meaning of the fourth half-line is that the people of Transoxiana, that is Turkistan, are proverbial for being bigoted Sunnis, so much so that the Shi'as consider them *Nasibi*s and Kharijites. Since the Mirza's origin was from Transoxiana, he says: how can a man from beyond the Oxus be a *Rafizi* or a Shi'a?

Those who are unacquainted with the Mirza's temperament and manner of speech may perhaps think that the Mirza misrepresented his religion in order to keep his influence with the King; but the real truth is that all the *ruba'i*s were written only to please the King and amuse the courtiers; for there was not a soul at court who did not know the Mirza to be a Shi'a, or at least a *Tafzili* (one who holds 'Ali superior to the other caliphs). The Mirza often recited verses of this kind at court on various occasions to please the King. One day the special bond between Sultan Nizam-ud-Din, may his secret be sanctified, and Amir Khusrau was being discussed at court; the Mirza at once composed and recited this verse:

By the power of God two spiritual guides have found two disciples:
Nizam-ud-Din has Khusrau, Siraj-ud-Din has Ghalib.

# Anecdote

It was the month of Ramazan; a Sunni maulvi came to see the Mirza. It was the time of the afternoon prayer. The Mirza asked his servant for water. The maulvi said in surprise, "Is Your Honour not fasting?" The Mirza said, "I am a Sunni Muslim; I break my fast when four *ghari*s of the day remain." [TN: that is, about an hour and a half before sunset, the proper time.]

Once Bahadur Shah fell very seriously ill. At that time Mirza Haidar Shikoh, nephew of Akbar Shah and son of Mirza Sulaiman Shikoh, had also come from Lucknow and was the King's guest. His creed was Ithna 'Ashari (Twelver Shi'a). When the King got no relief by any means, on Mirza Haidar Shikoh's advice *khak-e shifa* (healing earth from Karbala) was given to him, and after that the King recovered. Mirza Haidar Shikoh had vowed that if the King recovered he would offer a standard (*'alam*) at the shrine of Hazrat 'Abbas in Lucknow. So on going to Lucknow he sent the King a petition saying that he had not the means to fulfil the vow, and asked His Majesty to help. From here the King sent Mirza Haidar Shikoh some money, and he offered the standard with great pomp, in which the whole royal family of Oudh and all the nobles and *'ulama* took part; and the standard was offered by the hand of the *Mujtahid-ul-'Asr* (the chief Shi'a divine of the age).

After this event it became widely rumoured that the King had become a Shi'a. The King was much grieved at this rumour; and to counter it the late Hakim Ahsanullah Khan had some treatises published, and many notices were posted in the lanes and bazaars, and by the King's order the Mirza too wrote a *masnavi* in Persian, which was probably named *Damgh-ul-Batil* [TN: reading of the title uncertain], and in which the King was cleared of the charge of Shi'ism. In this *masnavi* the Mirza wrote nothing of his own, but put into Persian verse the themes that Hakim Ahsanullah Khan had given him.

When this *masnavi* reached Lucknow, the *Mujtahid-ul-'Asr* asked the Mirza whether he had himself written such and such things in this *masnavi* about the Shi'a creed and about Mirza Haidar Shikoh. The Mirza wrote in reply: "I am in the royal service; whatever the King commands, I carry out. Please consider the substance of this *masnavi* to be from the King and Hakim Ahsanullah Khan, and only the words from me."

# Soundness of Nature

The Mirza's nature was very sound. Although sharpness of mind and soundness of nature are very seldom found together, both were present in the Mirza in the fullest measure. It was the effect of this soundness of nature that the crooked road he had taken at the beginning of his practice of poetry he gradually left, without any master to guide him, as his reason and discernment grew, until at last he came onto the straight path of the acknowledged masters.

The Mirza, out of humility and modesty, used to say that in the prelude (*tashbib*) of *qasida*s I too reach, stumbling and rising, the point that 'Urfi and Anwari reach; but in praise and eulogy I cannot keep up with them. The Mirza's saying appears quite true; for the force found in his preludes does not remain when he comes to the praise. But we do not take this as a defect in his poetry; rather we consider it a proof of the utmost soundness of mind and straightness of nature. To polish false and baseless things, to join the hooks of earth and heaven, and to raise a storm of exaggeration and hyperbole is in truth not the excellence of a poet; rather, the more his nature refuses these things, the more he should be recognised as having an affinity with poetry. It is another matter that the Mirza's whole life was spent in writing *qasida*s and singing praises, for necessity makes a man do everything. But in truth, as we shall explain later, he did not know how to flatter as one should.

At this point we record an incident from which the Mirza's soundness of nature can be judged to some extent.

# The Question of the Impossibility of an Equal to the Seal of the Prophets

The late Maulana Fazl-e Haq was a very close friend of the Mirza's and held him to be a most authoritative poet of the Persian language. Since the Maulana was strongly opposed to the Wahhabis, he pressed the Mirza most insistently to write a *masnavi* in Persian against the Wahhabis, refuting their chief and best-known beliefs, and in particular setting out at length the question of *imtina'-e nazir-e khatam-un-nabiyyin*, the impossibility of an equal to the Seal of the Prophets. On this question the view of Maulana Isma'il Shahid was that an equal to the Seal of the Prophets is possible in itself and impossible by reason of something else (*mumtani' bil-ghair*), not impossible in itself (*mumtani' biz-zat*); that is, an equal to the Prophet cannot be created because his creation would be contrary to the Prophet's being the Seal, not because God has no power to create him. Contrary to this, Maulana Fazl-e Haq's view was that an equal to the Seal of the Prophets is impossible in itself; and that just as God cannot create His own equal, so He cannot create an equal to the Seal of the Prophets.

The Mirza was asked to set out Maulana Fazl-e Haq's view on this question in Persian verse. The Mirza at first excused himself, saying that it is difficult to express learned questions in verse; but he would not accept this. So the Mirza wrote a *masnavi*, which is the sixth in the series of *masnavi*s in his collected works, and recited it to the Maulana. He praised it beyond measure and said that even if I were as practised as you in Persian verse I could not have expressed these matters so well. But at what the Mirza had written on the question of an equal to the Seal of the Prophets, somewhat against the Maulana's view, the Maulana was very angry. The Mirza had not written plainly that God has power to create an equal to the Seal of the Prophets; but he had expressed the theme in this form: in this present world, indeed, no second Seal can be created besides the one Seal; but God has power to create another world like this, and to create in it an equal of the Seal of the Prophets, who would be the Seal of the Prophets of that other world. So he put this theme into verse thus:

As long as there is one world, one Seal is enough;
for the power of God one world is not enough.

If He wills, He brings a world out of every atom,
and every world will have a Seal as well.

Wherever there is the bustle of a world,
there will be a Mercy to the Worlds as well.

Is the multiplicity of the creation of worlds better,
or two Seals in one world better?

Do not seek two Seals in one world;
speak of a hundred thousand worlds and Seals.

When the Mirza first brought the *masnavi* written out to the Maulana, he had ended the said theme on this last verse. The Maulana said: "What nonsense is this you have uttered, that in several worlds there can be several Seals? No; even if God creates a hundred thousand worlds, the Seal of the Prophets will be one only. So take this theme right out of the *masnavi*, and state it as I tell you." The Mirza had neither any enmity towards the Wahhabis nor any connection with their opponents; he only wished to please a friend. He carried out the Maulana's command at once. What he had already written he left as it was, but added some more verses after it and connected the discourse thus:

Ghalib, I do not accept this thought;
I find fault with myself as well.

O you who have called him the Seal of the Messengers,
I know that you have called him so with certainty.

This *alif-lam* [TN: the Arabic definite article in *al-mursalin*, "the Messengers"], which is for comprehensiveness,
is a clear ruling for the sense of absoluteness.

The source of the creation of every world is one;
if there were two hundred worlds, the Seal is one.

After this he has expanded the same theme further, and then ended the *masnavi* on these two verses, in which it is stated explicitly that an equal to the Seal of the Prophets is impossible in itself:

He is unique in essential perfection;
so of course his equal is essentially impossible.

I will not turn from this belief; and peace!
I fold up the letter; and peace!

From the above account readers will have learned how much straightness there was in the Mirza's nature, and how much his mind refused crookedness. Although Maulana Fazl-e Haq had thoroughly impressed on the Mirza's mind his own view on this question, and the Mirza wished to state that very view in his *masnavi*, yet, just as a crooked thing becomes straight on entering a tube, so the Mirza's truthfulness of expression took all the twists out of that crooked opinion [TN: Hali's own judgement], and without the Mirza wishing to support the Wahhabis, what was the correct view dropped from his pen in spite of himself. What he wrote after that was written under the Maulana's compulsion, and has nothing to do with the Mirza's real thoughts.
