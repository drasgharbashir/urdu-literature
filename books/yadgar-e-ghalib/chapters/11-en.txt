# The Mirza's Character, Habits and Opinions

# Courtesy

The Mirza's courtesy was very wide. He met everyone who went to see him with a very open brow. Whoever had once met him always longed to meet him again. On seeing his friends he would be overjoyed, and he was glad in their gladness and grieved in their grief. For this reason his friends of every community and religion were countless, not only in Delhi but in the whole of India. From every letter of the letters he wrote to his friends, affection, love, sympathy and intimacy fairly drip. He considered it his bounden duty to answer every letter. Much of his time was spent writing replies to his friends' letters. Even in illness and distress he did not refrain from answering letters. He was never vexed by his friends' requests. Besides the correction of *ghazal*s, some of his true and sincere friends made requests of all kinds, and he carried them out. People often sent him letters unstamped (*bairang*), but he never minded it. If anyone sent a stamp in the envelope he complained bitterly. He sent one of his books to a prince of Mysore; the prince wrote acknowledging the book and asked its price. In reply he writes: "Why did a word asking the amount of the price come upon the tongue of your pen? This is not the way to favour humble, destitute men. I am without capital, not base; I am a poet, not a merchant; I wear the coarse woollen garment [TN: *mu'ina-push*, reading uncertain], I am not a bookseller; I am a receiver of gifts, not a taker of prices. Whatever the free send to princes is an offering; and whatever princes bestow on the free is a blessing. There is no buying and selling; there is no why and wherefore. Whatever I have sent is a present, and whatever I shall send will be a present." [TN: Persian in the original.]

# Kindliness

Kindliness and consideration were in the Mirza's nature to the highest degree. Although at the end of his life he had come to dread correcting verses, he never returned anyone's *qasida* or *ghazal* without correction.

# Anecdote

He writes to a gentleman: "As far as I could, I performed the service of my friends. I used to look over sheets of verses lying down and correct them. Now I can neither see well with my eyes, nor write well with my hand. They say that God excused Shah Sharaf Bu 'Ali Qalandar, because of his great age, from the obligatory prayers, and the Prophet from the customary ones. I hope that my friends too will excuse me from the service of correcting verses. Letters of affection I will answer in whatever way I can."

# Anecdote

Even so people went on troubling the Mirza. Once Mirza Tafta happened to write that you had agreed to correct verses because of your love of poetry. In reply he writes: "There is no power nor strength save in God! What accursed one agreed to correct verses because of a love of poetry? If I am not sick of poetry, may my God be sick of me. I wrote, as the saying goes, 'the dervish's wrath falls on the dervish's own life'; just as a good wife chooses to live and die with a bad husband, that is my case with you."

# Largeness of Heart

Although the Mirza's income was small, his spirit was large. A beggar seldom went empty-handed from his door. In front of his house blind, lame, crippled and disabled men and women lay at all hours. After the Mutiny his income had become somewhat over a hundred and fifty rupees a month, and his expenses on food and drink were not at all great; but he helped the poor and needy beyond his means, and so was often in straits.

After the Mutiny I once saw for myself that at the *darbar* of His Honour the Lieutenant-Governor he received as usual a robe of honour of seven pieces with three jewelled items. The orderlies (*chaprasi*s) and *jama'dar*s of the Lieutenant-Governor's office came, according to custom, to receive their tips. The Mirza had known beforehand that he would have to give tips, so as soon as he came back from the *darbar* he had sent the robe and the jewelled items to the bazaar to be sold. He seated the orderlies in a separate room, and when the price of the robe came from the bazaar he gave them their tips and sent them off.

He treated those of his friends who had been ruined by the turns of fortune in a most gentlemanly way. One of the notables of Delhi, a close friend of the Mirza's whose circumstances had become wretched after the Mutiny, came one day to see the Mirza wearing a *farghul* (a loose overcoat) of printed chintz. The Mirza had never seen him wearing so mean a cloth, but only coats (*chogha*s) of *maleeda* (fine wool), *jamawar* (figured shawl) and the like. Seeing the chintz *farghul* on him his heart filled. He asked him, "Where did you get this chintz? I like its pattern very much; get me some of this chintz too for a *farghul*." He said, "This *farghul* was made up and came only today, and I have put it on just now; if you like it, here it is." The Mirza said, "I would like to snatch it from you this very moment and put it on, but the cold is severe; what would you wear from here to your house?" Then, looking this way and that, he took down from the peg his own new coat of *maleeda*, put it on him, and in this graceful way made him a present of that coat.

He writes in a letter: "Of the impulses towards the *qalandar*'s life, freedom, self-sacrifice and generosity that my Creator filled me with, not one in a thousand came to light. Neither had I the bodily strength to take a staff in my hand, hang from it a cotton rug and a tin water pot with a cotton cord, and set out on foot: now turning up in Shiraz, now stopping in Egypt, now reaching Najaf; nor had I the means to become the host of a whole world. If it cannot be done in the whole world, so be it; at least in the city where I live, no one should be seen hungry and naked. Smitten by God, rejected by the people, old, feeble, sick, a beggar, caught in misfortune. Leave aside my other affairs of speech and accomplishment; the man who cannot bear to see anyone begging, and himself begs from door to door, that is I."

# Memory

Just as the Mirza's nature had quickness of perception and his mind sharpness and rapidity of comprehension, so his memory too was very strong. We have written above that there was no trace of a book anywhere in his house; he always had books brought on hire, and sent them back after looking at them. But whatever fine or useful thing caught his eye in a book was imprinted on his heart. In Persian verse he used no word, idiom or construction for which he could not give authority from the verse of native speakers. To those people in Calcutta who had objected to his verse, and in reply to whom the Mirza wrote the *masnavi* *Bad-e Mukhalif*, besides the *masnavi* he wrote and sent separately, in answer to each objection, ten or twelve authorities from the verse of the masters; he has described them in detail in his letters. What he wrote on *Burhan-e Qati'* he wrote relying only on his memory. His way of composing verse was that he often composed at night in a state of mild intoxication; and when a couplet was completed he would tie a knot in his waistband. In this way he would tie eight or ten knots and go to sleep; and the next day, thinking it over from memory alone, he would write down all the couplets.

# Understanding of Poetry

In understanding poetry and understanding books he was an exceptional man. However difficult a theme might be, he would often reach the bottom of it at a cursory glance. The late Nawab Mustafa Khan writes of the Mirza in *Gulshan-e Bekhar*: "He understands poetic themes exactly as they deserve, and grasps all their subtleties and delicacies; and this is an excellence peculiar to the elect among men of poetry. If you have a nature that knows poetry you will reach this point; for though a fine thinker is rare, a fine understander is rarer. Happy the man who has had a draught of both and carried off his share. In short, so subtle and fine-spoken a man has seldom been seen." [TN: Persian in the original.]

# Judgement of Poetry

The said Nawab told me of an incident which gives great proof of the Mirza's judgement of poetry. Maulana Azurda had written a *ghazal* in the pattern (*zamin*) of *dur nahin*, *hur nahin*. In it, as it happened, the opening couplet (*matla'*) had turned out very well. The Maulana recited his *ghazal* to his friends and said to them: "Although the metre is different, Naziri too has a *ghazal* with this same refrain and rhyme, of which the opening couplet is this: 'Love is a sin if it is not hidden; the one slain by the crime of the tongue is not forgiven.' Clearly, if Naziri had been of Indian birth and had written an Urdu *ghazal* in the pattern in which our *ghazal* is, his opening couplet would have been thus: '*Ishq 'isyan hai agar makhfi o mastur nahin; kushta-e jurm-e zaban naji o maghfur nahin*' (Love is a sin if it is not secret and hidden; the one slain by the crime of the tongue is not saved or forgiven). Come, let us go to Mirza Ghalib's today, and without naming the author recite to the Mirza our opening couplet and this Urdu translation of Naziri's opening couplet (given above), and ask him which opening couplet is better." Since Naziri's opening couplet had become very flat in the Urdu translation, everyone was sure that the Mirza would dislike Naziri's couplet and prefer Maulana Azurda's. So the Maulana, the Nawab and some other friends went to the Mirza's. After ordinary conversation the Maulana said: "There are two Urdu opening couplets; judge between them which is better." And without naming the authors [TN: *ba-taur-e mubham*, reading uncertain] he first recited this translation of Naziri's opening couplet. The Maulana had not yet had a chance to recite his own couplet when the Mirza, hearing this one, began to sway his head in delight, and in amazement asked, "Who wrote this opening couplet?" And he praised it so highly that Maulana Azurda had no hope left of receiving more applause than this for his own couplet. So he did not recite his opening couplet, and everyone rose from there in great astonishment.

# Understanding of Books

The Mirza often read books on spiritual truths and mystic knowledge, and understood them well. The said Nawab used to say that I was reading a Persian treatise of Shah Waliullah, which dealt with the most subtle questions of spiritual truth and mystic knowledge, and I could not understand one passage at all. As it happened the Mirza came in at that very moment. I showed the passage to the Mirza. After some thought he explained its meaning with such excellence and clarity that perhaps even Shah Sahib himself could not have explained it better.

# Eloquence and Wit

The Mirza's conversation had no less charm than his writing and his verse and prose, and for this reason people were always eager to meet him and hear him talk. He was not a great talker; but whatever came from his tongue was not without charm. There was so much wit in his temperament that if he were called a "witty animal" instead of a "speaking animal" it would be right. Eloquence, ready repartee, and drawing one remark out of another were among his special qualities.

# Anecdote

Once, when Ramazan was over, he went to the Fort. The King asked, "Mirza, how many fasts did you keep?" He replied, "My Lord and Guide, I did not keep one." [TN: the pun turns on *ek nahin rakha*, which can mean both "I did not keep a single one" and "there was one I did not keep", as if he had kept all the rest.]

# Anecdote

One day he came to see Nawab Mustafa Khan at his house. The covered passage (*chhatta*) in front of his house was very dark. When he passed through the passage and reached the door of the reception hall, the Nawab was standing there to receive him. Seeing him, the Mirza recited this half-line: "For the water of the fountain of life is within the darkness." When he reached the reception hall, its verandah, because it faced east, was full of sunshine. There the Mirza recited this half-line: "This house is all sunshine." [TN: both half-lines are Persian.]

# Anecdote

At a gathering the Mirza was praising Mir Taqi. Shaikh Ibrahim Zauq was also present; he preferred Sauda to Mir. The Mirza said: "I took you for a follower of Mir (*Miri*), but now I find that you are a madman (*Sauda'i*)." [TN: a pun: *sauda'i*, "crazed", also means a follower of Sauda.]

# Anecdote

The opinion of the late Maulvi Imam Bakhsh Sahba'i about *Panj Ruq'a* and *Mina Bazar* was that these two works too, like the *Sih Nasr* (Three Prose Pieces), were by Mulla Zuhuri; but the Mirza was against this. At a gathering both gentlemen were present. As it happened the subject came up. The Mirza said: "Leaving aside that there is a vast difference between the style of the *Sih Nasr* and that of *Panj Ruq'a* and *Mina Bazar*, it is not like Zuhuri not to write verse along with his prose. In the whole of the *Sih Nasr* one will hardly find a single page in which there is prose and no verse. On the contrary, in the whole of *Panj Ruq'a* and *Mina Bazar*, apart from a single verse, which is not Zuhuri's either, there is no trace of verse anywhere. It is not understandable that in the prose of a man who has equal power over both verse and prose no verse should be found anywhere." Maulana Sahba'i said, "Such chances often happen; it is merely a matter of chance." The Mirza said: "Certainly! But it will be the kind of chance by which a man is in every respect most sober, well-bred and sensible, and yet by chance now and then bites people too." Hearing this everyone burst out laughing, and Maulana Sahba'i smiled and fell silent.

# Anecdote

The room of the house in which the Mirza sat all day was on the roof over the house's gateway; and to one side of it was a narrow, dark closet whose door was so small that one had to stoop very low to enter it. A floor-covering was always spread in it, and in the season of heat and the hot wind (*lu*) the Mirza often sat there from ten o'clock until three or four. One day, when it was the month of Ramazan and the hot season, Maulana Azurda came to see the Mirza right at midday. At that time the Mirza was playing *chausar* or chess with a friend in that same closet. The Maulana too went in there, and seeing the Mirza playing *chausar* in the month of Ramazan he said: "We had read in the *hadith* that in the month of Ramazan Satan remains imprisoned; but today a doubt has arisen about the soundness of that *hadith*." The Mirza said: "Revered sir! The *hadith* is perfectly sound; but you should know that the place where Satan is imprisoned is this very closet."

In short, nothing the Mirza said was without charm and wit. If anyone had collected all his sayings, a thick book of witticisms and jests would have been made.

# Self-respect

Although the Mirza's income and means were very small, he never let go of his self-respect and the keeping up of his standing. He met the nobles and notables of the city on equal terms. He never went out into the bazaar except in a palanquin or a *hawadar* (an open sedan chair). Those of the city's notables who did not come to his house, he too never went to their houses; and whoever came to his house, he too would certainly go to his.

# Anecdote

One day, after visiting someone, he came to the late Nawab Mustafa Khan's house; I too was present there at the time. The Nawab said, "Have you come straight here from home, or have you been somewhere else too?" The Mirza said, "I owed him a visit, so I went there first; from there I have come here."

# Anecdote

One day the late Diwan Fazlullah Khan, riding in a *charat* [TN: apparently a "chariot", a carriage], passed by the Mirza's house without calling. When the Mirza learned of it, he wrote the Diwan a note, to this effect: "Today I am so ashamed that I am sinking into the ground with shame. What greater impropriety could there be than that you should pass this way some time or other and I should not come to pay my respects?" When this note reached the Diwan he was much ashamed, and at once got into his carriage and came to see the Mirza.
