In the name of God, the Most Gracious, the Most Merciful

# The Beginning of the Book

# Date of Birth

Mirza Asadullah Khan Ghalib, known as Mirza Nausha, entitled Najm-ud-Daula Dabir-ul-Mulk Asadullah Khan Bahadur Nizam Jang, whose pen name (*takhallus*) was Ghalib in Persian and Asad in *Rekhta* (Urdu verse), was born in the city of Agra on the night of the 8th of Rajab, 1212 of the Hijra [TN: 27 December 1797].

# Family

The account of the Mirza's family and lineage, as he has set it out here and there in his writings, is this: his forefathers were Turks of the Aibak tribe, and his line of descent goes back to Tur son of Faridun. When the Kayanians gained mastery over the whole of Iran and Turan, and the glory and majesty of the Turanians departed from the world, the race of Tur remained for a long time without kingdom or wealth; but the sword never left their hands, for among the Turks the rule had come down from ancient times that of the father's estate the son received nothing but the sword, while all the goods, chattels and house went to the daughter's share. At length, after a long time, in the age of Islam, by virtue of that same sword the sleeping fortune of the Turks turned over once more, and the foundation of a mighty empire was laid in the Seljuq family. For several hundred years they ruled over the whole of Iran, Turan, Syria and Rum (that is, Asia Minor). At last, after a time, the empire of the Seljuqs came to an end, and the descendants of Seljuq were scattered here and there. Among them a nobleman named Tarsam Khan had settled in Samarqand. The Mirza's grandfather, who came to India from Samarqand in the time of Shah 'Alam, was of the descendants of this Tarsam Khan. The Mirza writes in the preface to *Mihr-e Nimroz*: "Among the last of this caravan, my grandfather, whose birthplace was the city of Samarqand in the realm of Transoxiana, came from Samarqand to India like a flood that comes down from the heights to the lowland." And in *Dirafsh-e Kawiyani* he writes thus: "In short, after the decline of their fortune and the breaking up of the tumult of their empire, the Seljuqs were scattered over the wide expanse of the land of Transoxiana. Among them the prince Tarsam Khan, of whose seed we are, chose Samarqand for his residence; until in the reign of Shah 'Alam my grandfather came from Samarqand to India." [TN: both quotations are in Persian in the original.]

The Mirza's grandfather spoke only Turkish, and understood very little of the language of India. At that time Zu'l-Fiqar-ud-Daula Mirza Najaf Khan had complete influence at the court of Shah 'Alam. Najaf Khan obtained for the Mirza's grandfather a good rank (*mansab*), as befitted the state of the empire, and assigned him the *pargana* (district) of Pahasu for his personal pay and that of his troop. He had several sons, of whom the names of two are known: one the Mirza's father, 'Abdullah Beg Khan, known as Mirza Dulha, and the other Nasrullah Beg Khan. 'Abdullah Beg Khan was married to the daughter of Khwaja Ghulam Husain Khan Kumaidan, who was an honoured military officer of the *sarkar*[1] of Meerut and one of the notables of the city of Agra. Mirza 'Abdullah Beg Khan spent his whole life in his in-laws' house as a resident son-in-law (*khana-damad*), and his children too were brought up there. Mirza 'Abdullah Beg Khan had two sons: one Mirza Asadullah Khan, and the other Mirza Yusuf Khan, who went mad in his youth and died in that state in 1857.

The Mirza's father, 'Abdullah Beg Khan, as the Mirza himself has written in a letter, first went to Lucknow and took service with Nawab Asaf-ud-Daula; a few days later he went from there to Hyderabad, and served the Asafi government (*sarkar-e Asafi*) for several years with a troop of three hundred horse. But that service was lost in the tangle of a civil war, and he came back to Agra. On arriving here he set out for Alwar. Raja Bakhtawar Singh had not yet given him any satisfactory post when, as it happened, in those very days the *zamindar*s (landholders) of a fortlet rebelled against the Raj. Mirza 'Abdullah Beg Khan too was sent with the force dispatched to subdue that fortlet. As soon as he arrived there he was struck by a bullet and died on the spot, and was buried at Rajgarh[2]. Raja Bakhtawar Singh, ruler of Alwar, assigned two productive villages and a certain daily allowance for the upbringing of the two sons of the late Mirza, which continued for a long time. After the death of the Mirza's father, his uncle Nasrullah Beg Khan brought him up.

When the rule of the English government was well established in India, and Nawab Fakhr-ud-Daula Ahmad Bakhsh Khan joined the army of Lord Lake, he had Mirza Ghalib's uncle Nasrullah Beg Khan, to whom the said Nawab's sister was married, taken into the government's army with the rank of *risaldar* (commander of a cavalry troop). For his personal pay and that of his troop, two *pargana*s near Agra, namely Sonk and Sonsa, were assigned in his name by the government. As long as he lived both *pargana*s remained in his name; and after his death the government had pensions for his heirs and dependants fixed upon the State of Firozpur Jhirka, out of which seven hundred rupees a year were regularly paid to the Mirza until the end of April 1857. But after the conquest of Delhi this pension remained stopped for three years because of his connection with the Fort. At last, when the Mirza had been cleared in every respect, the pension was restored, and the government also granted the arrears of the three years. As long as the pension remained stopped the Mirza's friends were greatly concerned.

# Anecdote

Many people used to send letters to enquire about the pension. Once Mir Mahdi had sent a letter on this subject; in reply the Mirza writes: "My dear fellow, I have learned the knack of living without sustenance; set your mind at rest on that score. The month of Ramazan I got through by eating fasts [TN: wordplay: *roze khana*, "to eat the fasts", means to skip fasting]; for the rest, God is the provider; if nothing else is to be had to eat, there is always grief [TN: *gham khana*, "to eat grief", that is, to suffer]."

The Mirza has here and there taken pride in Persian verse in the loftiness of his family; since some of these verses are not without charm, they are quoted here. A *qit'a* (verse fragment):

Ghalib, I am of the pure soil of Turan;
so of course in lineage I am glorious.

I was born a Turk, and in race
I am joined to the great ones of the tribe.

We are Aibaks[3], of the company of the Turks;
in fullness we are ten times greater than the moon.

The craft of our forefathers was husbandry (*varz*);
we are the son of a marcher-lord of Samarqand.

Practising the husbandry of meaning, we have brought forth poetry;
what shall we say ourselves of what and how much we are? [TN: wordplay on *varz*, "tilling", in this couplet and the one before.]

Of the bounty of God we are the humblest pupil;
of Universal Reason we are the best son.

In brightness we are fellows of the lightning,
in giving we are like the cloud.

In whatever quest there is, we are victorious;
with the livelihood that is not, we are content.

All of us weep over ourselves;
all of us laugh at the world.

A *qit'a*:

Cupbearer, since I am of Pashang[4] and Afrasiyab,
you know that the root of my essence is from the line of Jam.

Jamshid's inheritance, which was wine, now hand over to me;
after that comes Paradise, which is Adam's inheritance.

A *ruba'i* (quatrain):

Ghalib, in essence I am of the line of Zadsham[5];
hence my breath has the keenness of the sword's edge.

When soldiering was gone, I took hold of poetry;
the broken arrow of my ancestors became my pen.

# Education

Mirza Ghalib, together with his younger brother, remained in Agra until he came of age. Although he had begun going to and from Delhi from the age of seven, his permanent home remained in Agra until after his marriage, and he received his education from Shaikh Mu'azzam, who was one of the well-known teachers of Agra at that time. After this a man of Parsi descent, whose name in the days of his fire-worship was Hurmuzd and who after becoming a Muslim was named 'Abdus-Samad, came to Agra, probably as a traveller, and stayed with the Mirza for two years, first in Agra and then in Delhi; from him the Mirza gained some insight into the Persian language. It is true that the Mirza was sometimes heard to say: "I have been the pupil of no one but the Bountiful Source (*Mabda'-e Fayyaz*, God), and 'Abdus-Samad is only an imaginary name. Since people used to call me 'teacherless', I invented an imaginary teacher to shut their mouths." But there is no doubt that 'Abdus-Samad really was a man of Parsi descent, and that the Mirza learned Persian from him, more or less. Thus the Mirza has here and there in his writings taken pride in having been his pupil, and has referred to him by the word *hamtasar* [TN: reading uncertain], which among the Parsis is a word of the greatest respect. But, as the Mirza has stated explicitly in some of his writings, he was fourteen years old when 'Abdus-Samad arrived at his house, and he stayed there two years in all. So when one considers at what age the Mirza had his company, and how short a time was spent in it, 'Abdus-Samad and his teaching come to the same thing whether they existed or not. Therefore the Mirza's saying that he had been the pupil of no one but the Bountiful Source is not at all wrong.

In one place the Mirza has expressed most beautifully the theme of drawing benefit from the Bountiful Source, and the verse is this:

Whatever is in the Bountiful Source is mine;
the rose not yet parted from the branch is in my lap.

In another place he has expressed this meaning in an even better way; he says:

In taking bounty from the Source I have surpassed my predecessors,
because I came somewhat later to that court.

My appearance in the world was in twelve hundred[6];
the appearance of Khusrau and Sa'di was in six hundred and fifty.

Mulla 'Abdus-Samad, besides Persian, which was his mother tongue and the religious language of his people, was also, as the Mirza has written, a very great scholar of Arabic. Although the Mirza had very little of his company, for a gifted nature like the Mirza's to find in childhood so kind, accomplished and bilingual a teacher was one of those rare chances that seldom occur. Although the Mirza did not get the opportunity to profit from him further, the blessing of his company at least produced in the Mirza that faculty of which it has been said: "If it is acquired, the learned and the unlearned are equal; and if it is not acquired, the learned and the unlearned are still equal." It seems that the Mirza's fine ability and fine aptitude had left so deep an impression on Mulla 'Abdus-Samad's heart that even after leaving here he did not forget the Mirza for a long time. The late Nawab Mustafa Khan used to say that in a letter which the Mulla had sent the Mirza from some other country this phrase was written: "Dear one, who are you, that in spite of all these freedoms of mine, you pass through my mind now and then?" From this it appears that he would certainly not have held back anything he could teach the Mirza in the short space of two years; and, as appears from *Qati'-e Burhan* and *Dirafsh-e Kawiyani*, he had settled firmly in the Mirza's mind all the first principles and secrets of the Persian language, the religious ideas and mysteries of the Parsis, which play a very great part in understanding Persian, the common origin of Persian and Sanskrit, and other necessary things of this kind.

# Marriage

Since the Mirza's uncle had married into the family of Nawab Fakhr-ud-Daula, and a kind of connection had thus arisen with that family, the Mirza's marriage was arranged in the house of Mirza Ilahi Bakhsh Khan Ma'ruf, the younger brother of Nawab Fakhr-ud-Daula. And, as the Mirza has indicated in a note, he was married at the age of thirteen, on 7 Rajab 1225 of the Hijra [TN: 9 August 1810]. Through this connection his visits to Delhi increased, and at last he settled here and remained in Delhi to the end of his life.

# His Mother's Family

The *jagir* (land grant) of the Mirza's maternal grandfather included several villages, and he had very large property in the city of Agra. The Mirza writes in a letter to Munshi Shiv Narain, a gentleman of Agra: "How was I to know who you were? When I learned that you are the grandson of Nazir Bansi Dhar, I knew that you are my own dear son. Now if I were to address you as 'kind and honoured sir' I should be a sinner. What do you know of the bond between our family and yours? Hear it from me. Your great-grandfather, in the time of Najaf Khan, was the companion of my late maternal grandfather, Khwaja Ghulam Husain Khan. When my grandfather gave up service and sat at home, your great-grandfather too loosened his belt and took no service anywhere again. These things are from before I can remember. But when I grew up I saw that Munshi Bansi Dhar was with Khan Sahib, and that in the claim he had brought before the government for Kaitham, a village of his *jagir*, Bansi Dhar was the manager of the matter and acted as his agent and attorney. He and I were of the same age; perhaps Munshi Bansi Dhar was a year or two older or younger than I. I was nineteen or twenty, and so was he; together we had chess, and companionship, and affection; half the night would pass; since his house was not very far, he would go whenever he liked. Between our house and his there were only the house of Machhiya the dancing girl and our two *katra*s (walled market enclosures). Our great *haveli* (mansion) is the one that Lakhmi Chand Seth has now bought; my seat was in the stone *baradari* (pavilion) over its gateway. Near it was another *haveli*, the one with the cot [TN: *khatiya wali haveli*, sense uncertain], and another *haveli* near the *takiya* (shrine) of Salim Shah, and yet another *haveli* adjoining the Kala Mahal, and further on a *katra* known as the shepherds' *katra*, and a *katra* called the Kashmiri woman's *katra*. On the roof of one house in that *katra* I used to fly kites, and fought kites with Raja Balwan Singh. A soldier named Wasil Khan was your grandfather's assistant, and he used to collect the rents of the *katra*s and have them deposited. Brother, just listen! Your grandfather made a great deal. He had bought estates and made the *zamindari* his own; he paid ten or twelve thousand rupees of revenue to the government. Did all those concerns come into your hands or not? Write to me soon about this in detail." From the contents of this letter it appears that the Mirza's maternal grandfather had a considerable establishment in Agra, through which his servants and dependants had become revenue-payers of ten and twelve thousand; and that the Mirza's childhood and early youth were spent in great luxury.

# Appearance

It has been heard from those people of Delhi who saw the Mirza in his youth that in early manhood he was counted among the most handsome and good-looking men of the city. And in old age too, when the present writer first saw him, traces of comeliness and beauty were plainly visible in his face, his stature and his build. But at the end of his life, through eating little and through chronic illnesses, he had become very thin, weak and wasted. But since his frame was very broad, his stature tall and his hands and feet powerful, even in that state he looked like a newly arrived man of Turan.

# Residence

The period of his residence in Delhi seems to have been nearly fifty years. In all that time he probably never bought a house here for himself. He always lived in rented houses, or for a long time lived rent-free in the house of Miyan Kale Sahib. When he tired of one house he left it and took another. But he never went to live in any quarter other than the lane of Qasim Jan, or the gate of Habash Khan, or their neighbourhood. The very last house, in which he died, was behind the mosque next to the reception hall (*diwan-khana*) of the late Hakim Mahmud Khan, of which he says:

In the shadow of the mosque I have made myself a home;
this humble servant is God's neighbour.

# Reading of Books

Just as the Mirza never bought a house to live in all his life, so too, although his whole life was spent in writing, he never bought any book for reading, save as God willed. There was a man whose trade it was to bring people books on hire from the booksellers' shops; the Mirza too always had books brought on hire from him, and returned them after reading.

# Notes

[1] Hali's note: *Sarkar* was the name for a portion of territory smaller than a *suba* (province) and much larger than a *pargana*, *mahal* and the like.

[2] Hali's note: In the *qasida* that the Mirza wrote in praise of Raja Shiv Dhyan Singh he writes [TN: Persian verse; the first three couplets are in very small print and read tentatively]:
It is long since I became a servant of your presence;
I am a weaver of colourful speech and an old pensioner.
For eight years I have had my neck in the ring of the chain,
now that my age counts sixty-three years.
One should hear it from those near the court;
one should hear the story from the elders of that land.
Witnessing is enough, no witness is needed:
in the soil of Rajgarh is my father's tomb.

[3] Hali's note: Aibak is a Turkish word, compounded of *ay* and *bak*. *Ay* means the moon and *bak* means perfect and great; so Aibak means "full moon" and "great". That is why the Mirza said: "In fullness we are ten times greater than the moon."

[4] Hali's note: Pashang is the name of Afrasiyab's father.

[5] Hali's note: Zadsham is the name of Afrasiyab's grandfather and Pashang's father.

[6] Hali's note: *Duwist*, that is, *du sad* (two hundred). Native speakers often use the word *-ist* instead of *sad* with the word *du* (two).
