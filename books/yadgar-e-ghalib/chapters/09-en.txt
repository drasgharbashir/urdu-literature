# Abuse in Anonymous Letters

When this case had been filed away, after some time people began to send the Mirza anonymous letters full of abuse and invective, in which harsh curses, taunts and reproaches were written about his wine-drinking, his irreligion and the like. In those days the Mirza was in a strange state; he remained very gloomy and out of sorts. And when the postman came with the post, his face would change at the thought that some letter of the same kind might have come.

# The Mirza's Dealings with the Present Writer

As it happened, in those very days I came to Delhi with the late Nawab Mustafa Khan. Since I knew nothing of the arrival of these worthless anonymous letters, one day I committed a blunder the thought of which has always made me deeply ashamed. That was a time when I was drunk with the intoxication of religious self-righteousness. Of all God's creation I thought only the Muslims, and of the seventy-three sects of the Muslims only the Sunnis, and of the Sunnis only the Hanafis, and of them only those who keep fasting, prayer and the other outward ordinances with the strictest observance, worthy of salvation and forgiveness. It was as if I imagined the circle of God's mercy to be narrower and more limited even than the extent of Queen Victoria's empire, in which men of every religion and community live in peace and security. The more love or attachment I had for anyone, the more I longed that his end should be in the state which in my opinion was indispensable for salvation and forgiveness. Since my love and attachment for the Mirza were of the highest degree, I always felt sorry for his state. It was as if I thought that in the garden of Paradise our company would be broken, and that after death I should not be able to meet him again. One day, putting aside the respect and reverence due to the Mirza's eminence, his mastery and his great age, I began to lecture him like a dry-brained preacher. Since his deafness had reached the extreme, and conversation with him was carried on only in writing, I wrote out a long, broad lecture on the obligation and importance of the five daily prayers and set it before him, in which I begged him to take up the regular practice of the five daily prayers in whatever way he could, standing, or sitting, or by gestures and signs; if he could not perform the ablution, then let it be *tayammum* (ablution with clean earth), but let the prayer not be given up.

The Mirza found this urging very unpleasant, and it was indeed a thing to find unpleasant; especially because in those very days people were cursing and reproaching his deeds in anonymous letters in a most unseemly way, and writing open abuse like the rabble of the bazaar. What the Mirza said on seeing my foolish writing is worth hearing. He said: "My whole life has been spent in sin and wickedness; I have never said a prayer, nor kept a fast, nor done any good deed. Only a few breaths of life are left; if now for a few days I say the prayer sitting, or by gestures and signs, how can that make up for the sins of a whole life? I deserve that when I die my relatives and friends should blacken my face, tie a rope to my feet, parade me through all the lanes and bazaars of the city, and then take me out of the city and leave me for the dogs, kites and crows to eat (if they can bear to eat such a thing). Although my sins are such that I should be treated even worse than this, there is no doubt that I am a believer in the unity of God (*muwahhid*). Always, in solitude and silence, these words are on my tongue: '*La ilaha illallah, la maujuda illallah, la mu'aththira fil-wujudi illallah*' (there is no god but God; there is nothing existent but God; there is nothing effective in existence but God)."

Perhaps on that same day, when this conversation was over and the Mirza was eating, the postman brought him an envelope. From the irregularity of the envelope and the strangeness of the writer's name he was sure that it was an anonymous letter from some opponent, like those that had come before. He gave me the envelope and told me to open and read it. When I looked, the whole letter was indeed full of obscenity and abuse. He asked, "Whose letter is it? And what does it say?" I hesitated to tell him. At once he snatched the envelope from my hand and said, "Perhaps it is written by some spiritual disciple of yours."

# Anecdote

Then he read it himself from beginning to end. In one place it contained abuse of his mother. Smiling, he said: "This owl does not even know how to abuse. An old or middle-aged man is abused through his daughter, so that his honour may be stung. A young man is abused through his wife, because he is most attached to his wife. A child is abused through his mother, for he is attached to no one as much as to his mother. Who could be a greater fool than this pimp who abuses an old man of seventy-two through his mother?"

After this I took my leave of him and came away. The next day His Honour wrote a *ghazal* and sent it to me, in which, although my name and pen name were not mentioned, it appeared from some of its themes and hints that the taunts and innuendoes in it were aimed at me. The *ghazal* is this:

Towards the goal which they call the way of God,
they say "Go, go!", while from that side they say "Come, come!"

How shall one who has no feet walk the road?
What do the people of the law say in this case?

Unaware of the secret of the tree that said "I am God",
they tell the tale of Moses' place of vision and his staff [TN: reading of this couplet moderately confident].

Do the worshippers of God feel no shame before God,
that they do not take God's name, and say only "I"?

Nothing of what they say finds a place in the hearts of the discerning
except those attributes that they speak of the Divine Essence.

Not read in books, and not heard from the jurists,
only the tasteless repetitions that they keep repeating [TN: reading moderately confident].

The ignorant talk of "Your existence is a sin"[1];
how can they call God's gift our sin?

Yes! To claim existence on our part is a sin;
say it thus to the people of the secret, so that they say it rightly.

And what courage have the self-reproachers (*malamati*s) to answer
if men turn to anger and speak abuse?

Without having turned their own copper into gold, they tell, for show and deceit,
stories of alchemy before the people.

Those who lay claim to goodness, if they do not
count me good, why do they call me bad?

Do not hope to be given the title *Maulana*;
it is enough for one like you to be called pious.

Give the good news that in the world the business of wretched Ghalib
has gone beyond being called a *darvesh* and destitute.

On seeing this *ghazal* I had the opportunity to express, in a short *qit'a*, with some complaint, the thoughts about the Mirza's poetic excellence that were hidden in my heart and which I had never had occasion to express. So I composed the following *qit'a* and sent it to the Mirza [TN: Hali's Persian verse; the print is calligraphic and several words are read with moderate confidence]:

# Qit'a

O you whose verse and prose, which you spoke in our age,
have broken the splendour of the men of old together,

what melodies you weighed on the instrument of taste!
What witticisms you spoke in a heart-ravishing manner!

The intoxication of mystic knowledge came when you spoke of wine;
the hearts of friends bloomed when you spoke of the morning breeze.

Roots ran into hearts when you spoke a word of love;
the palm of longing sprang up when you spoke of faithfulness.

You scattered pearls in the assembly when you recited praise;
you sent power running through words when you spoke a prayer.

A thousand closed knots you opened;
a thousand hidden subtleties you spoke openly.

You told tales of the secret of separation and union;
you spoke secrets of the journey through souls and horizons.

The song of delight rose from the hearts of strangers
in the gathering where you spoke familiar words.

Subtleties that could not fit into word and expression,
you came like an angel from the unseen and spoke them out.

By the right of the grace of your speech, which is upon our heart,
the rank of poetry was raised up when you spoke.

O you, every fine word of whom has found a place in the heart,
except what you spoke in hints about Hali,

whatever you have said in reply to my humble petition,
it would be wrong of me to take it amiss, even if you spoke amiss.

But I am quarrelling with myself over a few words:
if I did not say them, where then did you get them from? [TN: reading of this couplet uncertain.]

Strange that one who knows the rule of humble devotion
you called foolish, conceited, self-regarding and vain!

Strange that one who savours humility
you called a slave to the delight of singing "I"!

Strange that one ashamed of his counterfeit coin
you called one who keeps deceit in his purse and makes a show of alchemy! [TN: reading uncertain.]

Words have no road to you, nor have I a place in your heart;
what is the answer, if I ask where you said it from?

If my words were not addressed to you, I would say:
how did you say it? and why did you say it? and for what did you say it?

But it is not the condition of good manners to find fault with you;
whatever you have said about me, you said rightly.

At the time when this happened I was attached to the late Nawab Muhammad Mustafa Khan, whose pen names were Shefta and Hasrati, chief of Jahangirabad; he had come to Delhi in those days and I was staying at his house. When this *qit'a* came before the Mirza, he wrote and sent the late Nawab a most delicate *qit'a* of four couplets, which is given below:

# Qit'a

O you who bear the names Shefta and Hasrati,
I set my hopes on your kindness.

Since Hali has taken offence at distracted me without cause,
if you do not intercede, tell me, what shall I do?

If, supposing the impossible, they gave me life a second time,
I am resolved to do these two things in that life:

one, to perform the acts of worship of my former life;
the other, to make my apology in Hali's presence.

Although I am ashamed to set my light and worthless verse again and again before readers alongside the Mirza's lofty verse, the place and occasion require that the event which has been begun should be brought to its end. On this *qit'a* of the Mirza's I wrote another *qit'a* and sent it to him, which is written below:

# Qit'a

O you who have sent an apology to your servant,
it is fitting that I should sacrifice my dear life for it.

A complaint that can be called the very essence of sincerity,
if you count me a friend, I will make a thousand times.

No rule of thanks without hypocrisy remains in the world;
I will make the foundation of friendship firm with complaint.

Since complaint comes only from the demands of friendship,
I will thank strangers, and complain to the friend.

They have given me a pure nature and a clean heart;
with a bitter word I make my heart empty of dust.

How sweet that I should make an unjust complaint against you,
and you should apologise, and I should take pride in it!

How sweet that, since your apology uproots me [TN: reading uncertain],
I should in turn begin to apologise before you!

I am resolved that if death grants me respite after this,
of all the works of the world I will do especially these two:

repent of what I have done, and ask forgiveness for what I have said,
and then give thanks to you, in secret and openly.

When this *qit'a* reached the Mirza he wrote on it, "Enough, now let the exchange of verses stop," and sent it back to me; after that nothing more was written.

# Notes

[1] Hali's note: This is a well-known saying of the Sufis. In what I had written and given to the Mirza this sentence too was written at one point. The Mirza objects to it and does not accept it. He says: "Existence is a gift of God; how then can it be called our sin? Rather, the claim to existence is a sin. If it is said thus to the people of the secret, they may accept it." But it should be understood that those whose saying is "Your existence is a sin" do not believe in any existence except the one Necessary Existence. The unity of being (*wahdat-ul-wujud*) is their fundamental principle. So in their view the existence of what is other than God is not a gift of God, but one of the inventions of human fancy and imagination; that is why they say, "Your existence is a sin."
