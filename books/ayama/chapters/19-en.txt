# Chapter 18: Azadi's Last Illness and Her Final Thoughts

This inquiry was so interesting that if her health had permitted, Azadi would have stayed busy with it all her life; but the hip she had dislocated as a girl, when she fell from the roof, now, thirteen or fourteen years later, began now and then to give her a sort of shooting pain. No one's mind turned to the old injury of the hip. Hot poultices and steam baths and cupping and leeches and dry and wet horns and mercury and massage: whatever anyone suggested, they tried it all. The pain would not budge. The physicians gave purge after purge, and the pain only increased the more. A woman, and the place one to be kept covered: the treatment of physicians and surgeons too went on from verbal description. Several years melted away in this, and the leg came to such a state that it was as if the whole body had shrunk and gathered into it. Pain, and with the pain swelling. At the very last it had come to this, that far from touching it or laying a hand on it, she could not bear anyone even to breathe towards the leg. When it had come to this, they remembered the padre's Memsahib who had set the dislocated hip. That Memsahib had gone home to England, but another woman among those people did surgical work. As soon as she saw it she said: "There is an abscess inside; it will have to be cut." Several days passed in this hesitation, whether to have it cut or not, and whether it really was an abscess as the lady doctor said, or some melancholic humour with poison in it, as the physicians diagnosed. At last, compelled and at their wits' end, they had the lancet used. A seer and a half, less one *chhatank*, of pus came out by weight, and seeing it everyone was struck dumb. A wound of years, the outer skin hard, and inside several fistulas had formed. With the lancet the swelling as good as went down, the burning grew less too, and she who for months had never once slept even half an hour on end now began to sleep three or four hours at a stretch. Her appetite turned towards food; but the pain of washing and cleaning the wounds and putting in the wick was what it was. The disease had fastened on her in such a way that if those surgeons and the Indian physicians had gone on treating her, Azadi would have died long before. At last, by the lady doctor's skill, all the wounds healed after some nine or ten months, her strength came back too, and Azadi began to walk about quite well. Even so a hidden sinus was left, which could hardly be cured without a second use of the lancet. The lady doctor was waiting for Azadi to recover a little and gain some strength before she used the lancet a second time; but Azadi and those nursing her had been so frightened by the first lancet that they would not let her touch it. However much the lady doctor reasoned with them ("Although the wound is slight and in name only, and it may heal of itself, and perhaps even this occasional stain it leaves on the bandage will not remain, I am not satisfied; in my opinion the lancet must be used; and I have described the state of the wound to other doctors and asked their opinion, and they too advise the lancet. The place of the wound is so bad that if it turns inwards, no remedy will be of any use. That is why I say it is not right to leave even a little defect, and there will be no need for a deep cut yet. I hope that in two weeks she will be entirely clear"), those people, emboldened by her passing health, paid no heed to the doctor's, nay, the doctors' opinion; and that hidden sinus went on quietly growing and spreading inside.

In this illness too, whenever Azadi's mind was at all clear, she would grieve over her widowhood: not at all because there was any lack in the service and care given her (a hundred services and a thousand cares were on one side and the lady doctor's treatment on the other), but because she remembered the two or four times when, in Maulvi Mustajab's time, she had had fever and other ordinary ailments, and his sympathy and tenderness, and where was that to be found now? Again and again she thought: "The marriage tie is a wondrous kind of tie, which makes not only joy but grief and affliction too sweet." Through her illness another change also came about in Azadi's thoughts. Her illness was severe in any case; and though no one said anything to her face but words of comfort and reassurance, she could tell from people's faces that they had given up hope of her. It was the lady doctor's practice to come and sit by Azadi's bed, and before touching the wound she would close her eyes and pray in her heart for Azadi's health; then, after washing and dressing the wound, she would close her eyes for a few minutes and take her leave. Everyone had understood, but at last someone asked outright, and when asked, the Memsahib said: "I pray for her: 'O Lord, of Thy grace and kindness heal this Thy handmaid, take away her suffering, and help me not to err in diagnosing the disease and in treating it.'" In short, partly from the fear of those about her, and above all from the witness of her own heart within, Azadi too had understood well: "In the first place I do not see myself surviving this illness; and if perhaps I do survive, I shall be a cripple, and what kind of life is that? Rather than that, if God takes me away, I will make a prostration of thanks at His court." Along with the thought of dying, the questions and answers Azadi held in her own heart were most interesting.

Question: Am I really going to die some day soon now?

Answer: No one knows but God. As for the illness, it is a wound. I have seen people live without a leg. There is that old beggar woman who comes to beg; God knows what happened, but her leg was cut off at the root of the thigh, and she goes round the whole city on a crutch. And many cases too have been seen where people died of a little ailment. In short, it would be no wonder if I died and no wonder if I lived. And what use is this question? One has to die some day after all. Life is like a clock: some are wound for twenty-four hours, some for a week, some for a month. When anyone's winding has run down, it stops.

Q: Must I expect more pain in dying than I have already had from the wound in my leg?

A: That too no one can know but God. Some I have seen lie in the death agony for four or five days and give up their lives with great difficulty, and some depart while they are talking. At first I thought that the souls of good people must leave easily; then I saw in the hadith that even the Prophet of God, God bless him and give him peace, suffered hard pains of death for three days, so that he kept turning quickly from side to side and kept wetting his hands in water and passing them over his face; and the lady Aisha used to say: "Since I saw the death agony of the Prophet of God, God bless him, I do not count anyone's death agony as agony at all." And I have seen some saintly people die in pain, and heard people say: "What fortunate servants of God they were, that they paid here in this world the penalty for the sins they committed in the world!" Besides, the souls of innocent children often leave with great hardship. Many people's brains cease to work at the time of death, so that they do not feel the pain of the death agony, as you did not feel the setting of your hip or the lancet. In any case ease and hardship depend on the soul's attachment, and no one knows that but God. And then I say that this question too is idle. Dying itself is not in a person's power at all; then why should dying easily be in his control?

Q: Then it would have been better not to have been born at all. God gave life without being asked, and then He took it back with so much pain.

A: God forgive me, God forgive me, these too are thoughts like unbelief! If at the last moment a person's soul should leave in such thoughts, he would be cast out for ever and ever. This is meddling with God's power: "Why did You do this, and why did You do it so?" So vast a workshop as the whole world, who can know for what purpose it is kept going? On the one face of the earth there are thousands and hundreds of thousands of kinds of creatures, and among them one creature is man, the rules of whose birth and life and death are all fixed. Sometimes, seeing a goat or a cat or other dumb creatures, I say in my heart: "O God, what sort of souls are shut up in these bodies, and what thoughts do these souls dwell in?" Another kind of creature is the trees, whose kinds are beyond counting; and some of their states too resemble man's. They were born, grew, flowered and bore fruit, and at last rotted and became dust. Just as some fruits are not allowed to ripen and are lost, so some human children perish in infancy. In short, a strange sort of round is going on, which cannot be understood at all. And if a person sets about thinking absurd thoughts, he can raise many pointless doubts; for instance: "Why was woman singled out to bear children? Could it not have been that a woman sneezed and children dropped from her nose? Or a woman coughed and spat out sons and daughters from her mouth? Or, just as fruit grows on a single tree, why do children not come from a woman alone, and did they not come to the lady Mary?" But all these are the mischief of the little reason that has been given to man. When there is a flaw in reason, just such pranks come to mind. Proud of this little bit of reason, man thinks himself something. He is something, yes, but more contemptible than a thumb's snap, more despicable than I, weaker than an ant; and by the least little pain and the smallest trouble his abjectness and contemptibility and worthlessness keep being shown up; but that brother is like what they say, "A drop fell on a greasy pot and slid off." The pain went, the trouble passed, and he began to boast again. God Most High has stated this very theme in several places in the holy Quran; in one place it is: "*And when harm touches man, he calls upon Us, lying on his side, or sitting, or standing; but when We remove his harm from him, he passes on as if he had never called upon Us for a harm that touched him*" (and when some hurt reaches a man, lying on his side if he is lying, sitting if he is sitting, standing if he is standing, he calls on Us for help; then when We remove his hurt, he is seen walking about as if he had never called on Us for that hurt at all). Even without pain and ailment, man is a creature helpless and weak in the highest degree. If he does not get air to breathe even for a few minutes he cannot stay alive. His endurance of hunger and thirst is such that in the month of Ramazan those who keep a true fast, although until the pre-dawn meal they go on stuffing the oven of their belly full of food and water, by evening even a good sturdy man's joints are loose.

The reason that seeks a livelihood has drawn a veil over it; otherwise, has man any foundation at all? A little cold, and he begins to shiver; it grows hot, and he cries out. The Artificer of nature has given other animals such equipment: one has horns, one claws, one teeth, one a beak; to one swiftness of pace, to one flight, feathers or wool to protect it from the harshness of the weather; and man is deprived of all of these, cut off from everything. By bodily exercise and training, by drinking milk and eating ghee, he increases his strength and vigour; but he cannot carry as great a load as a buffalo, nor cover as many stages as a camel, nor run like a horse, nor draw the well-rope like a bullock. However many tricks of single-stick fighting he may know, a single fly is beyond his power, a single flea beyond his control. So helpless and powerless, and on top of that so proud and arrogant! But all his boasting is spoiled by death, which neither listens to prayer nor heeds medicine, neither pities entreaty nor feels for anyone's helplessness, neither fears strength nor bows to authority, neither is stopped by contrivance nor put off by delay.

Q: Oh dear, what will happen after I die?

A: What will happen? Your case will be everyone's case. Dead today, and tomorrow is another day; that is, the one whose time came has died, and then the workshop of the world goes on as before.

The pleasures of the world will never grow less;
The talk will go on just the same; alas, that we shall not be there.

The soul has hardly left before people begin to hurry to carry the body to its first stage. They bury it and come home, weep and beat their breasts a little, not for the dead but for their own advantages, their own interests; a few days later all have forgotten. God be praised, and all is well.

Q: No, I am asking what will happen to me after I die.

A: The thing is that when God hides something for some purpose, what right has His servant to set his reason racing over it? On the face of it nothing appears except that a person's body rots and becomes dust; but think and reflect well, and it does not somehow appeal to the heart that everything ends at death. Ask the reason, and it cannot be put into words; but look at anyone, and he certainly holds this conviction in his heart: "Even if my body becomes dust, or they burn it to ashes, or animals tear it and eat it, still, though I do not know what I am and what I shall be and where I shall be, I shall certainly be; I shall go on." This is a thought such that if it were not there, the whole ordering of the world would be thrown into confusion. No doubt this thought is not always before one's eyes, nor is it equal in everyone; but all a person's deeds, movements and rest depend on this thought. If today people came to believe that one dies and then there is nothing, the world could not go on for a moment. Among such people there would be no peace, no safety, no distinction of good and evil, no difference between bad and good; then what on earth would the world be? It is true that some people go so far as to deny it: "This world is all there is, and after death there is nothing to come." Well, what is hard about giving the lie to a matter of the unseen, whose secret cannot in any way be revealed in this life? But that is their obstinacy and contrariness. Just as they have no knowledge of the coming of the Resurrection, so they have no knowledge of its not coming. They blurted out whatever came into their mouths. And if they are really fully convinced that after death there is nothing to come, why do they not correct the whole world's misunderstanding? If they could explain it to us, and explain it so that this misgiving left our hearts, we would be greatly obliged to them. But how will they convince others, when their own state is that they deny it in word and affirm it in deed? For if in truth they had no fear of the Resurrection, they would be men of quite another sort, and would let no one but themselves live on the face of the earth. The very thought that God has put into the hearts of people in general, and by which they act, is exactly what religion too teaches and insists on: that after death a person will have a kind of existence which he cannot fully understand in this life, and that the good or ill of that existence will depend on the thoughts and beliefs he held in this life, and also on the deeds and acts he did in this life. And there is nothing at all improbable in such a connection between the two lives, when we see the influence of parents in children, of the seed in the fruit, of a person's own early states in his later states.

Q: What will my state be?

A: By that reckoning my next life ought to be the worst of the worst. As far as I can think, I have done no good deed and had none done, except to pamper my own body. If I was born only to pamper my body, to eat, drink, dress and sleep, then there is no difference at all between me and the animals. Then why would so much reason have been given me? Well, perhaps reason was given so that I might live in a better state than the animals; then what need was there to make me understand good and evil, so that this understanding should pinch my heart and give me pain? Alas, alas, these are the means of my everlasting destruction, ruin and perdition! What shall I do now? How was I to know that I would die so soon?

A: Just do not say, "How was I to know?" Why, did you not hear of and see people younger than you dying? Did you not know that it does not take long for the healthy to fall ill and the sick to die? Did you not know that there is no certainty in life? Did not hundreds and thousands of such instances come before you?

Q: Well, if I did no good, neither did I do any such evil. There are all kinds of people in the world; what is everyone's case will be my case.

A: If that is so, then why the alarm? If a person were fully, completely assured that in that life there is no suffering, no grief, then such a person who feared death would be a fool, a coward, a faint-heart. It is only that for two or four days there was the pain of dying; well, does not everyone have it, and there is no way of escaping it. And however great the pain, once it is over, it is gone and past. Then again, are the griefs and comforts of this world griefs and comforts at all? They are passing, and last a few days. The real griefs and comforts are the griefs and comforts of that place, which never break off and have no end anywhere. The great difficulty is just this, that while alive no human being can be assured about that life; so much so that the Prophet himself, whose former and latter sins God had all forgiven, as it is in the Sura *Inna Fatahna*: "*That God may forgive you your sins, past and to come*," he too used to say: "*I do not know what will be done with me or with you*" (I do not know how I shall be dealt with, or what will befall you). And this was the reason that His Holiness took great pains in the worship of God and went on asking God's pardon night and day. Among the Jewish people God had given kingdoms of both kinds, of faith and of the world; that is, some among them were prophets and some kings. Their descendants, seeing the qualities of their forebears, grew so proud that they began to say: "*We are the sons of God and His beloved*" (we are God's sons and favourites); and for this reason it was their saying that even if the fire of Hell touched any of us, it would be only for a few days. To convince and confute them God says: "*Then wish for death, if you are truthful*" (if it is so, then long for death, so that it may be known that what you say with your mouths is also in your hearts). For when a person is assured about the hereafter, why should he try to escape death? In short, this misgiving, "let us see what happens," plainly shows that there is a thief in his heart.

Whoever is guiltless will surely also be at ease. And as for what you said, that if I did no good, neither did I do any such evil, you have no eye to your own responsibility. It cannot be that anyone in the garment of humanity is sinless. Man's very creation has turned out so weak that without straying and going astray it is hard for him to walk even two steps on the straight road. On top of that there is so great a burden of responsibilities on him that he cannot easily carry it. Desire upon desire is at his heels, among which it is extremely hard to keep to the mean. On him are the rights of God, who created him, who gives him his daily bread and provides a thousand kinds of means for his life. On him are the rights of his own self. On him are the rights of the people among whom he lives: that is, of mother and father, of brother and sister, of kin and relation, of friend and acquaintance; if a woman, of the husband, if a man, of the wife; of children, of teacher, of those older than himself, of those younger than himself, of the ruler of the time, of the neighbour, of the fellow countryman, of the earth, of the heavens, of the whole world. It is beyond reason that a person could pay so many rights without shortfall or loss. So, willy-nilly, in one thing or another there is certainly excess or shortcoming on his part, and this very excess and shortcoming is called sin. In the same way your saying, "There are all kinds of people in the world; what is everyone's case will be my case," is utterly foolish and senseless. Then why were you so restless from the pain of your leg? After all there were and are other servants of God in the world who are afflicted with far greater sufferings than you. When the sufferings of others gave you no comfort in this world, why should they in the next? But say rather that the hereafter has not yet fallen on your head; the day it falls on your head the whole truth will be laid open.

Q: Then should I have no hope at all of a good end?

A: No, no; to despair of God is unbelief too:

It takes Him no time at all to show His grace;
Do not despair of Him, be one who hopes.

Q: Can God really show grace even to a sinner like me, who never in all her life did a single good deed? Now when I think of it, among my good deeds, if I can count them as good deeds, all told there is only this one, the prayer. And in the first place I did not say as many prayers as I let lapse; and those I did say, what dust I said them! Outwardly I stand before God with hands folded, and the heart, God knows where all it goes wandering off. Can there be any worse misfortune than that in some prayers I cannot even keep count of the *rak'ats* (the cycles of the prayer)? So I think that such a prayer said and not said are the same. As for the Ramazan fasts, year after year, I will speak plainly: I never kept a single fast with a glad heart. I kept the fasts, but along with it the thought would come willy-nilly that if I did not keep them I would be called a fast-breaker and people would tease me. And for those that lapsed, I never took any trouble to make them up. From this it is clear whether the ones I kept were for God or for show. The third pillar of Islam is *zakat*, about which all my life I had the objection that the *nisab* of fifty-two rupees is far too little. The times are no longer such that one who has fifty-two rupees to spare should be called rich; and when he is not rich, what is this *zakat*? A person with fifty-two rupees is not one who owes *zakat*; call him one who deserves *zakat* and perhaps he is. Yes, I certainly have something, more or less; I never happened to reckon it exactly, but it must be somewhat above a thousand. Even put it at a thousand rupees, and every year, with twenty-five going out as *zakat*, it would one day dwindle away to nothing. If there were twenty-five rupees, would I not rather have the wear and tear of it put right? And when the legal question was explained, then I was of the school of Imam Shafi'i, under whom there is no *zakat* at all on jewellery like mine. In short there was no *zakat*; but if a beggar came and stood at the door and would not budge even for pleading and scolding, then something or other has to be given to such a pest. Why would I not have given a crust of stale bread or a torn old rag? I certainly will have given; but can such giving count as charity? Never; rather, because of the contempt for the asker and the hurt to his heart, the good deed is wasted and the sin made certain. When prayer and fasting and *zakat* are in this state, what mention is there of the Hajj? I neither went nor had any intention of going. And, well, why depend on these outward acts of worship; in any case what sort of bond have I with God? By the custom of the world, God is not a thing to be remembered in times of ease and prosperity. Yes, in time of misfortune God comes to mind, and so He came to my mind too; but when? When no contrivance worked. For remembering so little and in such a way, it would be right to call me a servant of self-interest, not a servant of God. This is the truth of the rights of God. Now as for the rights of His servants, I never had anything to do with anyone beyond my own rights; and if I did, people will only have suffered some hurt from me: backbiting of one, envy of another, malicious joy at another's trouble. My principle of life was this, that is it a small kindness and favour to people that I raise my hand against no one, abuse no one, steal no one else's thing?

I never gave the least thought to this, that people's great right over me is that I should do them good as far as I can. I heard sermons enough, looked through ever so many books, but God knows what stones had fallen on my understanding, that not for a moment did I think what kind of conduct I ought to keep with God and with God's servants. I grieved so much at my husband's death, grieved so much that, well, if anyone else does it, they will do just as much; but I never thought that this same day was coming to me too. No doubt I did not let my chastity be stained; but in that, in the first place, one must see whether it was from the fear of God or from the dread of ill repute; but suppose it was from the fear of God alone that I kept myself safe, then what notable deed did I do, for which I should expect a reward? Of what happens in God's court I know nothing, but in the world rewards are not handed out in this way; otherwise hundreds and thousands of people would come and stand and say: "We did not jump into your house; bring us our reward." Why, sir, if they did not jump in, they escaped punishment; what reward for that? I am greatly amazed that the things which now seem to me so open and clear, why did my mind not turn to them before this? From this it is clear that my time has now come near; otherwise, did I never fall ill before? But such light in my reason and such clearness in my mind never came before. But what comes of thinking now? Thinking was of use only when I had the respite to act on it. Now death has come and stands over my head; or even if it has not come and stood there, the illness has broken me and crushed me and not left me fit even to stand up and say two *rak'ats* in peace. What can be done by me in such a state? If the pain would stop for a little while a person might set her heart on something; but here it will not let me draw breath. How could there be presence of heart in prayer? And grant that I even got well, what remedy is there for past omissions? The time that was going has gone, and now I cannot bring it back. What has already been written in the book of deeds I cannot wipe out. I try ever so hard not to despair of God; but hope of Paradise is idle: I sow thorns and want to pick flowers.

A: No doubt the deeds are such that there cannot be even the faintest chance of salvation; but God is most forgiving, most merciful. In His true and holy Word He has made a most firm promise: "*O My servants who have transgressed against themselves, do not despair of the mercy of God; truly God forgives all sins; truly He is the Forgiving, the Merciful*" (O you people who have done wrong against your own souls, do not despair of God's mercy; surely God will forgive all sins; surely He is the Forgiver, the Kind). Can anything be more comforting than this? In "all sins" every sin is included, whether against God or against His servants.

Q: Is there no need even of repentance and asking forgiveness?

A: When a servant has understood sin to be sin, this very understanding is his repentance. If a person understands a deed to be a sin, he will not do that deed; and the servant is human: even if he does it, sooner or later he will certainly be sorry, and in the end he will certainly give it up.

Q: Well then, for however many days I have to live in this state, what must I do?

A: As far as possible, make amends for what is past, and for the future, reform your thoughts and habits.

Q: But nothing can be done by me now, and there is no hope that in future I shall be fit to do anything.

A: Thoughts of despair are upon you. Most likely those dear sentences of the Quran are not in your mind at this time: "*God burdens no soul beyond its capacity*"; "*God burdens no soul except with what He has given it*" (God, glorious is His state, gives no soul hardship beyond its strength, and from each, according to the measure of what He gave, is asked in keeping with that measure). Neither the alms of the rich nor the poor man's gently saying, "God bless you; we are very ashamed that we have not the means to serve you."

Q: What a blunder I made! In the days when proposal after proposal of marriage kept coming to me from people, one person had written that it was both profit and merit together: if you take a second husband, then for as many widows as marry following your example, until the Day of Resurrection you will receive the merit of reviving this dead *sunnat*. If in those days I had had the marriage read with the intention that it should open the road for others, then no doubt it would have meant that I had, as it were, freed so many women from the torment of widowhood, and saved them from disgrace in this world and shame in the next. But that chance has gone, and gone in such a way that there is no hope of its coming again. Now, wish it a thousand times, and no one would have me. This was the one good deed within my power, and now I am done with that too. And in truth, now that I think, these prayers and fasts are good deeds, yes; but even so, in these acts of worship a person's own interest is all there is: that is, man takes all these pains from the fear of Hell or the greed of Paradise, just as in the world too all his efforts are for his own advantage. People read and write, sit examinations, become another's servants, wander about lost in foreign parts, soldiers have their heads cut off, and God knows what labours they undertake, for the sake of a livelihood. These efforts may be praised in some other way, but no one praises them as good deeds. In the same way, if worship is from the greed of reward or the fear of punishment, it counts as selfishness. The real and true and highest good is that in which a person's own interest has no share at all, and which is done only to carry out God's command or to do good to others. Again and again regret comes over me: why, in the days of my health, when I could do everything, did I not understand, why did I not think? And now what earthly good shall I do to others, when I lie here myself taking service from others, that is, giving them trouble instead? If doing good to others is good, and in truth if anything is good it is this, then I know myself that I have done no good; and if I have done none, why should it be in the book of my deeds? But what comes of these thoughts of mine? The days of life that were going to waste have gone; to bring them back is in no one's power, let alone mine. Yes, from remorse and regret there is this much: that if God has mercy (and He surely will; and if He does not, where will the whole world find a refuge?), repentance can be an atonement for past sins and a substitute for good deeds; but as long as there is breath in the body, all the responsibilities, that is, the duties, stay with a person. It may be that one did not say the prayer standing but said it sitting, or because of the excuse of a journey did not say it in full but said half; water does harm, so one did the dry ablution. In any case the prayer can be more or less, and hard or easy, but it cannot be that the prayer is cancelled altogether. In the same way, even if in this state I cannot do people any other good, still if I say two sweet words to someone, give comfort, ask after the health and welfare of the children, in that alone a person's heart is gladdened; and if you ask the truth, this too is in a way doing good. Granted that now I am not fit to marry; then am I not fit even to advise people and make them understand? Although in religion there is permission for it, nay, insistence, and by reason too there is nothing wrong in it, still, that among Muslims widows do not remarry, for certain they took this evil custom from the Hindus. Women, by being bashful beyond all bounds, have lost their freedom and washed their hands of their right. Men have no thought of it, and no reason to have any thought of it. Only if they were women themselves would they know women's [illegible] worth and recognise their misfortune. No doubt the truly excellent and effective advice would have been that I should not say a single word with my mouth, and should do it and show them.

But now that cannot be; so, well, let me do just this much: call everyone, kin and stranger, and tell them what I have been through. I am sure that people will understand and take warning; and then it is not right to delay either, lest I fail even to do this much. Although the sinus has filled up and for weeks on end even the discharge has stopped, there is not the least lessening of the boiling and the throbbing. It seems that the wound is there, and it would be no wonder if it has turned its mouth inwards, because from the very day the discharge of the wound stopped I have had looseness of the bowels. People call it dysentery; but if it were dysentery, gripes would have gone with it, and here there is no sign of gripes. And after all, why is it that with the looseness the pain in the side grows less? This plainly proves that, as the lady doctor said, the sinus has made its way inwards; and if it is so, and it surely is so, then it is impossible for me to survive this illness. What comfort did I have in the world, that I should be alarmed at dying? Let it be; by dying once I shall at least be rid of these sufferings of every moment. As for the concern of that place, there is neither respite nor strength. Yes, as I go, let me say a word or two to people about widows; perhaps God will accept it; it is nothing far from His gracious appreciation. Having thought this in her heart, she fixed a day and sent a men's invitation among the neighbours, and relations far and near, and acquaintances: "You know of my illness; now, by all outward signs, I have lost all hope of my life, and I do not know on what day and at what hour my breath will leave me. I have a great longing to see my dear ones once more in this life; and I am also about to make a bequest for the worldly and religious good of all of you gentlemen, and it is for this purpose that I have called everyone together. Kindly be sure, be sure to take the trouble, lest this longing of my heart stay in my heart and I take my leave." What a wonder of God it was that from the moment the invitation began to go round, Azadi suddenly became so well that it was as if there had been no illness at all. What she was going to say in the gathering of people she thought out at full leisure; and it would be no wonder if she also wrote down some notes, because to remember so many things in order on the spot is rather hard. But we have not copied this discourse from anywhere: several people were writing down whatever came from Azadi's mouth, and we too were one of them. Then, when we compared it with what the others had written, by God's grace it was ours that came out complete and correct.

Azadi was behind the curtain, it is true, but we heard her make her bequest from so near that even the sound of her breath kept reaching our ear. We assure our readers fully that we have not taken away or added a single letter of our own in this bequest, and the draft written at that time is kept safe with us. On it are the signatures of hundreds of great and trustworthy men, and they bear witness that this is the very discourse, without shortfall or loss, that they heard from Azadi; nay, one gentleman even wrote that the writer of the discourse had done the work of a phonograph[1]. Azadi was, in nobility, in learning, in family honour, in sociability, in every way a woman of such rank that if she sent for anyone falsely without need, he would come running truly; and this invitation of hers was in such terms that people let a hundred affairs of their own suffer, and all of those she had invited were present before the time. Until the bequest had been heard, everyone in his own place set his wits racing: what could it be, for which such preparation was being made? One would say: "She is well off; her mother's family has always been rich; in the days when she was married the whole city talked of the dowry; her in-laws were lower in means, but then her husband earned a great deal in Bhopal, and when he died that same salary was continued in her name. She has had no children; she must want to adopt someone, and she has gathered people for this purpose, so that tomorrow or the day after no one may raise a quarrel."

Second: No, sir, adoption has no basis at all in the law; an educated woman, acquainted with the questions and rulings of the *maulvis*, cannot do such a thing.

First: If adoption has no basis in the law, so be it; but a person surely has this power, to make a gift of his property in the name of whomever he wishes. And she has no children after her; she certainly ought to have such a power.

Second: You do not know her well; I know her very well. She is religious in the highest degree; she will not do such a thing.

First: Is disposing of one's own property against religion?

Second: No doubt, by the religiousness I mean, and such as Azadi Begam's religiousness especially is, even disposing of one's own property is forbidden. If you ask the truth, nothing in the world is anyone's property; whatever anyone has is all on loan, and the true Owner is God and none else. And this way people have of arranging their estate for the future by gift or bequest or division, I do not like that either. It shows that these people love property so much that even after death they want their own arrangement to run in it. God has made a complete arrangement for our estate and fixed the rights of every heir; we have no need to meddle in that arrangement. So God Most High says: "*Your fathers and your sons, you do not know which of them is nearer to you in benefit. An ordinance from God; truly God is All-Knowing, All-Wise.*" You do not know from which of your fathers and sons more benefit reaches you, so that he should have the greater right in the estate, or who deserves to receive more benefit from you. This is God's fixing; surely He is the Knower, the Wise. What I want is that with death no thought of inheritance or heir of any kind should come. Since when has the world been going on! Before us too this property was in someone's hands and he managed it; we took possession and we managed it; whoever takes possession after us will manage it. As the line goes, "*Each one has his turn of five days*." These things I have not heard from Azadi Begam's own mouth, but I know for a fact that her religious ideas are far advanced, and she cannot pay heed to such petty, common things as to adopt someone because she has no children. My guess is that the thought of having no children has never even come to her.

Third: Not adoption; perhaps she wants to make a *waqf* (a religious endowment).

First: But what is this message she sent, that she is about to make a bequest for the worldly and religious good of all of you gentlemen?

Third: You'll see if anything comes out but a *waqf*.

Fourth: Or perhaps she will want to say something about her own shrouding and burial, the grave and so on.

Second: I cannot say what bequest she will make; but adoption, *waqf*, shrouding and burial, it is certainly nothing of that sort. This calling of all the people is for some other purpose; and the secret does not come out why she has not called the women. In any case, why quarrel? If not tomorrow, then the day after, before then everyone will hear what bequest she made.

On the day that people gathered to hear the bequest, at the morning prayer in the mosque of the quarter there was such a crowd of people as was never had even at the farewell Friday of Ramazan. It was fine, mild weather, neither very hot nor very cold. By the time it struck eight in the morning the house had been given over to the men and everyone had come and filled it. A bamboo screen hung before one archway; inside it was Azadi, and round about her a few women, and in all the rest of the house there were men and men packed in.
