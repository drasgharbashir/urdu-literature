# Chapter 2: Azadi Begam's Early Life, Her Education and Upbringing

She was born into a house where the whole family was short of girls. Among these people girls were born rarely, very rarely, and when one was born now and then, people counted her in advance as a guest for a few days; and, as God would have it, within the year their thought would prove true. The sixth-day ceremony, the *aqiqa* (the naming sacrifice) and the first head-shaving are remembered, but birthdays, the first tasting of salt, the hair ceremony and the other festivities that come later, we asked even the old women of the house, and not one could tell of any held for a girl in that family. So Azadi was not only the only daughter but the only granddaughter on both sides, the only niece to her mother's people and the only niece to her father's. When Azadi was born she was strong and healthy, plump through and through, with fine thick curly black hair four and five finger-breadths long. What could one expect of a baby not yet six days old? Yet when you saw her asleep in her mother's lap (dust in the eyes of those who cast the evil eye!) she looked like a well-fed child of a year or a year and a quarter. With the old misgivings, and her looking like this, nobody said she would live; but she had come with a frame so sturdy that it let no sickness come near her. God made her grow quickly. She grew like a vine and spread like a net. At her first birthday she was running about, and by her second she chattered like a myna from Bengal. From her childhood she was so clever that her relatives grew more and more frightened as they watched her uncommon sharpness, which is so often found in children who do not live. Seeing the talk of books in the house, she took a liking to reading of her own accord, and by the age of eight or nine she had learnt and understood thoroughly all that well-born girls in this country need to know and learn. Her upbringing was caught in a tug-of-war between two opposing forces. Her father had received an average English education at the Mission College. Although it was long since he had left school, and after school he had had nothing whatever to do with reading books (which is in general the way of students), the influence of his early education had settled in his heart. He looked on all the ways of Indians with contempt and dislike. English had not gone to his head so far that he would raise a clamour and turn reformer, but even in the small matters of the household he would often pull a face and wrinkle his nose, so that his real views became plain to everyone from time to time. He would go on grumbling, and all the work of the house went on in its own way. In the matter of the children, however, he carried the day.

The custom was that when children were five years old their *Bismillah* (the ceremony of beginning to read) should be held, that they should go to the *maktab* (the mosque school), read the primer, after the primer the last section of the Quran, then the Quran, then Persian. He snapped all this off. What *Bismillah* and what *maktab*? He had the boys taught their letters and set them to Urdu. They gained a smattering of Urdu, began to write names and easy sentences, learnt some multiplication tables, and when meanwhile they had grown a little older he took them and entered their names at the Mission school, where (as the saying goes) there was "neither the sight of God nor the intercession of Muhammad". There was a great stir about it in the family, until one day husband and wife fell to quarrelling over this very thing. There was some talk of the children's education, and it slipped from the husband's mouth: "You look after the children in every other way, but you take no notice at all of their reading and writing. When I came back from my tour I examined them, and neither of the two remembers anything of what he learnt before. Don't they go to school every day, or don't they look at their books at home? The master must be overlooking it out of regard for me, but for how long? If it goes on like this, one day their names will surely be struck off the school's rolls."

Wife: Let that school burn! I pray to God that my children's names, which are to be struck off tomorrow, are struck off today.

Husband: Oho, you seem to be very full of spite against the school.

Wife: Full of spite? For fear of you I have not said a word till now; otherwise, if I had my way, I would not let any Muslim's child so much as turn his face that way. Can anyone go to that school and stay a Muslim?

Husband: That is a new rule of religion I hear today, that going to school makes a man an infidel. Then in your eyes I too am an infidel, and everyone who studied at school is an infidel, and the greatest infidel of all is your cousin, your mother's brother's son, who passed the M.A. examination, and every Muslim teacher is an infidel. But do think a little before you speak. Ask your own father what the ruling is on one who calls a Muslim an infidel. And do you know that if either husband or wife commits unbelief, the marriage is broken?

Wife: How should I know? The whole city is crying shame on us, and people keep coming to my father and needling him: "Such a great *maulvi* (religious scholar) and preacher, and his grandsons enrolled in the padres' school?" As the line goes, "When unbelief rises from the Kaaba itself, where will Islam remain?" From the shame of it I have even stopped going to people's weddings.

Husband: Well, has your father said anything too?

Wife: This much he has said many times: "There are any number of schools all over the city. It was a very wrong thing to enrol the boys in the padre's school. These people teach the boys their own beliefs until they make them irreligious." And Father is very angry, too, that you have not had the children taught the Quran. It is a scandal that Muslim children should not know a single word of the Quran. They are your children; you will answer for it when you go before God. But I tell you two and two make four: in this matter I am no partner of yours.

Husband: Since they cannot understand the meaning, what use would it have been to have them recite the words like a parrot?

Wife: Now these thousands and hundreds of thousands of Muslim men and women read the Quran and do not understand its meaning; in your view they are all fools.

Husband: Certainly. And what proof do you take from their reading? I am a man who believes that even prayer without understanding is no prayer.

Wife: When I said a tiny word against the schoolboys you jumped up and down so much; now you are calling the Muslims of the whole world infidels at the top of your voice. That is your affair.

Husband: How true the saying is: "A half-mullah endangers faith, a half-doctor endangers life." Where is "infidel", and where is "the prayer is not valid"? Well, why argue over that? Each has his own opinion. Tell me this: is it useful for children to read the Quran without understanding it, or not?

Wife: What kind of use are you looking for? The use is just this, that they learn to read the Quran. There is reward and blessing even in the words of the Quran.

Husband: Are children taught the Quran for reward and blessing, or so that they learn to read and write?

Wife: Both are the purpose.

Husband: You may wash your hands of the reward. The Quran, of which God says, "None shall touch it but the purified"[1], is cut up section by section and leaf by leaf and handed over with great disrespect to heedless children, who tear it and spoil it and paw it. The sections they have read from are filthy and soiled, so that one cannot bear to look at them properly. All this disrespect is done to the Quran, and on top of it you expect a reward!

Wife: Of course proper care is not taken, and cannot be taken, but innocent children are not called to account at all.

Husband: If children are not called to account, they get no reward either.

Wife: But when they grow up they will recite it, and later on they will get the reward.

Husband: Without understanding?

Wife: Yes, yes, even without understanding.

Husband: Let your family and those who follow them grumble for the sake of such an empty reward; I will not put my children to this trouble. For the present all I want is that they become fully able to read and write Urdu. And I hope that in the time it would have taken them to finish the Quran, careless as they are, they will be reading and writing good Urdu, and I will show you; and they will learn arithmetic and so on besides.

Wife: I say that even this purpose of yours would have been gained easily and quickly through the Quran itself.

Husband: How so?

Wife: I have not read English like you, and God forbid that anyone should; but do I not understand even this much?

Husband: No, you understand everything. But make me understand too.

Wife: Why do you knowingly pretend not to know? I went through it myself, and I have taught a score of children and seen it. With the help of the vowel marks they soon learn to read fluently; then when Urdu is begun, God knows, they get some knack of it, and I will call it the blessing of the Quran: in three months they are reading Urdu at full speed. Your boys have the curse of not reading the Quran upon them, so that to this day their Urdu does not run smoothly; it is the same stumbling drone.

Husband: That is quite a mistaken idea of yours. Just as a beginner gets the support of the vowel marks in the Quran, so in Urdu he gets the support of understanding the sense. You would feel the difference if there were two children of the same kind of mind, and one were started on the Holy Quran and the other on Urdu, but Urdu with the vowel marks written in; then you would see that the one reading the Quran lies crawling along while the reader of Urdu goes off at a gallop.

Wife: For God's sake, will you ever let me off? Why have you come after me? I have not interfered in your boys' education and I will not in future. As for the girl, she does not have to take service or show off her learning to people. Such as I know, good or bad, I will teach her and train her.

Husband: About the boys I am fairly easy in my mind. They are still too young to understand; it is not the age for keenness; I am not always at home; but I will arrange for their education. Alas for the ruin of Azadi! You will bring her up just like yourself.

Wife: Why, what is wrong with me? Is there rot in me? Don't I know how to sew? Don't I know how to cook? Can't I read and write? Can't I manage a house? Don't I know how to bring up children? Am I a slattern? Am I clumsy? What value will you put on your own words? Ask people, enquire of the neighbours. Not only that: compare the things of my house with those of a house with half as much again, or twice, our income. Sit at home for a few days and see how many women crowd round this "unskilled" person of yours. One comes to have clothes cut out, one for advice, one to ask for a medicine, one to have a letter written or read, one to hear a book read, one to ask a point of religious law. Of all the weddings and other gatherings, great and small, held in your family and mine and in this neighbourhood since my marriage, name a single one to which I was not called to manage it. Don't you know it? If you go back on your word now, in the middle of the argument, it will not go well with you. I swear by eating dust: if Azadi, God keep her alive, turns out like me, then whatever house she goes to, her in-laws will wash her feet and drink the water, you'll see.

Husband: How long have I been telling you, and I tell you again now: just come and take one look at our padre sahib's house, and your boasting will be crushed to grit in a moment.

Wife: Heaven forbid! God forbid! Why should I go to that wretched padre's house? Spare me such affection.

Husband: Why, what is there to be nervous about? Purdah will be kept at the bungalow; there will be the memsahib and her daughters and perhaps an ayah. The poor padre sahib is poorer than we are and has a very large family; he lives in a humble way. There is no rich man's show, nor has he the means for it; but the order, the good management, the cleanliness, the thrift, the skill, the ability, the love and the goodness, you would have to see them to know. If you will come I will send for a carriage; we will be back within an hour, and it is not so cold just now.

Wife: I will neither come nor go anywhere.

Husband: That is just the kind of thick-headed talk I mean. For my sake go once and see; if the least thing happens that is against your liking, I will answer for it.

Wife: Going at all is against my liking. A foreign people, a foreign religion; what ill luck has struck me that, for no reason, I should go among strangers and get myself a bad name in the city?

Husband: But I will be with you.

Wife: Be with me, then.

Husband: Purdah is my right, and you will have to do as I say.

Wife: I fold my hands before you. You cannot force me, can you? If women kept purdah because it was their husbands' right, then unmarried girls and widows would wander about the bazaars with their faces bare. Purdah is not the right of any human being, but the right of God. God has commanded women to keep purdah.

Husband: Right, then, here is a wager between us on this very point. Either you show me in any book that a woman is commanded to keep purdah from a woman; or else, women joining the congregational prayer, going outside to relieve themselves (and outside where? outside the town, as is still the custom in the villages), tending the wounded and serving the soldiers in holy war, speaking and talking with men not of their family: I will prove these things from the hadith (sayings of the Prophet). Look, read, ask; then will you believe it? And who were these women? Not people like you and me, but the wives and daughters of the Prophet and his Companions, may God be pleased with them. You have turned out greater than they are, to start keeping purdah from women! And tell me, madam, don't you show yourself to the sweeper-woman, the washerwoman, the goldsmith's wife, the leather-worker's wife, the water-carrier's wife?

Wife: Of course purdah has become much stricter than before, but then the intentions of the people of old were pure as well. Now people do not behave even with all this strictness; but it is God who keeps one's honour veiled. And as for the argument you took from the sweeper-woman and the rest, our needs depend on them.

Husband: Why, that is the greatest need of all, for which I am taking you to the padre sahib's. When you have met his wife and children and seen his house, your ideas and the whole style of your housekeeping will certainly change entirely.

Wife: God did not make my heart so wavering. I understand my own position and needs very well. I have to live among my own people and die among my own people; what shall I do by looking at the ways of the Frankish women? Let me first come out of purdah, and then I will compete with them too.

Husband: Tell me, don't you get tired of being shut up in the house day and night?

Wife: God forbid, why should I get tired? When I sometimes have to go somewhere in a litter for six or seven pice, all the way there is a dread on me as to when we will arrive.

Husband: Then this verse of Sauda's fits your case:

The tongue fails to give thanks for these broken wings,
Which wiped from the heart the ache to be set free.

Do you remember that parrot too, which used to hang here in the doorway and which the mongoose killed some years ago? You may remember that it often came out of its cage, but the poor thing could not fly. Whenever I saw it like that, I felt sorry for the women of my country, that from lying so long in the prison of purdah their hearts have died. Well, if you will not go, will you let Azadi go?

Wife: (Sarcastically) Yes, take her by all means; her eyes are on the loose as it is. A great lump of seven years, she does not hide from the water-carrier, she does not hide from the dung-cake seller, she does not keep purdah from the servants of the men's quarters or from the hawkers. I keep stopping her, and when she speaks it is so loud that you can hear it clearly in the lane. You will take her, I know; but those who are to be her guardians and heirs will give you and her both a taste of it.

Hearing this, Azadi's father was rather frightened, and the matter was dropped. In the same way there were many arguments over the piercing of Azadi's ears. Azadi's mother spoke truly: "See what a mistake was made! If her ears had been pierced in infancy, she would not now be able to make this fuss that she won't let anyone so much as touch her ears; and the flesh was soft then, and it would have hurt her less." As for Azadi, the moment the ear-piercer's voice reached her ears she would break free and vanish. There were some women in the house hard-hearted enough to have pierced her ears there and then, but Azadi could read their looks. Once or twice she was caught, and then she began throwing herself about and crying for help, and they let her go. Partly there was a dread of it lodged in her own heart, and mostly there was her father's hint and support, for he would say to his daughter, face to face: "Azadi, don't you have your ears pierced. It hurts terribly, and you will ruin yourself; you will be caught in years of bother."

Wife: She would say, "Why tell me? What do I care? Let her go about with her nose and ears bare. We get off cheaply; we won't have to give so much jewellery. That was good advice you gave her, to suit yourself."

Husband: (Turning to his daughter) No, my little daughter, don't you be led astray by your mother. When the Lord brings that day, then instead of nose and ears you may have as much as you like put on your hands and neck. (Turning to his wife) And I expect that Azadi, when she grows up, will not stay foolish like ordinary Indian women, and will understand how much adornment women really need. I am sure she herself will prefer to take banknotes or property instead of jewellery. One great difficulty is that you people do not let reason enter into anything. It seems God has cut down on the making of men; the people of today are not men but sheep: wherever the leading sheep turns its face, the whole flock follows after it. The people of the past who laid down customs may well have been mistaken, for they were not sinless angels but men like us, and what human being does not make mistakes? Or a practice is suitable and sensible up to a certain time, and now the times have changed altogether. God has set over the empire a people who are worthy of it in every way. Under this rule the country has made, and is making, very marked progress. Railways have spread in every direction, and a day is coming when they will be spread everywhere like a spider's web. It is thanks to the railway that we cover journeys of months in weeks and of weeks in hours, without trouble or fatigue. For one pice you may send a letter wherever you like. One of the countless blessings of English rule is the electric telegraph, which, had it been invented in former times, would have been thought a miracle and a marvel. Eight annas is a small thing; Calcutta, Burma, Madras, Bombay, Peshawar: take any place in all India, however far away, and the news is there in the snap of a finger. Schools in every village, hospitals everywhere, hundreds and thousands of comforts and conveniences come ready-made from England, cheap and of the very best. As for peace, the lion and the goat drinking at one ghat was something we used to hear of in stories; now we see it with our own eyes. It was the insecurity of former times that made people hide their means and bury their wealth in the ground, or keep it in the form of jewellery; and then, with little value in it, at the least alarm they would bury it in some corner or tuck it into the waistband and go about with it.

Now every man's life and honour and wealth are fully safe, the poor man's as much as the rich man's, the ruler's as much as the subject's, the traveller's as much as the resident's, the lone man's as much as the crowd's. In short, the rule has changed, the government has changed, the earth has changed, the sky has changed; and the only things that have not changed are our customs. You must have heard this line too: "For gold draws gold into the world, treasure upon treasure." Does that mean that gold and treasure have a power of attraction like a magnet? No, no; it means that wealth earns wealth. There is a common complaint of poverty among Muslims, and Muslims are indeed poor, but the chief cause of their poverty is that they do not know how to make wealth earn wealth. Even if God gives them wealth they will bury it, press it down, hide it, or have jewellery made of it to lie idle and useless; that is, in former times others used to steal it, and now they will steal their own wealth themselves; but it will not happen that they make it grow or earn anything with it.

Wife: If these are your ideas, you will not let your daughter's match be settled anywhere either. And it is your habit; you must certainly have said something among people, and that is why no offer for her has come from anywhere to this day. You are doing your children fine service.

Husband: Is it so late, then?

Wife: How long does it take to be late? It is true that among our people girls are always married in their fourteenth year; but, God be praised, she is growing so fast, what can I say, she will be ready to send off in her twelfth.

Husband: Why, for God's sake! If not eighteen, then sixteen.

Wife: Then why don't you say straight out that you mean to keep your daughter at home unmarried? She is our only child, it is true; but kings and ministers have not been able to keep a daughter at home, so what are we?

Husband: She will certainly stay at home till sixteen, and I say sixteen only because you keep remarking on how fast she is growing. Why, are marriages made without understanding marriages at all? Man and woman should at least be old enough to be able to give their own opinion. I think that below sixteen the capacity for an opinion does not even develop.

Wife: Give their opinion, my foot! Come to your senses, where are your thoughts? Nowadays a woman of sixteen is the mother of two children, if not three.

Husband: And so the children too are weepy-faced and dozy, and so too, compared with other countries, the death rate is higher and the average age lower, and so too there is often a lifelong lack of joy and discord between husband and wife.

Wife: For God's sake don't talk like that. Hearing it, the bloom on the faces of unmarried children fades away.

Talk of this kind went on between Azadi's parents almost every day, and apart from this what else was there to pass their time? These things must have had some effect on Azadi's heart. Whatever little was lacking was made up by the padre sahib's wife. Azadi's father, Khwaja Azad, tried with all his might to open visits between his women's quarters and the padre sahib's house; whenever he got the chance he would speak of it, whenever he found the moment he would argue, but none of it worked. For his people to go to the padre sahib's bungalow was very hard indeed; Azadi's mother would not even agree to so much as the padre sahib's wife or daughters coming to her house.
