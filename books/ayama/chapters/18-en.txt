# Chapter 17: Azadi Sets Out for Her Parents' House, and the Go-Between Has the Palanquin Set Down at Her Own

Azadi got into the palanquin readily enough, but before she was out of the lane she began to have a sort of suspicion: "The palanquin seems somehow new; I have never ridden in it; and the bearers are certainly different: neither that pace nor the same grunting." After a little while it seemed to her that the palanquin's direction had turned another way too; for since she very often went to and from her parents' house, she had a sense of every detail. "I cannot speak to the bearers in the open street; I have none of my own people with me; and now we have been going so long that there is surely something black in the lentils. If I jump out of the palanquin on the way, I shall be exposed to view. If I ask the bearers, crowds upon crowds of people are walking along the bazaar; they will all hear my voice, and if there is anyone I know among them, it will be a great disgrace. But what can anyone do in broad daylight? The bearers must stop somewhere at last; when we get there, I shall see as things turn out. I know we have not gone out of the city, and it is some inhabited place; but if, God forbid, this is the doing of some ruffian, it is a difficult matter. The ruffians of the city are so fearfully bold; what am I, all alone? They untied and carried off the Commissariat's elephant; the English searched and searched till they were worn out, and at last sat down tired. They carried off the police station's gong, weighing a maund or a maund and a quarter, from under a double guard, and to this day no trace has been found. It was last year that the Lord Sahib went to see the Jama Masjid and left his blanket in the carriage; when he came back, the blanket was gone. But the true ruffians have this great merit, that they do not violate anyone's honour, do not betray a trust, and do not keep anything back for tomorrow. On these things there is a very strict watch among the ruffians." Just then the bearers took the palanquin from their shoulders and set it down. Azadi looked through a chink in the curtain and saw that it was the courtyard of a small house, room for some five or six cots, in which the palanquin had been set down. On one side there was a single verandah of timber, in which perhaps a carpet had been spread only that day. Beside the verandah was a closet whose doors were pulled to. No one could be seen outside; whether anyone was hiding inside in the closet, who could tell? The look of the house showed plainly that nobody lived in it; God knows for what purpose it had been swept and a carpet and white floor-sheet spread. Now Azadi was sure that someone, in league with the bearers, had had her palanquin set down in this house. She wanted to run straight back, but she had no *burqa* or sheet to throw over her head; besides, she did not know the name or whereabouts of the place, and God knew what people lived in the neighbourhood. At last, nothing else coming to her mind at that moment, she slipped out of the palanquin and, quick as could be, put up the chain of the entrance hall from inside and the chain of the closet from outside, lest anyone should push in from outside, or someone hiding in the closet should find the latch open and come out.

Then she went and stood close against the doors in the entrance hall to listen. Two or four people could be seen coming and going too, but she did not dare to speak. At last she saw a sweeper woman passing alone; she called her, and when she came near she opened the door and took her inside. The two recognised each other.

Azadi asked: "Why, you wretch, Subhagi, what are you doing here?"

Subhagi: God and the Prophet keep my lady safe! O God, may friends rejoice and enemies be trodden down. My lady, I was drawn here; it is two months since I came to my in-laws' place. My mother is well. Every day I keep saying I'll go, but I can't get free of work. By the blessing of your shoes I have plenty of neighbourhoods. The wretched sanitary men give me no rest, and worst of all the report, that every little thing has to be reported to the government police station. My lady, God keep you as you are, this place is not fit for you.

Azadi: Why, tell me something about it, Subhagi.

Subhagi: There is a go-between called Chhalawa; she is very notorious. She has taken this house; it stays shut up, and has only been opened today. But how did this go-between, God's curse on her, get to you?

Azadi told the whole story: Azmuda's coming, her bringing up Khwaja Mushtaq's name, the uproar people made, her setting out meaning to go to her parents', her coming here by trickery, and her putting up the chains of the closet and the entrance hall. Hearing it, Subhagi said: "No doubt all this is that go-between's doing, and she is very sly. I know her well, and I know all the places where she has made her raids. And, my lady, to tell you plainly, I have earned a good deal from her too; and if she did not keep us people on her side, her business would not run for a single day. But if the cruel woman has played you false, she has done very wickedly. If I had had the least hint, I would have stopped her there and then. And if this is what you wanted, there are a thousand ways to it. Chhalawa is still very well protected; she has fixed monthly payments for all the police station people; but the man who is *thanedar* (sub-inspector) now is a very hard man; he takes nothing from anyone and has nothing taken. He has long been on the watch for Chhalawa; so far she has not fallen into his hands; but, as they say, a hundred days for the thief and one day for the king. God knows what folly she has committed today. If the *thanedar* gets the least wind of it, he is not one to spare Chhalawa: he may clap her in the stocks, impose a fine, blacken her face and have it proclaimed through every street and lane; whatever he does would be too little."

While Azadi and Subhagi were talking on this side, on the other side someone inside the closet was trying to lift the doors off their pivots. In the midst of their talk, because of the rattling of the doors, Azadi and Subhagi had both looked towards the closet several times; but both their minds were on their talk, or perhaps, thinking that some cat must have got shut in, they looked and fell silent. And now, as the talk was coming to an end, they saw that it was not the rattling of a shut-in cat; someone was knocking from inside.

At last Subhagi said: "My lady, the whole business is in that very closet. You were very clever indeed to latch the closet from the front as soon as you came; otherwise you would have been caught in a fearful fix."

Azadi: Subhagi, what can I tell you? As soon as I saw the look of the house a sort of trembling came over my whole body. First I felt like screaming and running away; then I thought: a strange neighbourhood, I have neither sheet nor *burqa*, and above all the fear that there would be an uproar and people would hear of it. At last, nothing else being possible, God gave me sense enough to put up the chains inside and out. And no doubt you are right that the whole mischief is in that closet. Then what do you advise now?

Subhagi: If you say so, I'll go this minute and fetch the *thanedar*; and then the filth of the neighbourhood will be cleared out for ever. He bristles at the very name of Chhalawa; he has told me several times: "You are certainly in league with Chhalawa and keep her secrets; well, some day or other she will fall into my hands." Finding this case he will jump for joy, and it would be no wonder if he got me some reward from the government.

Azadi: But, Subhagi, I don't want my name to come out. Just understand: is even the talk of this not a great disgrace?

Subhagi: It is a disgrace, true; but I'll bring the bearers for you; you get in, and then I'll see to it. My owl hasn't flown anywhere [TN: i.e. I shall still get my profit]: in the first place I'll squeeze something out of them, and perhaps they will open their purses of themselves; and if they don't give, is it a small thing that the *thanedar* will come into my fist?

Azadi: Subhagi, look here, my dear, mind my name is not revealed to anyone.

Subhagi: My lady, what are you thinking of? Whoever plays you false, may God play him false. I am not fit to do you any kind of service, and what harm have you done me, that I should disgrace you for nothing? If I wish you ill, know that there is a flaw in my birth.

Azadi: Well, I am sorry I did not see Chhalawa.

Subhagi: Black-faced creature! May God not show her face to you, or to any gentlewoman. And I say this same Chhalawa came to you as Azmuda. Well, what did Azmuda look like?

Azadi: Her dress was like that of women from outside: tight trousers, bangles on, a long shirt with sleeves, old-fashioned embroidered shoes. I have an idea that her two upper front teeth are bound with wire. A dark complexion, pock-marked, thin, short, and she seems rather sickly too; there is a kind of yellowness in her colour.

Subhagi: Enough, enough! I swear by my dear children, it is Chhalawa. She is a cunning woman; she must have come to you in disguise. All the marks you have described are Chhalawa exactly. Her two front teeth are bound too: dark, pock-marked, thin, short, all exactly Chhalawa. And the yellowness of colour you mentioned: she eats a great lump of opium; she is bound to turn yellow of herself. And look, I'll certainly find out the exact truth of everything and let you know. Now let us learn whether Azmuda really is this Chhalawa or not, and what today's affair was.

Azadi got into the palanquin from there and went straight to her parents' house. It was somewhat late, but no one learnt of the affair in Kunjri Lane [TN: the greengrocer women's lane] except Azadi and Subhagi. Azadi herself had not meant to stay more than two or four days, but the people there kept her back, and only let her go, with great difficulty, after a full month. When Azadi came back to her own house, that commotion had died down entirely: no voice was heard from next door, and no notes and papers came running from outside. Azadi's state was strange too: in the days when her suitors were clamouring from every side she was distressed by the clamour, and now that people had fallen silent each in his place, she wished there would be some overture from somewhere. As it happened, the sweeper woman of her neighbourhood was Subhagi's sister-in-law; through her she sent for Subhagi and complained: "A fine lot of news you gave me!"

Subhagi: My lady, I came to your house twice and went back again; you were at your parents'. And what news was there? You had no sooner gone out of the entrance hall than Chhalawa was standing behind me. It turned out that she had been hiding in the cowdung-cake closet and heard all that you and I said. You got in, and I began looking after your palanquin; Chhalawa came out, first opened the latch of the closet which you had put up, and then came and stood over me and began to say: "Yes, now say it; what were you saying?" What should I say? "So now it has come to this, that you want to have respectable gentlewomen fetched by force and dishonoured? Shame on your station! What is this you have done? And if the *thanedar* hears of it, what a state he will put you in! Don't you know he has long been after you? I'll go and tell him now, and see what a sight he makes of you."

Chhalawa: If you don't go this minute and tell that fancy man of yours, the *thanedar*, a curse on your seven generations! And I'll see what such a weapon he wields. If I don't have his uniform stripped off him, let me not be called Chhalawa. Here's a fine well-wisher of gentlewomen! What need have I, devil take it? Gentlewomen send for me from their homes and beg me hundreds of times before I let them come once. Would anyone today forcibly bring to her place some woman sitting quietly at home?

Chhalawa was saying this when a strapping young fellow came out of the closet. He said nothing, but with a sign of one finger told me to go outside. I would have fought with Chhalawa, but there was no way to make my case without bringing in your name; and there that fellow had come out of the closet. My lady, I put my tail between my legs and ran. After a long while that fellow came out by the gate of the lane, and I followed him, and learnt that he lives here in Jan Nisar Khan's covered alley, and his name is Mushtaq, and he used to come and go a great deal at your house.

Azadi: Why, Subhagi, why would this Chhalawa have had me brought to her house?

Subhagi: My lady, who knows anyone's heart? But the house she lives in herself is a great ship of a place, in which hundreds of people could be stowed away and nobody know. And the house you went to always has a lock on it from outside; she has never brought anyone there or anything. This shows that she had no other purpose than to get you and Mushtaq to exchange a word or two standing there. Then, if you say so, I will make up to Chhalawa and ask her. Don't go by my quarrel with her; we fight and we make it up again.

Azadi: Subhagi, what shall I tell you? My wits are not working at all. When you come again some time, I'll tell you.

Subhagi: I understand everything that is passing over your heart, whether you say it or not; and today, and a year from today, nay, all your life, you will stay in this same turmoil. Well, if you call me even at midnight, the slave girl may make excuses, but I won't. Others come on their feet, and I would come on my head and eyes. But if you would take my advice, make someone you feel at ease with your adviser; tell her the secret of your heart, and let her find some way for it.

Azadi: If I have to take advice on anything, who would be a better adviser than you? You are an old servant of my parents' house and know every little thing about me. Don't you understand even this much, that one can keep *purdah* from everyone, and the people one cannot keep it from are you? And I don't know what God intends, that out of the blue an affair has come up through which you have become my confidante willy-nilly. But look, Subhagi, I ought not to keep saying it again and again, but what can I do, my heart will not rest: take such care that not a soul hears a whisper of it.

Subhagi: My lady, I have given you my word; if it proves untrue by a grain, your shoe and my head.

Azadi sent Subhagi away, and then, when she lay down to sleep, sat thinking over the affair in Kunjri Lane. At first Azadi was very angry: "Who was this low-born scoundrel who has done this to me, that he took it into his head to have my palanquin set down in a strange place without my leave? If there were no fear of disgrace, I would have that shameless ruffian put right in such a way that he would remember it all his life. And alas, Chhalawa did not fall into my hands. Kill her I would not (she goes about looking for a pretext to die anyway), but I would certainly have cut off the wretch's plait, and on top of that given her a good thrashing, so that she would never again dare to lay hands on a gentlewoman. Such lies, such deceit, and such audacity!" As long as Azadi thought of the house in Kunjri Lane, one colour came over her face and another went. She said in her heart: "If I had not put up the chain of the closet from outside, and some man had come out from inside, what would I have done? I had no weapon of any kind. In that agitation the cowdung-cake closet never crossed my mind, and Chhalawa stood hidden at her ease right beside me. Surely she did not come out for fear of me; she knew I would crush her like a mosquito. But it was God too who put that into her heart; otherwise, her own house, her own neighbourhood: she need have done nothing but raise an outcry, and if her supporters had jumped in by way of the neighbouring houses, or lifted the outer door off its pivots, it would have been a very ugly business. In short, God saved me by His power; otherwise that wretch Chhalawa left nothing undone to take my honour. But Subhagi does not suspect her of that; and it is true too: if that was what she wanted, she would have had me set down at her own house, where none of my devices would have availed. But why did she not tell me? Perhaps something of the kind came from my lips that she twisted that way. Yes, no doubt, when she named Khwaja Mushtaq I did say this much: 'Let me think it over by myself, and then I will take counsel with you.' That is just where the water stagnates: I ought to have flared up at her and told her flatly, 'Mind you never mention anyone again.' To say to a go-between, 'Let me think it over, then I will take counsel with you,' is to consent in an undertone. And then, the plain truth is that my mind is made up for a second marriage, and it is not from today but from the start that I have wanted to do it after seeing for myself. And even if Chhalawa did bring a message, she did not bring one from a stranger. Khwaja Mushtaq must have seen me hundreds of times at least. So if Chhalawa found this way of letting the two of us exchange a word or two, she did nothing so very improper; indeed in one sense she did a thing deserving praise and reward; what else could she have done? But would I have gone before Khwaja Mushtaq and talked to him face to face? No, no; the doors of the closet would have stayed closed, Khwaja Mushtaq inside and I outside, and whatever I had to say I would have had Chhalawa say for me. My haste spoiled the whole thing; such a chance never comes to hand again. Well, if I send for Chhalawa again through Subhagi, she will come, of course; that is these people's trade; they have neither shame at lying nor pride at being driven off. But what such need have I, to go about flattering go-betweens and sweeper women? If it is to be done, why be afraid? Let me stop hesitating, and if I reveal my intention to, if not more, then two or four of my close acquaintances, my purpose may be achieved well and gracefully. But so many years have passed for me in this same weaving and unpicking, and nothing has been settled. Subhagi was right when she said, 'You will stay in this same turmoil all your life'; so now I will decide it one way or the other. I have never considered what other women in this situation think.

"Either such thoughts as mine do not come to them at all, and then they are better off than anyone, and if I learn their way I too will contrive something of the kind to be rid of these worries; or other women do have thoughts like mine, and then how do they get through life? These thoughts have made me so restless that there is no end or limit to it. In any case this inquiry will not be without use; something to my purpose will certainly come out of it. There was no lack of widows; I had only to think a moment and scores of widows came to mind."

From then on Azadi made it her practice to seek out widowed women and meet them, and to grow intimate with them and learn what was in their hearts. For several years she was absorbed in this. At last, at the time of her death, she stated: "I happened to meet hundreds of widows, but of four hundred and two women I can say that nothing of their state remained hidden from me."
