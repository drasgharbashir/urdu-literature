# Chapter 12: Azadi Considers Ways of Diverting Her Heart, and at Last Suicide

There, when Azadi found herself free in her own house, her heart too felt a kind of relief. Thinking and reflecting had not been forbidden in her mother's house either; but no, she was glad of just this, that she would not have to see anyone's face. As soon as she got down at the house she told the maids: "Mind, keep the inner door chained all the time, and let no one in." This was the very house in which Maulvi Mustajab had lived for two years, or nearly two, before going to Bhopal. Seeing it, Azadi remembered: "Here he used to sleep, in this place he used to sit"; and today she shut herself in the closet and wept as she longed to weep, with no one to stop her and no one to reason with her. When she had thoroughly relieved her heart, she wiped her tears and came out, and called her old nurse and one or two of the household maids to sit by her and talk. From weeping her head had begun to ache very badly, but her spirits were much lighter than on other days. Her routine was this: she said her prayers on time; as long as her mind stayed collected she recited the Quran; she lent the maids a hand; she went and stood in the kitchen; she lay alone thinking; she sat down to her sewing; she had kept all her husband's letters together, and she would read them. The nurse knew a great many stories, and when she was free of the night's eating and drinking she would begin some story at a suitable hour, and Azadi would listen. While Maulvi Mustajab was there it was of course another matter; after he left she had spent ten months alone in this same house, and never felt desolate or was troubled by loneliness, because there was still the hope of his coming, or the thought now and then that a letter summoning her was about to come any morning or evening. Now, although there were just as many people and the same house, the house stood desolate and empty. At night it seemed as if the walls were closing in on her chest. The day passed without savour, the night went by without pleasure. Sometimes there was no particular memory, but there was a weariness that kept wringing her heart moment by moment, instant by instant. There had been a time when Azadi was averse to marriage itself, and in the days when her betrothal was being discussed she had wished: "It would be better if I stayed unmarried"; for she did not know what marriage really was, and clothes and jewellery and entertaining guests and outward show had no value in her eyes. When she was married she knew that she had been born a second time, and that what deserved to be called life was what began after marriage. She had always had the habit of reflecting on everything; before her marriage too she often used to think: "Although people keep up ties of kinship, relationship, friendship and acquaintance, and have regard and respect for these ties, if one considers it, as long as a child drinks its mother's milk, that one tie is indeed a tie, for without its mother the child cannot get along; and the other ties are mere self-deception and in name only.

"The greatest tie is with parents, and below that with brothers and sisters; and as the child grew older, the tie with parents kept lessening and dwindling, until the children set up separate houses and the parents were outsiders; indeed in some cases such quarrels arise between parents and children that meeting stops and even greetings cease. As for brothers and sisters, perhaps there was some age in which brothers and sisters had love and affection for one another; otherwise the relation is one of rivalry and envy. What day is there when the little ones do not come to blows over sharing out some sweets? What is that if not the stuff of envy? This same childhood bickering, after they have grown up, sets them fighting and quarrelling. The first stain of blood on the face of the earth was when Adam's son Abel [TN: so in the text; in the tradition it is Cain who kills Abel] killed his brother Cain out of envy." In short, until her marriage Azadi did not believe in any tie, and thought that a human being has no real attachment to any of his own kind. No sooner was she married than her opinion changed entirely. Before, she used to wonder: "Human beings are all alike; then why the distinction of man and woman, what purpose is there in it?" When she married her eyes were opened, and she understood that in man there has also been placed a substance of love, which is the food of his soul, the comfort of his heart, and the springtime of the garden of life. She held her new tie very dear, as the food of her soul; she valued it greatly, and was happy when she considered her state. Anyone can understand how hard widowhood must be for such a woman; and indeed from the day her husband died Azadi was neither among the living nor among the dead. She was not mad, to go about picking up straws, nor in her senses enough to take hold of herself. She tried every means to give her heart some rest, but her mind would not settle to any work or be diverted by any pastime. She sits listening to a sermon, and if her attention wanders for a moment she cannot make out what the Maulvi Sahib is saying. Often it happened that she had made her intention for prayer, and however much she wished her heart to be turned to God, she kept forgetting how many *rak'ats* she had said; she did not know whether she had said only the *Alhamd* and gone into the bowing without adding a surah, or whether, God knows, she had added one. She could not remember whether she had made one prostration or two. She sat down with the Quran, and neither did it come back to her from memory nor could she make out the letters. She stayed at her parents' house, and could not settle for a day. She went to her in-laws, and grew desolate there. She came to live in her own separate house, and here too it was the same fire in the bowl.

She gave away all the Maulvi Sahib's clothes and all his books, every one of his things, to the poor, thinking that if they stayed before her eyes they would keep the memory fresh; but this was a mistake in her understanding. The memory came not from outward things but from her own heart; she herself was the one who remembered and the one who reminded, the one who wept and the one who made her weep. For many days after her *iddat* she was in this state, that if she heard people somewhere telling her mother, "Get her busy with some occupation," then, although the thoughts of grief tormented her, she found a kind of pleasure even in that torment, and bristled at the very mention of any other occupation. At last she came of herself to understand this much, that "this state of mine is a very bad state. The world, well, it has been ruined as it has been ruined; but behind this grief my religion too is going to ruin. And what have I achieved by grieving so much, and what shall I achieve if I go on grieving like this all the rest of my life? When I take the measure of my nature, this grief will be with me as long as I live. People say, 'Set your mind to some occupation'; they know nothing of my nature. When do I want to keep myself mixed up with the dust like this? But if the heart itself is not under one's control, what remedy is there for that? I don't know what occupation people propose for me. Shall I keep pets? I kept plenty of pets in my childhood. Once I had two cages crammed full with three score red munias (avadavats), such wonderful singers that all day long it seemed as if an organ were playing. When eight or ten sang together the verandah rang. Winter came, and they began to die of themselves; I tried many remedies, but every morning when I opened the cage I found two or three dead. From that day to this I have never spoken of keeping munias again. I sent for a young hill parakeet from Dehra Dun and wore out my head over it for a year and a half or two. 'Mithu, my son'; 'send blessings on the Prophet'; 'God is the Truth, God is the Holy Being'; 'Brother, it's God, and God's Prophet'; 'Don't be heedless, don't forget God'; 'Qutb, Qutb the Divine'; 'Food to the hungry, water to the thirsty': it said all these things so well that we of the house ourselves often doubted whether it was the parakeet speaking or some child. It knew the names of everyone in the house, and talked with each according to his worth. Its talking was famous from neighbourhood to neighbourhood, so that every day two or four new people came to hear it talk. However much I hid it, lest someone's evil eye should fall on it, when people begged hard I would perforce bring the cage and hang it in front of them, and the parakeet would talk for hours.

"One day what do I see but that, in the middle of its talk, the parakeet, against its habit, squawked two or three times like a wild parakeet, fell off its perch and was dead. I quickly opened the little door and took it out, and it was stone cold [TN: reading uncertain]. The parakeet's death was such a blow to me that I did not eat for several days from grief; and my nature too is so weak that it cannot bear grief at all. In short, I took a vow against keeping parakeets too. Some great man had come from the Bengal side and had presented Grandfather with a myna. He knew I was fond of pets, and Grandfather gave that myna to me. I simply cannot tell you how marvellous that myna was. It spoke five languages, Arabic, Persian, Urdu, Bengali and English. I did not understand Bengali and English, but Arabic, Persian and Urdu I could understand clearly; and so tireless that it never stopped; take it up at midnight and it was ready to talk. I knew that mynas get a 'thorn' [TN: a disease of the throat] and often do not survive it. For this one, may God not make me lie, I collected scores of remedies and kept medicines ready, so that at the least sign of the thorn I might treat it. The thorn came, and I tried remedies, but the myna's hour had come, and at last the lamp of its life was to go out, and it did. I grieved so much over the myna's death that even now, if I weep an hour for my husband, I weep a minute for the myna. The fact is that man has no full knowledge of the temper and nature of animals; a person does what he thinks is good, and it turns out bad. God knows whether being shut in a cage hurts them, or whether not being able to meet their own kind causes them distress. In any case there is certainly some reason why pets so often pine and waste away and die before their time. If animals' blood is shed for nothing, how great a sin before God it must be! And the death of a pet: only the heart of one who has kept pets knows the blow of it. So keeping pets is needlessly putting oneself into a tangle. Who would buy such a headache when sitting at ease? One plan is this: the courtyard of the house is big enough; I could lay out a pretty garden in it. That is not free of bother either, but there is no doubt that it is a thing of recreation. But recreation for how long? An hour or two in the morning, an hour or two in the evening, and not all the year round at that. In the days when the hot wind blows in summer, or when the autumn comes, it is very depressing to see the trees withered and stripped bare. No, this too is no cure for my desolation.

"Of the women I know and the women related to me, most are of the opinion that I should adopt someone's child. In the first place I cannot think of anyone who would hand over their child to me. Ask even those who, God keep them, have four or five, and they will say they would like as many more. It is the same proverb: has anyone ever had their fill of bread and children? And then my state is such that if superstition does not come into it, it may well [TN: i.e. as a widow she would be thought unlucky]. And if someone, out of pity for me, or tempted because I am alone and have a fixed income of thirty rupees a month, did give me their child, I do not find so much room in my means. An Indian court and the giving of favours: who knows what may happen, whether the allowance will come in future or not; then how shall I meet the expenses of the one I adopt? If it is a girl, then in my fallen state she will need at the very least two thousand rupees for her marriage and wedding procession; and if it is a boy, then besides the marriage and procession at least as much again for his education; and two thousand rupees I do not see being collected here in two thousand years. If, relying on my parents or my in-laws, I take on someone else's child, could anyone be a greater fool than I? And if, when the time comes, it cannot be done, what will the one I adopted say, and what will those who hear of it say? The child will curse me, and they will spit on my whole existence. And even if I did adopt someone, I might have the parents write me a thousand deeds of release and have them renounce their claim before the elders, but I cannot break the natural tie. That would mean that in the eyes of the child's parents, or whoever is his guardian and protector, I would be the one who had insured the child's health and life. And if I had such power over God, why would I have let my own husband die young? Besides, adopting a child is a sheer sham. Bring it up and rear it, take trouble, live in hardship yourself and give it comfort, only in the hope that it will be called ours; and in the end it will be called theirs whose it is. 'The elephant is led from village to village, but it bears its owner's name.' My mother keeps longing and longing over this: 'Alas, she had no child'; and I say it is well that I had none. If I had had a child before me today, it would have been a double calamity: should I weep for my widowhood or for its being an orphan? And then, boy or girl, what certainty is there how a child will turn out who has no father over its head? And if there had been a child and it had turned out bad (and the way things were, it would certainly have turned out bad), then, well, my life has been ruined as it has been ruined, but the honour and good name of the family would have been in danger. No act of God is without its purpose; I am very grateful to Him that I have been spared that calamity.

"All deaths are to be grieved for, especially this death. What an age, and far from home, and how sudden, and what goodness and what ability! In every respect it was such a calamity that even a stranger who heard of it stayed stunned for hours. But there was a general regret that he left no name or trace, that is, no children. But when his own life was gone, what would he have done with a name and trace remaining? That too is a worldly idea; otherwise, the very children for whose sake people do not care even about the ruin of their religion, those same children, when their time comes, do not acknowledge their parents' kindness even as much as a neighbour's; they answer back, are insolent and disobedient, give them pain, and are counted as a name and trace! Seeing the ways of the age, my heart has been so soured towards children that I never longed for a child, and even now I have no regret at all at having no child, nor any thought of adopting one. If there was perhaps any way of diverting my heart, it was this, that I should open a *maktab* for girls. It would have been a good occupation, and a meritorious thing too; but if my head is not up to it, from whose house shall I bring another? God knows what has happened to my brain, that if anyone speaks a little loudly my brain begins to shake inside. I could not possibly bear even the noise of a *maktab*. And if my brain were fit for it, I would set about learning the Quran by heart, so that by its blessing my heart might be steadied too. In short, there are means of diverting the heart, but some are not fit for me and for some I am not fit. So it does not seem to be God's will that I should live; and when is such a life right anyway? I am grieving all the time; that means that I am sulking and angry with God: 'Why did You call my man to You and set me down as a widow?' Then where is any religion or faith left? I may not say it with my mouth a hundred thousand times, but God knows the thefts of the heart, so that in His sight I have become a sinner, since I cannot bear to see any married woman now. I am filled with longing: 'Her husband is alive; what such fault had I committed, that it was my husband who had to die?' I understand everything, that envy is such an evil thing that, as fire burns wood to ashes, so envy destroys all good deeds; but what can I do? Such thoughts come into my heart of themselves; I cannot stop them, I cannot deny them. So rather than such a worthless, absurd, wretched and afflicted life, it is better to die.

"At least I shall be rid of it for ever. But the one thought that comes is that to die an unlawful death is also a great sin. Then I think that as that is an unlawful death, so this is an unlawful life; indeed dying is dying once; if a person died an unlawful death, he committed one sin, while in living like this there is, besides sins, the fear of unbelief. I have not seen this particular case in any book; if I ask some *maulvi* the point of law, he will at once see through it; and then, if it is decided that I must die, God has made living hard, and people will make dying difficult. What need is there to ask anyone? I have heard in a sermon that a person's heart is a very truthful giver of rulings; why should I reveal my secret to anyone? This thing has come into my mind for the first time today. I shall think it over well, and then whatever I understand I shall go and do. But suppose I have decided to die (and so far it seems to me that there is no escape from this affliction without dying), then what way of dying shall I choose? I have heard of people dying in several ways; some of these ways can be managed only by men, and not by all men either, but by those whose hearts are very hard, such as cutting one's throat with one's own hand. Low-caste Hindu women throw themselves into wells and drown, and they have the chance to because they do not keep *purdah*. They probably do not even think that people will of course pull their corpse out, and that is a thing to raise an uproar through the whole city, and I want a quiet death. Many people take some kind of poison, such as nux vomica, arsenic or something else. I have heard that with nux vomica a person dies of purging and vomiting. That is a very hard way to die, and I don't want that. And yes, if it is found out early, the patient may even be saved by treatment. Arsenic is a very strong poison; with it the liver comes out in pieces by way of the mouth, and the life goes out with great agony. Once there was an uproar in our neighbourhood that someone, God knows by what mistake, had taken arsenic. It was found out quickly and they began giving him bowlfuls of ghee to drink. At last the man did survive, but his whole body had broken out in sores. The best and easiest way of suicide that one often hears of is to take opium. The other poisons are rather hard to get, and the government forbids their sale; but opium is a thing anyone can bring whenever he likes. They say that an anna or an anna and a half's worth of opium finishes off a good young man; so I need not even trouble about it.

"I know that in the nurse's little bag there is always four or five annas' worth of opium. She swears a thousand oaths on her side that she has never let the opium grow bigger than a lentil, and that only once a day; but who believes what she says? Every month her son comes to the door and quarrels: 'If you would give me once, to put me to sleep, as much opium as you eat in two days, my sin would be wiped out. This is lying on my head, and in front of your habit you care for nobody. Whenever I asked you for anything, you gave me a flat refusal.' In short, the opium is as good as in my own purse; I can take it whenever I like. And since it is an intoxicant, a person who takes too much must surely become unconscious, and in unconsciousness must feel nothing of the agony of death. He took it at night and lay down, and stayed asleep as he lay. But there is this drawback in it too, that if it is found out before it is digested, the doctors pump the whole of the opium out of the stomach, and among us too people make one vomit and do not let the opium stay. And if, in a case of suspicion, someone goes and reports it to the government, they even cut open the dead body. That is a great scandal: one's life is lost, and on top of that an honour worth hundreds of thousands is dragged in the dust. But even so, taking opium is better than the other ways. What hurry is there? I shall think over this matter again some other time."

Whenever Azadi thought about it, it seemed to her that there was no way out without suicide. When she looked at herself, it seemed to her that she was a house without a dweller, a ring without its stone, a garden without anyone to gather flowers, a throne without anyone seated on it. So at last she decided: "Life and widowhood, and cheerfulness and piety: I cannot in any way keep these things together." She was so weary of her state that, far from fearing and hesitating, she longed to die with a thousand lives. If she hesitated at all, it was only lest her hereafter should be ruined. A great mark of wisdom is that the wise do not do even the smallest thing without taking others' advice, and this was a matter of staking one's life. Azadi was restless: "Whom shall I ask, whose advice shall I take?" In this distress one night she dozed off, and what does she see in a dream but that she has made the ablution and said two *rak'ats* of the prayer for guidance, and prayed; and as she opened the Quran to take an augury, at the beginning of its first line was: "*And do not kill yourselves*" (do not destroy your own lives); and in the margin it was written, citing some hadith, that whoever kills himself in whatever manner will remain in that same torment for ever and ever. The moment she saw this dream she started up and rose, and understood that it was not God's command. She was certainly somewhat sorry at this, that one means of escaping this affliction had come to her mind and that too had not succeeded; but she gave great thanks to God that by His grace and kindness He had saved her from the torment of the hereafter, and she repented for the future: "Even if I die choking and choking, God willing I will never, even by mistake, think of suicide again."

For months Azadi kept people from coming. People, considering her widowhood, hesitated anyway to go to her house: "Why should we go, and why trouble the poor creature?" And Azadi herself would go nowhere. She had been seized by such desolation that she could not bear even to see people's faces. And indeed, what can a person who is weary of their own life gain from meeting people? Now, since God had overthrown one great plan of hers, she wished to meet people and mix with them, to say her own say and hear others', in case something useful came out in the course of talk. When people learnt that the ban had been lifted, the neighbours, who had long been watching for a chance, came flocking in as soon as they saw the door open; and now the shape of Azadi's life as a widow was set.
