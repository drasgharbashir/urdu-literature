# Chapter 6: Azadi's Opinion about the Maulvis and about Religion

Azadi never said anything aloud from which one could tell what she thought of this disagreement between her parents. But from her age, and from the way she grew rather sad on hearing of her betrothal among the *maulvis*, one gathers that if she was not set against the *maulvis* like her father, she did not have her mother's sweeping devotion to them either. People put her low spirits down to this: "She is not an ordinary girl, to be pleased with jewellery and clothes; God bless her, she is very sensible; she must be having some thoughts about the marriage." Azadi's thoughts now turned in another direction. She reflected: "Just as they betrothed me without asking, so one day they will have the marriage read without asking too; and what is the difference between betrothal and marriage anyway? Among gentlefolk, what is said with the mouth is as good as a marriage read. Well then, what ought I to do now? The matter is delicate, the case is difficult, and there is no chance of taking anyone's advice." Sitting alone she would think to herself in her heart: "Such a great change is about to come in my life; must I make no preparation at all for it? Until now I have lived in my parents' house so free of care that I have not known where the morning rises and where the evening falls. I have had no worry about food and no trouble about clothing; I have eaten what was cooked for me and worn what was sewn for me. The people among whom I lived and was brought up and grew, every one of them has always held my heart in their hands. Father is such that he will not let me out of his sight for a moment. If I go and stand for the least little while at the neighbours', there is an uproar. Father sets foot in the entrance hall and calls for me. Not one of my brothers can bear that a speck of dust should come into my eye. It is only some such necessity that has made them accept parting from me; but what of it? Father is silent, my brothers too look sad, and Mother, hiding it from me, has a cry two or three times a day. Nobody has ever had, or can have, the comfort of her parents' house. I too have seen plenty of married girls; when they left their parents' house they went weeping. But when a state of things is coming, and will certainly come, I know it will certainly be useful to make my mind ready for it beforehand, and even to grow used to it."

Now she began, in her own way, to look into the lives of married women, and she also went carefully over what she had glanced at in books; and she found that with marriage a wholly new kind of life begins: it is, as it were, a second birth, as the Hindus believe. The old ties, if not all at once then by degrees, fall away and vanish, and new ties attach themselves to one. In place of mother and sisters there are mother-in-law and sisters-in-law; instead of father and brother, father-in-law and brother-in-law; and there is one person more, who stands in place of no one, with whom there is a special kind of tie, and who is the root of all the new ties; or rather, let us say, all the old and new ties put aside, there remains this one tie which has to be kept up all one's life. This is a woman's destiny and this her fortune; this is her trouble and this her blessing. If there is sweetness in this tie, there is the pleasure of the world, ease, honour and good name; and if, God forbid, there is no joy in it, then besides ill fame and disgrace, life is bitter. "So the great thing I have to do after marriage is this: somehow to win over the person with whom I have so strong a tie, and bring him into my power. It was for this very day that I was so restless, wanting them to ask me before tying me to anyone's hem. Well, that did not happen, and could not have happened; but am I then to sit patiently until I get a chance to learn his temper? And if in my ignorance I do something that displeases him, and the foundation of a quarrel is laid, then where will I be?" In this train of thought the same ideas began to return that she had, in defeat, driven out of her heart by force; but her nature was such that they kept her restless. She would say: "If only I could somehow get one glimpse of his face, I would set my wits to work. Nobility, gentleness, forbearance, goodness, a cheerful nature, good manners: whatever a man is, his looks speak it out, and a man is known by his bearing. The maid from my future in-laws' house comes every day without fail to ask after our welfare, and sits for hours talking of things there. I am not allowed to go before her; otherwise from her talk alone I would find out everything. Well, this much is a mercy, that they are *maulvi* people. Although Father runs down the *maulvis* dreadfully, his objections are of another kind; and it is also true that not all *maulvis* are alike, any more than all ignorant men are the same. No doubt some *maulvis* keep fasts and say prayers for show, preaching to others and disgraced themselves; but at least their outside is good. If a man does a thing a hundred times for show, the real thought of it will come to him at one time or another. Is it better that both the outside and the inside should be bad? Besides, regard for appearances also keeps a person from many evils. This objection of Father's I cannot understand. And the one thing worth a hundred is this: when *maulvis* are written in my fate, and I must go to a *maulvi*'s house willy-nilly, then if I start now looking at their faults and turning my heart against them, it means that the beginning of the fight, the preface to the quarrel, came from my side, and then how on earth shall I win anyone over? Besides, the way to win someone over is to be won over oneself, and that cannot be unless their good qualities are fixed in my heart like a carving in stone. And whatever faults the *maulvis* may have, they are not bad at household life; and what more do I want?

"Whenever I go to my mother's family, I see quarrels between husbands and wives coming before Grandfather again and again; but as for seeing any quarrel of that kind among the *maulvis*, I have never even heard of one. Yes, there is that story of Maulvi Muhammad Umar, which I heard from God knows whom: that he divorced his wife. Even that shows the Maulvi Sahib's piety of the highest order. It happened long ago and I had almost forgotten it; now when I think, I remember that the Maulvi Sahib was very God-fearing and abstinent, and night and day had no work but worship. He loved his wife very dearly too, and why should he not? That itself was the mark of his being religious at heart. He had three or four children by that wife. The Maulvi Sahib, as was his habit after a meal, was lying down reading a book, when some lady related to his in-laws came and began to describe her husband's state: 'Today is the fifth day; he has such a fever that one cannot put a hand on his body. I have brought this cup; breathe a prayer over the water, and if there is any medicine, tell me.' The Maulvi Sahib breathed a prayer over the water, and looked in the *Prophetic Medicine* and told her a remedy. On hearing the name of the medicine, the lady of the house spoke up:

"Wife: Oh dear, look, heaven forbid you give this medicine; it is very heating, it is fire. Mind it doesn't go to his head and bring on brain fever.

"Maulvi Sahib: I have told the medicine from the *Prophetic Medicine*; God willing, by the blessing of the Prophet it will do him good.

"As ill luck would have it, the wife began to say: 'There is this fearful heat, and such a strong fever, and you say mix nigella seed in honey and give it him to lick? No, sir, that is not my advice.'

"Maulvi Sahib: When I cite the *Prophetic Medicine*, to dispute it is against the dignity of Islam.

"Even so the wife went on saying her own say. The Maulvi, in a fit of religious zeal, divorced his wife on the spot; and husband and wife both died within the year, grieving for each other. But that was a special case, and no blame of any kind falls on the Maulvi Sahib in it. Worldly people, to tease the *maulvis*, call them henpecked; but let someone ask their wives' hearts! I think no *maulvi*'s wife would even like to be a queen. Among the rich you see only the outward jewellery, the clothes and the servants; God is the master of hearts. The husband is sulking apart and the wife sits pouting by herself. For weeks no greeting, for months no talk. Is that any kind of life? Far better hunger with harmony, want with one heart. Now there remains the question what the *maulvi*'s calling is in itself. Father certainly looks on it with contempt; but even that contempt is not, God forbid, because the *maulvis* are in any way lower than others in birth and family, or because learning is not a thing of worth and honour, but only because some *maulvis* are hypocrites and greedy. That is a fault not of the calling but of those particular men in whom the vice of hypocrisy and greed is found. In the same way plenty of men in service take bribes and rob people of their rights; is service then to be thought base? No, no. And the vice of hypocrisy and greed is mostly found in those *maulvis* who are only half-baked mullahs and have begun to be called *maulvis*, so that to make up for their lack of ability they need improper devices. Against the recognised scholars, like our grandfather or my elder uncle, not even Father can say a word; and if he found an opening, would he ever be the one to miss it? Although people turn against the *maulvis* through studying English, by God's grace the real *maulvis* are still respected just as before, and God willing will be respected for ever and ever. It is not the *maulvis* that people respect; rather, if you ask the truth, they respect religion, they respect God and God's Prophet. And I say, whatever a *maulvi* may be like, even if he does not respect himself, since he knows his own state, still God fills his belly with honour. Look at other people of the same income and the same standing, with what hardship and disregard they earn a living. God has given learning nobility; whoever has it, in whatever form, that nobility goes with it. Father finds fault with the *maulvis*; what do I know of the ways of strange men? But as far as I have seen women and heard about them, the women of worldly people are no match at all for the women of the *maulvis*. These are human beings, those animals; these are angels, those devils.

"At the wedding of Khwaja Miran Sahib's daughter, women of every kind were gathered in great numbers. He himself, as it happens, is a rich man, and great ladies from his estates had come as guests; and his wife, by birth and blood a *maulvi*'s daughter, had the *maulvi* women of her own family in one verandah. There I had a thorough view of both kinds. All living in the same city, and all women, and yet in looks, dress, style, speech, sitting and rising, walking and talking, the one did not resemble the other in the least. I saw the great ladies: hair plastered flat down to the eyebrows, ringlets hanging, buns tied, eyes thick with smoky *surma* (collyrium), *missi* (black tooth powder) on the teeth and red lac over the *missi*, a big roll of betel tucked in the cheek, a beauty spot drawn on the chin, spangles on the forehead, henna on hands and feet, laden with seers of jewellery, their own and borrowed (may God not make me lie), one pair of trousers for the whole women's quarters, and from head to waist as good as naked. Swaggering as they walked about, putting on airs as they rose and sat, slandering this one, running down that one, a jibe here and a gibe there, abuse for the slave girls, a scolding for the maids. A shoe for this one, a kick for that; first the oath and then the words. Practised in puns and repartee, with shame and modesty laid up on the shelf. I tell you truly, it was they who behaved with such vulgarity and frivolity, and it was I who felt ashamed, that women should be so brazen, so lacking in all decency. About half of them, whenever you looked, were standing in the entrance hall. Young girls of my age and older were standing, in name only, behind the door-leaves, buying sweets without any shyness and eating and tasting them; and those who were inside the house made a din worse than the street and louder than the bazaar. I don't know whether their throats were split, or whether in their view even shouting when they spoke was a medal of gentility. Then the rudenesses they committed at meals are not to be told. A fresh, unused cloth taken out and spread, and in one sitting soiled and filthy. They did not eat as much as they scattered and pilfered. The white floor-sheet was changed twice a day, and whenever it was taken up it was found stained all over with spittle of betel. Well done the lady of the house, that she saw all this with her own eyes and the poor woman never said a word. In short, in those great ladies I did not see one thing of decent humanity; every action was unseemly and every manner vulgar. Watching the great ladies I kept thinking: what must the men be like whose women's quarters are full of such filth?

"God save us from their shadow, and may He not settle any decent person in their neighbourhood. Right opposite them, across the courtyard, was the verandah of the *maulvi* women. There were so many people on that side too that there was no room to set down a sesame seed, but who dared to let her voice be heard? Leave aside the outer curtains: no one in the courtyard would have so much as suspected that there was anyone in the verandah. Even the girls were so well behaved that each one you looked at sat quietly against her mother's knee. They did not ask for sweets, they did not make a noise to play. It was a daughter's house, and a rich daughter too, and for the daughter's name the *maulvi* women had gone well dressed and adorned, and in clothes and jewellery every one of them was fitting to her station. Costly suits, heavy jewellery, and, each according to her age, who does not comb and plait her hair, and on such an occasion besides? But the thing was that there was sobriety in everything about them. Waves of scent came from their hair, but the plait was plain: no flat-plastered hair, no ringlets, no bun. The fragrance told you their clothes were steeped in perfume, but there was no scented cotton in the ear. The cloth was costly, but modest and according to the sacred law. They wore their clothes in such a way that for a long while I watched for a chance to see what kind of earrings one lady had, and could not see them, simply could not see them. Two or four ladies, from the moment they got down from their palanquins, I found sitting on their prayer-mats every time I looked. Among the *maulvi* women too there was some disorder, running here and there; but morning and evening, when the time of prayer was short, all of them rose at once for the ablution. The lady of the house, out of regard for her family, showed great consideration for the *maulvi* women and asked them first of all for breakfast and dinner; but the answer from them was: 'What hurry is there for us? Feed the others; we are very ashamed already that you do not give us any work, when it is for just such a day that one's own people exist. One of us would pour water for the guests' hands, one would spread the cloth, one would stand with water, one would serve the food. You have made us sit like idlers; sitting idle, one gets bored.' I listened hard to the talk of the *maulvi* women, and it was all about God and the Prophet. One said: 'Look, an eloquent *maulvi* from outside has come; his speech is like the people of the East, but praise be to God, how he preaches! However stony a heart may be, once at least it is bound to become as soft as wax. He described Paradise and Hell, and the men wept till they sobbed.'

"The second: 'Yes indeed; God has given His word just such power and blessing. Did you invite him to your house to preach?'

"The first: 'No, the Maulvi Sahib has come and is staying in our own mosque. His practice is to teach his pupils the translation of the Quran after the morning prayer; that is his preaching. Come one day and listen. Our house adjoins the mosque, and every little thing in the mosque can be heard sitting at home.'

"The second: 'I would certainly come, but there is a rush to get the meal ready early for going to the court.'

"The first: 'Why, is all well?'

"The second: 'That same quarrel over *Amin* has hardly been settled yet.'

"The first: 'Yes, I did hear something of it; but is your husband on one side in that case too?'

"The second: 'Why, the real dispute is with him. It was he who began to say *Amin* aloud. People stopped praying behind him, and then barred him from coming to the mosque. Then there was a fight among the men, and it came to blows. The case was fought in the criminal court for two months; there were fines, and bonds were taken. Now a civil suit for the trusteeship of the mosque has been filed; let us see what happens.'

"The first: 'Why, look, did you in the end find out whose fault it is? The affairs of *maulvis* only *maulvis* understand; our understanding is of no use; we are blind, we go whichever way someone points. Don't be offended, but one plain thing I do understand: people have been praying in one way for hundreds of years, and no age has been without *maulvis*; so what are these new things we hear of now?'

"The second: 'I am not learned enough to understand the arguments of both sides, but yes, when the fight broke out over saying *Amin* aloud, I quarrelled with my husband a great deal. At last he said: "Prayer is not something one may say whenever one likes and however one likes. Prayer is God's, and He taught it to the Prophet through the angel Gabriel. It has come in the hadith that first Gabriel led the prayer and the Prophet prayed behind Gabriel as a follower, and then the second time the Prophet became the leader and Gabriel the follower. So we ought to see what kind of prayer it was that the Prophet learnt from Gabriel and said and led. That will be found from the books of hadith, which contain the Prophet's sayings and doings. There are several points of difference in the prayer. First, the *Amin*: we say it aloud and the Hanafis softly. Second, the raising of the hands: before and after the bowing we raise our hands as at the first *takbir*, and they do not. Third, behind the leader we recite the Fatiha in every *rak'at*, and they stand silent. Fourth, the sitting of rest: before standing for the second and fourth *rak'at* we sit for just a moment, and they rise from the prostration and stand straight up. Fifth, behind the leader we stand foot to foot, and they stand apart from one another. Well, as for saying *Amin* aloud, the Hanafis too have some traditions, though weak ones; but against raising the hands and joining the feet there is no authority in any book; it is sheer high-handedness and obstinacy on their part." Hearing this I said: "If people take it ill, what is the necessity? Pray the way other people pray; after all, you prayed that way for so long." He gave such a ready and reasonable answer to that, that I was left quite silent and understood that it is the Hanafis who are in the wrong. He explained to me gently: "Don't object to me that I prayed the Hanafi way for so long. That was my negligence. Like ordinary Muslims I too believed that Imam Abu Hanifa, Imam Shafi'i, Imam Ahmad ibn Hanbal and Imam Malik are the four pillars of the building of religion and the four elements of the body of Islam, that the truth lies within these four, and that I had no need to inquire for myself. And since I was born in a Hanafi house, I too was a Hanafi, and following Imam Abu Hanifa was enough for me. So I studied only Hanafi law, and whatever Imam Abu Hanifa said on any question, I went on taking for revelation from heaven. Until I began the hadith; and the more I mastered the hadith, the more my eyes opened, and I thought: God sent down one law upon His Prophet, may God bless him and give him peace, and at the last said: '*This day I have perfected your religion for you*': today I have completed your religion. The Prophet lived for some thirty years [TN: so in the text] after the coming of revelation, and all that time was spent in teaching religion. All that God said is gathered in the Quran, and all that God's Prophet, may God bless him and give him peace, said and did, in the hadiths. So for the completion of their religion Muslims need first the Quran, and after the Quran the hadith.

"'"I do not know where these four Imams jumped in from in between. If there was some good reason for not naming themselves after the Prophet, then they should have taken the name of a Companion, and one would call himself a Siddiqi, one an Umari, one an Usmani, one an Alawi, one an Anasi, one an Abu Hurairi and so on; that would at least have made some sense, for the Prophet said in praise of his Companions: 'My Companions are like the stars; follow whichever you will, and you will not lose the way.' They left the Prophet, turned away from the Companions and the Successors, and landed all at once on the Successors of the Successors, like someone who skips two steps of a staircase and falls with a thud on the third. I consider it a kind of disrespect to the Companions, God be pleased with them all. In the same way, about those who keep the death-anniversaries of other saints, such as Hazrat Piran-i Pir, or Qutb Sahib, or Sultan Ji Sahib, I often think that after the Prophet comes the rank of the Companions, after the Companions the Successors, and after the Successors the Successors of the Successors: '*then those who come after them, then those who come after them*.'[1] These saints are, compared with them, simply members of the community. They are no doubt good, very good, exceedingly good, compared with us black-hearted sinners, and we cannot equal even the pure dust of their feet; but, as the line goes, 'If you do not distinguish between ranks, you are a heretic.' With all that, they cannot reach the Companions, who stood by the Prophet in hardships, who for the sake of religion left their homes, left kin and kindred, left wealth and goods, who sacrificed their lives for the Prophet. So this is my belief. If I say anything wrong in it, let someone convince me. I say my prayers to God, and the way I have adopted after careful thought, even if people drive me out of India, nay, off the face of the earth, I will not give up a sesame seed's worth of it. If today I give up *Amin* because the Hanafis say so, tomorrow prayer to please the Hindus, and the day after the Unity of God for the sake of the Christians, then it is not religion but a children's game."'"

Recalling these things, Azadi kept leaning towards the *maulvis*; but after all she was a young girl. One day it occurred to her: "Whatever the *maulvi*'s life may be, it is certainly dull and cheerless. It is not that there are no people of means among them; there are, but very few; and the trouble is that even those who have means live like the poor, or rather worse than the poor; for the poor man has not a farthing to his name, and is shabby out of the helplessness of want, but the *maulvi* is well off and yet poverty and wretchedness rain from his face. I don't know whether, as Father says, they make themselves look miserable on purpose for show, so that people will pity them and give them something, or whether this too is part of humility. In any case one must first starve one's belly, reduce one's body to dust, become old in youth and poor in wealth, before one can speak of joining the *maulvis*. And these are my days for eating and dressing; did this much not occur to Mother, and where has she gone and trapped me? Did she not think that this girl too has a heart, and in her heart longings? That she too is a human being, and what desires a person has at this age? After all, this time once passed over her too; she could have remembered her own time and understood. And even now, what has she given up? Does she not wash her hair every Friday? Or put on henna every fortnight? Or wear new bangles every month? Or wear dyed scarves? Ha! If she were in some *maulvi*'s house today, we would see how dressed up and adorned the lady would keep herself!"

These thoughts kept Azadi suspicious of her mother and troubled about her betrothal for weeks; but a person's heart does not stay the same all the time. She steadied herself once more; and, having heard of someone's death, God knows whose, she was gripped by the thought of the impermanence of the world, and she reflected: "What is the world? Millions upon millions of people are born, live up to a certain time, and in the end pass away. It is exactly like a crop. The peasant ploughed and sowed the land and left it; God sent rain. Only a few days ago dust was flying in the field, and one did not care to look at it even once; and now the grain has begun to wave, and its greenness refreshes the eyes and gladdens the heart. The peasant, seeing his field, cannot contain himself for joy, and great rich men who own gardens hover round it morning and evening to enjoy the sight. Then it came into ear; that first bloom was gone, but the peasant's life is bound up in it. A cloud came, and he took fright lest hail should fall; the west wind blew, and his heart went cold lest the frost should strike. At last, after much praying, the grain was ready, cut and carried away. And the field lies desolate and empty: the same wildness, the same silence, the same cheerlessness, the same gloom. The next season came, and again the same round. The world is in truth a museum of wonders, in which each thing is more marvellous than the last. Man is wonderful, his birth is wonderful, his life is wonderful, his death most wonderful of all. A life so short that at the very most it is sixty or seventy years, and that too with nothing settled or certain: when morning comes there is no trusting the evening, and when evening comes no relying on the morning. The child has no certainty of growing up, the young man no assurance of seeing old age. And on top of this, what plans, what ambitions, what provisions, as if one were to stay for ever and dwell for all eternity! Reason, and with reason folly; alertness, and with alertness heedlessness; helplessness, and with helplessness bravado. If this is not God's ordering, then what is it? Suppose an angel, knowing nothing of man and man's condition, were to come down from heaven to earth just for a little while: by which of our ways could he tell that we are mortal creatures? He would see that we live in very lasting and solid buildings, that we think out arrangements for hundreds of years to come, that we make contracts for the greatest and longest terms, that we plant gardens in which some trees do not bear fruit within a man's natural span, and yet expect to eat their fruit ourselves. Even when we buy a pair of shoes or a piece of cloth at the last, we take it only after seeing that it will last. No doubt there are some of us who, seeing others die, sometimes think that perhaps we too shall die, and that is no wonder; but that thought has no more permanence than a cloud of the rainy months: here it came, there it went. With our own hands we have laid in the earth a man like ourselves, perhaps younger than ourselves, sturdy of limb and strong of frame, healthy and robust; and in the graveyard we have seen with our own eyes that countless people lie buried in the earth. Of most of them not even the mark of the grave remains; of some it does, and the grave may be of masonry with an inscribed stone set on it, but no one can tell who the gentleman of that name was or where he lived.

"All this is so, and yet we have hardly come back from the graveyard before there is the same freedom from care and the same indifference. People say that man is compounded of four elements, earth, water, fire and air; but from his deeds it is proved that heedlessness too is certainly in his clay, and indeed more of it than of the other elements. The ordering of the world, so far as man has a hand in it, rests on this very heedlessness. Were it not for heedlessness, a man could not even get up and drink water, let alone farming and trade and other work. And I have heard for certain that criminals who have been sentenced to be hanged, and know for certain that now they cannot escape, give up food and drink for weeks beforehand and become worse than corpses. People say that sleep is so bad a thing that it comes even on the gallows; but that is the saying of those who have not heard the sentence of hanging. Let them hear the sentence of hanging and show us that they can sleep, and we will believe them. Nor is the heedlessness in man's nature without its wisdom. If death stood before one's eyes all the time, a man's senses would be deranged with fear; and worse than that, no work of any kind could be done, and the purpose for which man was created would be entirely lost. So heedlessness is necessary, and the world's work cannot go on without heedlessness; but not to such a degree that we never remember death even by mistake, and take ourselves for a clock whose winding will never run down, or a lamp whose oil will never be used up, a building that will never fall, or a spring that will never dry. It is true that no man denies dying, and none can deny it, for to this day no man has been seen, or even heard of, who escaped the eye of Azrael by any device or trick. But not denying it with the mouth, and not being able to deny it, is one thing, and proving it in one's dealings and daily conduct is another. I can never forget that story of Hazrat Ali: in the Battle of the Camel, when the fighting was crashing down like a mountain, he was dozing as he sat in his tent. A servant said in alarm: 'People's heads are bouncing like balls, and you think of sleeping?' Hazrat Ali smiled and said: 'Have you forgotten that verse of the Quran? "*When their term comes, they cannot put it off by an hour, nor bring it forward*."'[2] How could that servant have forgotten it? And what Muslim is there who does not remember this verse?

"And even if the words are not remembered, its meaning is on everyone's tongue like a proverb. But if anyone can show us that he can sleep at such a time as Ali did, we are ready to swear allegiance at his hand. When there were beliefs so firm, it became them to say: '*If the veil were lifted, I would not increase in certainty*.'[3] There were Muslims who believed in the unseen in that way, and there are we, seekers of salvation, hopers for Paradise, claimants of intercession, longers for forgiveness, who see with our own eyes and the thing does not sink into our hearts; because we have no habit of reflection, no leisure to think, no eagerness, no inclination. If the thought of death ever comes at all, it is only so far as this, that death means dying. No doubt death means dying; but have we looked at what goes with it, have we understood its consequences? Until a man dies, he cannot know what death is like. We see others dying: some give up their lives very hard; their senses stay with them to the end, and they tell others what they are going through, and from their agony the others come to recognise the pain. Some become unconscious before the death struggle, and those about them do not feel their suffering; and there are some servants of God whose breath goes out as they are talking, and they are gone. Who knows what manner of dying is written in one's fate? And since the end of the greatest fear is death, it is clear from this that death is a very dangerous thing. But oh, if only a man's troubles came to an end with death! If that were so, my petition would be to die first of all, even with the hardest death struggle. But what end? With death man's everlasting and eternal life begins, whose being good or bad, comforting or full of affliction, depends on the deeds he did in this life of a few days. So this life, besides being fleeting and uncertain and of a few days, and leaving aside its many kinds of affliction and sorts of pain and all manner of anxieties, why call it life? For man it is a torment and a burden, since its outcome in the hereafter is not known.

"And when it is reported of some holy men that they used to wish, 'Alas, that we were not trees or stones,' this is the reason. This is a religious thought, and if a man does not let it come into his mind, that is another matter; but if he thinks and reflects even a little, he cannot fail to be convinced of it. But even apart from that, the life of this world is not a thing to value and to love; and why should it be a thing to value and to love, when a man is on the throne today and in the ground tomorrow, has means today and is helpless tomorrow, has a treasury with him today and empty hands tomorrow, robes and garments today and a bed of dust tomorrow, honour and credit today and a worthless speck tomorrow, beloved today and despised and nothing tomorrow; today the resort of great affairs and tomorrow among the people of the graves (that is, alive today and dead tomorrow, here today and not tomorrow)? How fine is that Hindi *doha* (couplet) of someone's:

You must go, you cannot stay; going is certain, twenty in twenty:
On so fleeting a married bliss, who would have her hair braided?

These thoughts, which Azadi had read in some books and heard often in sermons, cooled all her excitement; and indeed these things are of a kind that pass before one's eyes every day, nay, several times a day. If God grants the grace to see and understand them, they make a man as straight as a wire drawn through the wire-drawer's plate. Now Azadi made a firm vow in her heart: "God willing, from now on I will not worry in any way about my marriage. Whatever God wills will happen. I was not born by my own contriving, nor did I grow up so far by my own contriving. Whatever ease and comfort I have had up to this hour, I did not provide it by my own contriving. He who by His grace has done all this, will He not look after me in future? Surely He will. It is a weakness of my faith not to trust Him fully and to bring my own contriving into it. What am I, and what is my contriving? Fate and destiny, fate and destiny."
