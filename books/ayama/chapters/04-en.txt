# Chapter 3: Azadi Becomes Acquainted with the Ways of English Society

At last, as God would have it, one night Azadi was coming down from the roof on some errand. The lamp usually burned all night long, but that night, God knows whether the wind blew it out or the oil ran out; in any case it was pitch dark. In her hurry Azadi did not wait for a lamp. She was young and quick of hand and foot, and she began to come down with a clatter. As it happened, a cat lay asleep on one of the steps; her foot came down hard on it, the cat screamed with a terrible noise, Azadi started and could not recover her balance, and she stumbled and fell to the bottom. As she fell a loud cry burst from her mouth in spite of herself. What with the cat's yowling, the thud of her fall and the scream that came with it, the whole house woke up. They came and found Azadi lying face down on the ground, moaning. They tried to turn her over and lift her, but at their touch she writhed. When they asked her, she had not the wits to answer. Although it was not very warm, she was bathed in sweat from the shock of the injury and lay trembling in every limb. When at last they had fanned her for a long time and washed her face with cold water, she managed with great difficulty, moaning, to say: "My right leg is badly hurt. I cannot breathe. Let me lie quiet; don't talk to me." The confusion of everyone at that moment cannot be described. It was Azadi who was hurt, but it was her mother's face that had gone white; she meant to say one thing and something else came out of her mouth. Nobody had the skill to tell what the injury was, and Azadi could not say, so they sat there dumbstruck, staring at one another. It was night, and it was a matter of a daughter; of the people who were known for setting injuries, one was a grain-parcher, one an oil-presser, one a butcher, one a barber; though they all thought hard, they could not think of any woman who knew the work. Time was passing, and they feared the injury would set cold. At last Azadi's mother, Hadi Begam, herself said to her husband: "You were always saying you would bring the padre's memsahib, and I seem to remember you praised her highly in these matters too. See if you can bring her." No sooner had these words left Hadi Begam's mouth than Khwaja Azad went off and fetched the memsahib within half an hour. When Khwaja Azad arrived, the memsahib was asleep in her room. He had the ayah wake her, and as soon as she heard, she came along just as she was in her night clothes, with her case of instruments and her box of medicines under her arm. As soon as she came she first gave Azadi a medicine to drink, and no sooner had it gone down her throat than her restlessness stopped. Then she pulled the toes, moved and swung the foot, and felt all round in her own way. When she had examined her thoroughly she said: "For fear of the pain I cannot handle it much, but to me the hip seems to be out of joint. If you people allow me, I will give her chloroform to smell and examine her properly."

Hadi Begam: What thing will you make her smell, and what will it do?

Memsahib: Nothing much. There is a spirit in this bottle; I will sprinkle it on a handkerchief and put it to her nose. The girl will become unconscious for a little while, and I will lay her on her back and turn her on her side and find out where she is hurt and what kind of injury it is.

Hadi Begam: How long will she stay unconscious like that?

Memsahib: As long as I have been here already, and as long again.

Hadi Begam: Will it hurt her?

Memsahib: She will not feel the least pain from the injury; but, yes, after she comes to, her head will swim somewhat, and perhaps she will feel sick too, because this medicine is the spirit of wine and it is very strongly intoxicating. But I will give her something to take the intoxication off, and she will soon settle down.

Hadi Begam: Oh no, you will make her smell wine? Wine is forbidden in our religion.

Memsahib: I know wine is forbidden among Muslims, and we too think it bad; but one has no power over illness. It is necessary to make her unconscious. If the hip is out, as I think, and I believe I am right, it cannot be put back without unconsciousness. If she is not made unconscious with chloroform, she will become unconscious by fainting, and in that there is more danger. If you want her to be comfortable, be quick, before the swelling comes up.

Hadi Begam kept hesitating, and Khwaja Azad said in English: "Madam, this woman is very superstitious and bigoted. Please give her chloroform on my word and do what you have to do." Saying this, Azad withdrew from the room out of propriety. The memsahib by herself gave the chloroform, put the hip back and bound it up. The last turn of the bandage was being tied when Azadi opened her eyes, red and heavy. The memsahib reassured the family, then gave Azadi some medicine, and as she left she gave them some powders: "If she settles down, give her one every hour; otherwise every half hour. I hope that by morning the stupor will have worn off completely. In the hurry the bandaging was not done well; it needed strength, and my hands have not the power. As soon as it is morning I will send my eldest girl, Miss Mary, with bandages and powders, and she will put the binding right."

Hadi Begam: Will your daughter make her smell that same wine again?

Memsahib: No, there is no need to make her smell anything now. The binding only has to be tightened, that is all. Don't be anxious in any way; to my mind the hip has gone back very well.

Hadi Begam: When will she walk about now?

Memsahib: For the present great care is needed that she does not stir or move in any way. Well, let her lie like this for a week at least. After a week I will come myself and open the binding, and then I will be able to tell when she will walk about.

The next day, before the sun was properly up, Miss Mary arrived with the things for the binding, and with great quickness she strapped her up firmly and set pillows on both sides of her in such a way that the patient could not move even if she wanted to. Azadi's general health had, God be praised, been so excellent from the beginning that, though she had had a very great shock, she would have been perfectly well within the month; but she was rather restless as well, and though they took many precautions she could not keep still. Miss Mary would frighten the patient and those who nursed her alike: "The injury is getting delayed in healing, and if, God forbid, the leg ends up shorter, she will be lame for the rest of her life, and we shall get a bad name too. But you people don't care at all. Whenever I come I find the binding loose. If she does not move, how does the binding come undone of itself?"

Hadi Begam: For our part we do not let her move. She herself is sensible too and takes care as far as she can. If her leg gets restless in her sleep, that cannot be helped.

Miss Mary: It can be helped. Let one person stay awake by turns. But you people can't manage anything.

Meanwhile Azadi began to run a fever as well. The fever had not left her when she got dysentery, and now she was truly ill, and the family began to waver in their minds. But the memsahib told them plainly: "Till now we have been treating her for your sake, and from now on we shall treat her for our own. There is nothing wrong with the treatment; it has been carried out in the proper way from the beginning. All the fault lies with the household; she is not nursed properly. She ought to be under our eyes all the time, but I know you will not send her to my bungalow, where she would certainly be more comfortable than in your house. Well, from now on one of us will stay here. Mary will sleep here at night. The fever and dysentery are passing things; I will vouch for her recovery. It cannot be that we wear ourselves out over her for a month and a half, and now, when the days of her getting well are near, someone else makes a name for himself and says, 'I cured her.'" Miss Mary had the house cleaned, had Azadi's bed and her own set up in an airy place, had Azadi's pillows and bedding changed, and saw to medicine, diet, massage and everything herself. In a single week all the trouble was gone. When the weather was dry there was a slight ache in the hip now and then, and for that the massage went on. Azadi had been caught in a great tangle, but through the good management of the Englishwomen she was completely well by the third month, and when she rose from her illness her colour bloomed like a rose. The memsahib less so, but Mary became so close that even after Azadi was well she came every day without fail and sat for hours.

Azadi was utterly devoted to her, and Hadi Begam herself had grown so fond of her that if Mary now and then came late she would wait for her, or if she wanted to leave early she would stop her. The strangeness of nation and religion between Azadi and Mary was itself the reason for their growing closeness. They both wanted to know each other as if they had been born in one house, lived in one place and played together. Hours would pass and their talk would not come to an end. There was not much match in their ages, for Azadi, by God's grace, had only now entered her twelfth year and Mary was a few days short of nineteen. But God knows whether it was the pull of Mary's friendliness, or the effect of the services, sympathy and kindness, quite without self-interest, without greed, without any earlier acquaintance, that she had shown in Azadi's illness: we have never seen or heard of such a bond even between two sisters as there was between Mary and Azadi. At first Azadi was rather reserved; and why should an unmarried girl soon be at ease with a strange woman? But for three months there were two or three visits a day, and at last the staying at night, the bond of patient and healer, and the strangeness too: from meeting came fondness, from fondness affection, from affection love. Azadi too was as well grown as any girl in this country, but Mary was a fully grown woman. Judging by her figure, as they would judge themselves, the women reckoned that she must be the mother of two or three children at least. Misled by this, Azadi said to Mary one day:

Azadi: Why don't you ever bring your children to our house? We want so much to see them.

Mary: (Smiling) Why, I am not even married yet. I am only Mary (Maryam) in name[1], do you see?

Azadi: Are you one of those women among your people who do not marry, whom you spoke of one day?

Mary: No, I am not a nun, and I would not like to be a nun. In fact my mother, in her lifetime, wanted to make me a nun, but I refused outright. It would be no wonder if she died a little displeased with me because of it. There is an ayah from my mother's time, a very old one from Lucknow; she nursed me and brought me up and played with me. She has grown very old now, and I have pensioned her. In her youth she had worked somewhere for the nuns and knew their life well. Because she had nursed and reared me she had, and still has, a special kind of love for me. I was young in those days; the ayah explained it to me: "Missy baba, in Lucknow I saw plenty of the 'untouched' women of Nasir-ud-Din Haidar [TN: King of Oudh, 1827–37] and of the nuns. Don't you ever agree to become a nun. I don't say I love you more than your mama[2], but religion has eaten up your mama's sense; she wants to throw you into hell while you are still alive. I have quarrelled enough and will quarrel again, but, my good missy, just do this one little thing for me. I say it for your own good and comfort; there is nothing in it for me. God forbid that you become a nun, or you will remember what I say today and be very sorry." Because the ayah said it so insistently, her advice sank into my heart, and I steeled myself and told my mother plainly. In her own way of thinking she was very much grieved, and though she died a natural death, that grief wore her down quickly.

Azadi: Then is this memsahib not your mother?

Mary: She is my mother, but my stepmother. After my mother died my father married her. She has children by her first husband too, and she has had five by my father already. The family has grown very large, and, if it were not for the comfort of religion, I could say that they are not well off.

Azadi: How much can a stepmother love you?

Mary: Whether she loves me or not does not matter to me. My mother left so much for me when she died that I can live in comfort on the income.

Azadi: How is the padre sahib with you?

Mary: How should he be? I have not lived with him. All along I was not with him [TN: reading uncertain]; there is a school on Mount Abu, I lived there and studied there. Now for about four months I have been here as a guest.

Azadi: Perhaps it is because of your mother's death that your marriage has been so late.

Mary: It is not late at all. We generally marry at just about this age. To marry before this is a game like marrying a boy doll to a girl doll.

Azadi: Then what was the hurry for the memsahib? The padre sahib must be settling a match somewhere.

Mary: My marriage, and the padre sahib to settle the match? What would that mean? No, I have settled my own match. You do not know our custom. Our rule is that a man and a woman come to like each other and agree between themselves. The permission of parents and other elders is a formality.

Azadi: Then among your people can unmarried girls meet men not of their family?

Mary: Of course, just as you and I meet. And if they did not meet, how would they come to know each other's temper and each other's real nature? To give your hand into someone's hand for life without knowing him is a shot in the dark: it may hit the mark, or it may be a fluke.

Azadi: Well, suppose you agreed to marry someone and the padre sahib would not allow it, what would you do?

Mary: That happens very rarely; in fact it does not happen. After all, I have sense too. Why should I become friendly with a man against whom the padre sahib or anyone else could have any objection? Looks, family, personal ability, health, temper, habits, income, profession: these are the things to look at. Can I not look at them? And since it is I who will have to make the marriage, my looking comes first. When a bond of this kind is about to form between a man and a woman, it does not stay hidden from the family, nor is there any custom of hiding it. Frequent visits, letters, gifts: there are many things from which it becomes known that they are drawn to each other. So if the family has any objection, they simply do not let the acquaintance grow.

Azadi: Where you have settled your match, has the padre sahib approved it?

Mary: The thing is, I have always passed my examinations with great distinction. For two years people had been asking, and I said, "There is only a little time left; why should I leave my education half finished?" Now it is about six months since my studies were completed. I came here thinking that if I married while living with my father I would have the chance to take his advice; and besides my father's life, whom have I in the world? Of those who had thought of marrying me, two gentlemen came here too when they heard I had come, and a few Englishmen here also expressed the same wish. In the end, after four months of thinking it over carefully, I have chosen one gentleman; but so far I have neither said yes nor refused; I have kept the matter pending. There is nothing else wrong with this gentleman. He is of a good family, he is able, he is cool-tempered too and good-hearted. Though at present he draws only four hundred a month, he is in the line of promotion; as the days go by his rank will go on rising. Only one thing about him troubles me: he has the habit of betting on horse races, which is a kind of gambling. I have told him plainly: "Promise to give up horse-racing, and I will give you my word." So far he has not promised, nor have I given my word, nor have I got my father's consent; but on his side there has been no falling off in friendliness. The poor man is in a strange tug-of-war: on one side a passion for racing since childhood, on the other his love for me. From the delay it seems he is a man of his word; if he says it with his mouth he will certainly keep it, otherwise what was difficult about it? He could have said it long ago. But when I sounded his heart I found there is much room for me in it. It is twenty to one that he will swear off racing; and suppose he does not, I will not press him too much. I know that after we are married I shall be able to put more pressure on him, and he will have to do as I say.

Azadi: Have you settled the *mahr* (the marriage settlement due to the wife) too? Among us there is a great wrangle over it, and often matches that have been arranged are broken off because of the *mahr*.

Mary: Among us there is no *mahr* or anything of the kind, because the man can neither leave his wife nor marry another while she lives.

In short, many talks of this kind used to go on between Mary and Azadi, and their closeness kept growing, until Mary read Azadi many novels[3]. Several times she brought big albums[4] and showed her pictures of people, buildings, mountains and all sorts of things, some of them drawn by Mary herself. One day she had a big bundle brought on the head of a coolie [TN: reading uncertain]. In it were mufflers, stockings, gloves, tablecloths, covers, borders, mats and things of that kind, sewn, knitted and embroidered by her own hand. Sometimes, when she felt like it, she would play the English instrument and sing, and when she was in a very good mood she would dance too. It happened several times that she put on Indian clothes, looked in the mirror for ages and kept asking each person, "Do these clothes look strange on me?" This closeness with Mary did not last very long. In just the fourth month after Azadi's bath of recovery Mary was married; the wedding was in the afternoon, and that same evening she left with her husband for the hills on her honeymoon[5]. But even that much intimacy changed Azadi's nature. While Mary was there she did not think much about it; in Mary's talk she lost all awareness of everything else. When Mary went, she grieved over the parting for a long time; Mary's face was always before her eyes and her words kept coming back to her.

# Notes

[1] An allusion to the Lady Mary (Maryam) having given birth to Jesus without a husband.

[2] In English they call a mother "mama".

[3] A storybook is called a novel in English.

[4] A book in which pictures are pasted.

[5] It is the custom of the English that in the first month of marriage the bride and bridegroom go alone to some pleasant place and stay there; this is called the honeymoon in English.
