# Chapter 16: Azadi Besieged by Admirers

The things said at the third meeting with Azmuda made Azadi's resolve firmer still. It is not that a woman can be beautiful and reach that age without knowing her beauty; but there is no doubt that Azadi learnt the worth and value of her beauty from what Azmuda said. From the day she was widowed, nay, from the day Maulvi Mustajab set out for Bhopal, she had never once happened to look in a mirror. Today she set the mirror before her, put her hair and so on in order, changed her clothes, and thought: "I too am something." But, as they say, a knife at the throat of "I"; today's combing her hair before the mirror and changing her clothes turned out a calamity, and people embroidered on it as each one fancied. Some gentleman who lived, or had come to stay, next door with only a wall between began loudly reciting verses and *ghazals*. Someone knocked at the outer door; the maid went to ask, and a boy handed her a note and darted off. The maid brought the paper and gave it into the lady's hand; the lady read it, and it was this *ghazal*, by the present author:

Ah, who knows whether there is any power in you or not,
Whether she too has any news of my love or not.

Go and tell her of my state, at least,
O breeze: do you pass through her lane or not?

But how late an hour has the evening come, O God!
O night of separation, have you a dawn or not?

You who allow yourselves such cruelty and oppression,
O idols, have you any fear of God or not?

As for us, we say our prayers with our faces turned towards you;
We do not know whether the Ka'ba too lies that way or not.

If there were a jeweller, we would show him the pearl of a tear and ask:
"Have you so lustrous a gem with you or not?"

Human beings unite in themselves every kind of excellence;
O man of God, have you any skill in you or not?

We take correction from no one, it is true,
But we do look to see whether it has Zafar's style or not.

You who lie weeping at her door at all hours,
Have you any home of your own to stay in or not?

She is sitting with a cot spread out in the courtyard, and a ball of paper falls into her lap; she opens it, and it is a verse of Insha Allah Khan:

You will see my skill at leaping walls,
When I come down with a thud and say, "Here, lady, my salaams!"

The maid has brought a leaf cup of curds from the bazaar; something is written on the leaves; it cannot be read properly, but it is something of the same kind. When the flour is sifted, scraps of paper come out of the bran. Anonymous letters come in the post. Everything confirms what Azmuda said, and it appears that not one or two but many men are bent on this. Seeing herself in this state, Azadi wished to remember God; but what she remembered was Pharaoh, how through Moses' curse the plagues of lice and frogs came down on him: frogs in the food and lice in the hair. Azadi's state became just like that: in places no one would think of, there was a paper.

In a few days Azadi collected so many letters, notes, papers, scraps, *ghazals* and verses that if they were all gathered together they would make a good-sized book of ten or twelve sections, and a book so popular too that it would sell from hand to hand and the supply would never keep up; for people's tastes are known, and only two kinds of books sell: first of all the fifth chapter of the *Bustan*, on love and youth, and below that religious squabbling. Azadi folded all the papers up as letters and kept them, and it made a big bundle. When at the time of her death she made her famous will, of which mention will come later, she handed that bundle over to us, and it is preserved to this day. Of these papers one letter pleased us greatly, and a copy of it is set down here. In it there were no formal titles and greetings, expressions of longing and so on; the writer went straight to his purpose:

(The letter) "If I took you for an ordinary woman I would never have dared to write this; for I know very well that the ideas of ordinary women on most matters in general, and on the marriage tie in particular, are entirely nonsensical, absurd from head to foot, and altogether laughable. I regard the modesty and shame of women with the greatest respect and with high honour and reverence, and I think it baseness of the lowest degree to interfere with it. Modesty is the ornament of women's beauty, the lustre of loveliness, the guardian of honour, the shield of chastity, the surety of faithfulness, the medal of nobility, the badge of decency, the touchstone of false and true, the mark of good and bad, the charm of household life, the savour of marriage. For others it is part of faith; for women it is a condition of humanity. A woman without modesty is a mirror without clearness, a pearl without lustre, a flower without scent, gold lace without glitter. But women, out of needless vanity, have carried modesty to such a degree of excess that it is now worse than a fault. It is like a beauty spot, which is a kind of beauty, but up to a fixed limit. When the spot goes beyond that limit, as the line goes, 'it will be thought ugly, not a heart-stealing beauty spot'; and as another line goes, 'the beauty spot that grew beyond the limit became a wart.' Excess of modesty has not only kept women deprived and cut off from lawful enjoyments, nor only kept them from acquiring the accomplishments for which they have a God-given aptitude and capacity; it has made them so helpless and powerless in men's hands that women are, as it were, things for men's use and their servants, not partners in household life or counsellors in affairs. In the matter of marriage women's helplessness has come to such a pass that there is no difference between them and the slaves of Africa. The master sold his slaves to whomever he pleased; in the same way the guardian, elder or protector married the woman to whomever he fancied. Can any greater oppression and injustice be imagined than that a woman who must spend her whole life with one man can neither say anything nor express any opinion? So marriages among us are a blind man's stick: if it hits, there is no prize and no reward; if it misses, no complaint and no grievance. And the most regrettable and astonishing thing is that women are not conscious of their wretched state and do not feel it; that is, there is no hope or expectation of their improvement and reform. If they were conscious, they would complain and raise an outcry; if they felt it, they would fight and quarrel for their rights and stand their ground. I have known you since the time when you had not the sense to understand these things; but even in your play and games your intelligence and cleverness were plain. What would you remember? But I remember scores of instances that proved that God had granted you sound understanding and straight judgement. I never saw you insist on anything unreasonable or hold out obstinately for nothing. A greater merit than that was that, after all you were a child, if you committed any fault you would admit it without hesitation, which showed that your spirit was strong and your nature firm, free and fearless.

"Once you spoiled an important government paper of your father's. You went off into the house, and after you had gone a hunt began for it. Your father was extremely angry with the servants, and some of them were about to be dismissed and some fined. Just then you came out, and as soon as you heard you took the paper out from under the carpet and gave it into Khwaja Sahib's hand. Except that you had not torn it, there was no way in which you had not spoilt it. The conversation that then took place between you and Khwaja Sahib I remember word for word to this day, and shall remember word for word till my dying breath. Khwaja Sahib: 'Azadi, what is this you have done?' You: 'There is no need to ask that; what I did is there before you.' Khwaja Sahib: 'Don't you know that government papers lie on my table?' You: 'I can't tell which are government ones; yes, I know papers lie there.' Khwaja Sahib: 'Then why did you go to my table without asking me?' You: 'Because you never forbade me.' Khwaja Sahib: 'What business had you at the table?' You: 'The business I had, I have brought and given into your hand.' Khwaja Sahib: 'Now you tell me what I am to do with it.' You: 'If you won't be angry again, give it back to me so that I can draw a few more lines on it; this paper is very smooth and the pen runs on it beautifully.' Hearing this, everyone began to laugh, and Khwaja Sahib laughed too. Khwaja Sahib: 'Alas, Azadi, you don't know what a heavy loss you have caused me now.' You: 'Certainly I don't know; and since I don't know, it is a matter neither for rejoicing nor for regret. If I had done the harm knowingly, it would have been a matter for regret.' Khwaja Sahib: 'Now when the Sahib asks, what shall I say?' You: 'Give him my name.' Khwaja Sahib: 'Aren't you afraid of the Sahib?' You: 'People call the ruler mother and father; is anyone afraid of mother and father?' Khwaja Sahib: 'Well, I am afraid.' You: 'Then you must be doing him some wrong.' Khwaja Sahib: 'Just such a wrong as today, when this paper has been spoilt.' You: 'What has been spoilt about it? It is a printed paper; I have drawn a few lines in the blank spaces with Indian pen and ink; give it here and I will pour water over it, and the ink will wash off.' And so it happened, and the paper became even cleaner than before.

"Then Khwaja Sahib asked: 'Well, Azadi, why had you hidden the paper under the carpet?' You: 'I didn't hide it; to dry it with dust I lifted the edge of the carpet, put it under the edge and patted it on top; just then, God knows who, someone called me, and I left the paper there and went into the house.' I know many such stories of yours, and it is these ways of yours that have made me devoted to you, not from today but from your childhood. People are infatuated with beauty and looks, and so am I; but my infatuation began with what is within. Looks are subject to change, and what is within is a line cut in stone. So people's love is fleeting, and mine will last as long as my life. It was for this purpose that I had grown so close to Khwaja Sahib that those who did not know well took me for his son; and I did long to be his son, indeed I tell you today that Khwaja Sahib had in a way given me his word; but you know well the disagreement between your parents. Your mother, merely out of obstinacy against Khwaja Sahib, did what seemed proper to her. The effect that disappointment and failure had on me is witnessed by the jungles and wildernesses in which for months I wandered, beside myself and bewildered. It is attested by the doctors and physicians of the city, the fools who, taking me for an ordinary patient, wasted much of their own time and of my nurses' money. In short, those days passed for me harder even than the agony of death, and I wondered: 'O God, what toughness of life is this, that there is so much pain and the soul does not leave?' But no act of God is without purpose. After a long time I learnt that this was the reason I did not die: I value very highly a success of the degree that I now fully hope and expect. That was the choice of others, and not even of others but of your mother alone; this will be your own selection. That was other people's opinion, this your own inclination. That was the singing of drum-beating minstrels, this the song of a master musician. That was a foolish child's spinning of a top, this the aim of a crack marksman. That was the groping of one who did not know the way, this the journey of one who knows the stage, a means to victory. In one respect you have more ease than before, in that this time there is not the same restriction, and you can have as much say as you like; but it would be a kind of hateful selfishness for me to hide or lessen the difficulties you are about to meet.

"I am fully assured that you have accepted the necessity of a second marriage and have decided that your state calls for a second marriage; but there is a great distance from opinion to resolve, and from resolve to action. A person is under many kinds of pressure: he is subject to religion, to the ruler of the time, to the elders of his family, to his company, to his education and upbringing, and perhaps, if not most of all then no less than any, to the customs of his country. It is a great matter for thanks that of these obstacles only one stands before you, that is, custom, and that not with the same severity as among the people of the small towns. God reward the *maulvis*, who have striven to make the marriage of widows customary; and in the city there is no longer the aversion of former times or of the villages and small towns. But though a second marriage is no disgrace, alas, it is not yet general custom. Thinking that most of your mother's family are *maulvis*, I had hoped that perhaps there would be no difficulty in finding precedents for a widow's remarriage; but I am sorry to say that, as far as I have inquired, both your father's and your mother's families are deprived of that honour, and for that reason I fear that if no one will lead you astray, no one will urge you on either. The great difficulty is that the matter is delicate, and it is in just such matters that a person seeks the support of others' advice. If anyone can be your advisers, it is women of your own sex, who have not the capacity to advise. So whatever you have to do, you must do by your own unaided judgement. It is a mercy that the case is not complicated. Religion, reason and the demands of your situation, all these are on one side; and against them there is, if anything, only that one empty and baseless idea, that it is not general custom. Although you have not tasted the bitterness of widowhood to the full, from what you have tasted you can judge what a hard affliction widowhood is, how many of God's handmaids are caught in that affliction, and that there is no other way of escaping it. So by marrying you will remove not only your own affliction but the afflictions of hundreds and thousands of widows, who are waiting only for this sign, that some God-fearing woman like you should step forward, and they will follow her.

"You must surely have read in books and heard in sermons that whoever revives a dead *sunnat* (practice of the Prophet) will have, besides the reward of his own deed, the reward of the deeds of all who follow that *sunnat* after his example until the Day of Resurrection. So this is the occasion for you to use your reason and courage and not let this great reward slip from your hands. Who knows whether this reward was written in your fate, and God made you a widow for this very purpose? Finally I draw your attention to myself as well. If you do not marry again, or if you do and marry someone else, then by dying I shall show not only you but the whole world whether my suit was a pretence and a show or true and real. I enclose in this an envelope addressed to me, and I have made firm arrangements with the postman that letters to this address shall fall into no hands but mine. Rest assured that if you wish to write to me, it is impossible that anyone else will know of it; and for this pledge of mine I make God my surety."

Seeing all these goings-on, Azadi said in her heart: "Every day that comes brings a new affliction of widowhood with it. What need to speak of those whose husbands are with them? Do those whose husbands are abroad not wash their hair, change their clothes, wear flowers and bangles, put on henna and adorn themselves? No one says anything. I just looked at my face in the mirror for a moment, and a bombardment began from all sides. How can I check these forced suspicions? Yes, I could smear my face with mud and shave my head like Hindu widows; but that I have not done and will not do in future. Let people have not one suspicion but a hundred thousand; I don't care. What a calamity the men have raised! Is it a good thing to disgrace someone for no reason, out of the blue? There are weddings, there are marriages, there are *nikahs*, but this way of going about it I have never heard of anywhere. And I do not see this scandal stopping without my taking someone's protection. If I really am beautiful, as people think, my beauty did not come with widowhood; but people knew that I was married to someone, and so no one had the courage. Now they have seen that I have no protector, and they have begun to boast of their love. So in this there is no fault of the people; if there is a fault, it is mine. If today I became someone's, all this loving and being loved would be over. But how can I become someone's in such haste? It is easy to say with the mouth and hard to do. Honour once gone does not come back. That first time was a different matter, when I was in others' power; and now it is a different kind of occasion: ten to speak ill and only one to advise, my own self, and that is all. After all one is only human; if I think one thing and it turns out another, then, as they say, the loss of one's capital and the gloating of the neighbours. People will make my very life a misery with their taunts. If I do it at all, I will do it after testing it thoroughly, so that tomorrow nothing can be said of any kind. Here what little sense I have left goes even more astray: what if the same thing happens again? Then it will be the proverb: neither did I eat nor did I enjoy; I just went and put my neck in it for nothing. In short, who knows whether it will happen at all or not, and if it does, is it a morsel in the mouth to be gulped down at once? And people will not let me draw breath. O God, what a plague is upon my life! Well, there was no great harm as far as notes and letters went, but the threat of leaping the wall has made the night's sleep unlawful for me. There is a wretched black cat that now and then jumps over the wall, and my heart pounds for hours. If, God forbid, some strange man jumped in at night, I would certainly die of fright. But is there such lawlessness under English rule? It is no threat; it must be a poet's humbug. Still, it would be better if I went away somewhere for two or four days. Maid, go and tell them at the bearers' stand to bring a palanquin; I'll just go over to my parents' house. You people stay on your guard and keep me informed that all is well. One man [illegible] go on ahead; what need is there to run alongside the palanquin?"
