# Chapter 14: The Go-Between Wins a Hold over Azadi's Mind, and Azadi's Opinion Begins to Change

In short, up to the time when Azadi went to her own house and kept people from coming, nothing of Chhalawa's handiwork appeared. What schemes she may have been working quietly underneath, we did not ask and she did not tell. When Azadi lifted the ban and people began coming and going to see her, Chhalawa, after lying low for two or four days, put on a special guise and turned up too. Azadi looked at her: a strange face and an outlandish style. When she asked, the woman said: "They call me Azmuda Khanam (the Experienced Lady). I am from Lucknow, but for a long time I have happened to live in Bhopal. Your husband, the late Maulvi Mustajab, God grant him Paradise, was on very close terms with us; he died before my eyes. He often used to talk to us about you. When I was setting out to come here, I said to myself as I started, 'I shall certainly meet her'; and today God has fulfilled that wish. But between your state as the Maulvi Sahib described it and your state now there is the difference of earth and sky. I recognised you now only by guessing; had I seen you in your true state, I would not have mistaken you perhaps even among a thousand ladies. Perhaps no one else in the world would have grieved for a husband as much as you have." The pretext for a meeting that Chhalawa, whom we must now call Azmuda Khanam, had hit upon was better than any other could have been. Hearing Mustajab's name Azadi wept, and her weeping was natural; but the wonder is that Azmuda too brought tears to her eyes. When the storm of grief had subsided, Azadi began to ask the Maulvi Sahib's story in detail: how he fell ill, what illness it was, whose treatment he had, who looked after him, and whether at the time of his death he said anything by way of a last wish or not.

Azmuda: The Maulvi Sahib's death was a strange kind of death. It was a Friday; as usual he had his beard trimmed, bathed, changed his clothes, ate, and, as was his habit, lay down for just a moment; then he got up, made the ablution and went to the prayer. Standing in the prayer he felt a sort of giddiness and fell down unconscious. People thought it was a faint; when it went on a long while some thought it was a seizure, and some began to say it was epilepsy. The Chief Physician and the General's doctor were sent for, and both said that it was nothing at all. If fate had given time, what lack of medicine and care would there have been there?

Azadi: Did the physicians name any illness at all, in the end?

Azmuda: What do I know, my dear? They spoke of weakness of the heart. I asked the Nawab Chief Physician and argued with him at length, and he said: "This is a special kind of illness; wind is generated and surrounds the heart. Just as in a country there is a ruler, so in man's body the heart is the chief of all and the mainstay of life; and God has made the heart of so delicate a temper that it cannot bear even the least strain. In all illnesses of the heart a person is finished off at once in this way; there is no time for remedies."

Azadi: Is there no warning of this illness beforehand?

Azmuda: What will you gain now by inquiring into it?

Azadi: That is true. Then how is it that you have come here to Delhi?

Azmuda: I have some relations here too, but I have come on business which is government business, though in one sense my own business as well.

Azadi: If there is any business I can do, or that you think I can get done by speaking for you, please tell me freely. For in the first place I belong here, in the second place I eat the salt of that same court, and in the third place acquaintance has its rights; you were on terms with the Maulvi Sahib like kin, and if the Maulvi Sahib were alive today you would not think of staying anywhere else, nor would he let you.

Azmuda: God is my witness, I think of you as more than him; and do not give a thought to my staying elsewhere. The business I have come for can best be managed from the very place where I am staying. And what need is there for you to say it? I am sure already that you will not hold back in helping me; but let me inquire thoroughly first and then I will tell you; and perhaps my purpose may be served of itself, so what need to trouble you? And now I am going; God willing, I shall come again some day a little earlier, so that I can talk with you to my heart's content.

Azadi: It would have been proper for you to stay here; but perhaps you are thinking of my poverty. God has given me plenty through the favour of your court; I am not so badly off that I cannot keep you as a guest. Only you will not get such tasty food; but a poor woman's heart will be glad.

Azmuda: And what great rich woman am I? And even the rich do not eat gold and silver leaf instead of chapatis. Nothing is lacking with God; if He wills, in the twinkling of an eye He can make a beggar a great noble. No one's days stay the same for ever. Be content with God; He has sent this affliction, and He will also give you its recompense, He certainly will. And what objection would I have to eating at your house? But there is no escaping invitations; whoever's hospitality you do not accept takes offence. Now my niece will be watching for me. Well, God be with you.

Azadi: Tell me your address and whereabouts, and I will come to see you myself.

Azmuda: No, no, never even think of setting foot outside the house. I shall come myself, if not tomorrow then the day after, without fail.

At the second meeting, besides concocting many other lies, the wretch laid a slander on the poor innocent late Maulvi Mustajab too, so that Azadi's heart might turn against him; and in the end it did turn. She said: "You must have heard the name of Nazuk Andam Begam. She was originally a favourite maid of His Highness, the *murshidzada* (royal prince) of Lucknow. When Lucknow was ruined in the Mutiny, she somehow (it is a long story) came to Bhopal and became a companion of Sikandar Begam Sahib. Where could she have the wealth of Lucknow there? But still she got an estate of twenty-five thousand rupees a year in Bhopal; you might say her tears were wiped. Since she is a good manager, she is counted among the respected households there. No child was born of her own womb; she has adopted a girl. Now that girl, by God's grace, has grown up, and a husband was being sought for her, and still is. Nazuk Andam Begam Sahib is always going in and out of the royal palaces; God knows how she saw your husband, and as soon as she saw him she was smitten. The moment she got down from her conveyance she said to me: 'Look here, Azmuda, my dear, today I have seen the son-in-law of my choice.' What was hard about making inquiries? The very next day she sent word to the Nawab Sahib: 'Give this Maulvi Sahib to us.' So relations began between the Maulvi Sahib and our household. The Maulvi Sahib would come, and the Begam Sahib would have him write the orders and instructions for the estate, and when there was nothing to write or read, she would talk of this and that. God forbid; the Maulvi Sahib has died and I have to die; I will not tell lies about a dead man. In front of me there was never any talk of marriage; but the Begam Sahib had begun to appear unveiled before the Maulvi Sahib, and she certainly did, just for a moment, bring her daughter too before the Maulvi Sahib, not often, only once or twice, I don't know whether on purpose or by mistake; but everyone inside and outside had seen what was going on. Just then the Maulvi Sahib's death happened. The Begam Sahib grieved greatly, but what could be done? She had been looking for a son-in-law for a long time; now she had found a man of her choice and had become a little easier in her mind, and now she is as anxious as ever again. Certain complications have come up so that she does not wish to give her daughter there, and, God bless her, the girl is shooting up like a gathering cloud. I too wrote to my relations: 'If you have any young man in view, write to me.' They gave no answer. I wrote again urgently: 'There must be thousands of suitors for such a fine match; can't you find anyone in Delhi?' Truly the people of Delhi have rather broken fortunes. Now they named a man, and wrote: 'These are matters of the court; don't let the Begam Sahib know of it yet; come yourself and see with your own eyes.' That is the reason for my coming; otherwise, what have I to do with Delhi?"

Azadi: Then did you see that young man, and how did you find him?

Azmuda: I saw him, and saw him thoroughly, as he should be seen. I myself talked with him for hours and sounded him out in every way. There is no doubt that it is hard to find a better husband for a woman, a better son-in-law for parents-in-law, a better son for parents, in short a better man than he; but harder still is getting him to agree.

Azadi: Who is this human being in Delhi?

Azmuda: Given someone who knows how to judge, they can pick out a diamond from among stones; but I don't want to tell you his name yet; there is a reason for it. God willing, when I come again I will tell you the whole story of him.

Azmuda said this much and took her leave, and Azadi began to think in her heart: "This woman apparently has no interest or purpose with me. I wanted her to stay with me and she refused. I said that if there was any work I could do I was at her service, and so far she has not asked me for help of any kind. Everything she said was to the point and well grounded. No doubt it would be no wonder if the Maulvi Sahib had gone astray from sitting in the company of the rich; wealth is just such an evil thing that its intoxication would make even an angel a devil at once. What is the state of the rich of our own city? The men's quarters foul and unclean, the women's quarters filthy and impure, evil worthless ways, idle absurd pastimes; one is born of a slave girl and another of a dancing girl's womb. Nobility has not touched them, they have not passed near decency. What we have heard of the doings in the Fort, and the kind of things that are well known about Lucknow, make us tremble. The poor Maulvi Sahib must have been in the same state as Joseph. But he was a prophet; God saved him from Zulaikha's snares. How could the Maulvi Sahib have been safe from the net of that harlot Nazuk Andam? Well, what shall I say now of a dead man? But he was certainly unfaithful. The world can see that I did not neglect a grain's worth of his rights. As long as he was here I never in any state went outside his obedience; I never complained of him to anyone behind his back; whatever he brought me, little or much, I gladly accepted on my head and eyes. What was I worth? But thanks be to that pure Lord, I was never false in any way to what he entrusted to me, so much so that that Self-sufficient One had me complete the *iddat* too according to the rule of the sacred law, and I was released from his wifehood. I grieved so much at his death that God alone knows it well; I even resolved to destroy myself with my own hands and thought out all sorts of schemes; but never even by mistake did I think of a second marriage, only because, since he remained mine as long as he lived, the condition of fidelity was that I too should remain his as long as there was breath in my body. But if what I have heard from Azmuda's lips is true (and I can see no reason why it should not be true), then even that condition of fidelity no longer holds. And now when I think about it, that idea of mine was wrong anyway; for if a second marriage were a breach of fidelity, God would never have commanded it. A breach of fidelity is a bad thing; then it would mean (God forgive me) that God commands His servants to do evil. One thing more comes to mind: the chief reason why a breach of fidelity is bad is that it pains the one towards whom you break faith; and here that is not the case either; so it is wrong even to call a second marriage a breach of fidelity. If I were not of an age for a second marriage, or if I had children, then perhaps it would have been improper to think of a second marriage, but not because of fidelity; because of other considerations. As it is, there are women much older than I sitting unmarried, with not even any talk of their betrothal; and Mother married me off in such a hurry; otherwise, had it been left to Father's opinion, perhaps I would not have been married even yet. Oh dear, it would have been better if I had not been married at all! Mother needlessly cut short my journey. In every house there are unmarried girls sitting and nobody asks for them; why should anyone value me before them? And even if it is a second marriage, the overtures for it cannot come from my side. Perhaps people would have made them; it was I who behaved in such a way that nobody dared, and seeing my manner they all held back where they were. But what has been lost yet? It is some days since I gave people leave to come to me; women began coming a week or ten days ago. If I do not talk to them distantly (and what have I done?), one or another of them is sure to hint at it. If I just keep silent on hearing it, people will understand that I do not refuse, and then the matter will go further. But as yet my own mind is not settled.

"First let me decide in my own heart whether to marry again or not. If not, then perhaps there is not much need to meet people either. When I began to meet people and mix with them, it was with this aim, that some way might be found of diverting my heart; and who will know the state of my heart better than I? Of all the ways there are, or can be, of diverting women's hearts, I have left none that I have not thought out thoroughly from beginning to end. None of them appeals to my heart; and indeed it is foolish even to expect that anything in the world can make up for widowhood." Then she remembered that someone had written a tract on the remarriage of widows, and that it must surely be somewhere among her books. She searched it out and looked, and in it was written: "The faculties of sight, hearing, smell, taste and touch that God has given man, which are called the five senses, are, as it were, so many kinds of needs. One need cannot remove another; the work of seeing is not done by hearing, nor of hearing by smelling, and so on. In a person's heart too there are all kinds of desires, for instance the desire for wealth, the desire for children, the desire for name and fame, the desire for health. One who longs for wealth: his craving for wealth cannot be lessened by children, name and fame or health, by nothing of these. In the same way one who longs for children will only be happy when he gets children. If money were given him instead of children, or bills with his name printed on them were distributed throughout the world, why should he be satisfied? So when no need is removed by another need and no desire fulfilled by another desire, nor can be, then the desire for married life too can be fulfilled, if at all, only by married life. There is no doubt that human needs are not of equal degree; for instance, one need is respiration, that is, breathing, without which a person cannot live even two or three minutes. Below that is the need for food and drink; well, one has to keep the fasts perforce even in a Ramazan that falls in the month of Jeth [TN: the hottest month]. And there is also a need to live in company with one's own kind, so that if a person wishes to live all alone in the jungle and wilderness, he cannot. Of these needs some are unavoidable, some more pressing.

"In the same way the need for married life is a need too, though it may not be as unavoidable as breathing. This need arises on reaching a certain stage of life, but no one can dispute its being necessary on that account. After all, the need for food and drink too arises only after weaning, and yet everyone knows how necessary food and drink are. The need for married life has been acknowledged in every age by the people of every country, every nation, every religion and every community; even among the most savage nations married life is the custom. And since the spread and survival of the human race depends on married life, this need is a very useful need. When the yogis among the Hindus and the monks among the Christians try to wipe out or suppress this need or its stirrings, what difficulties they meet, until they have to go beyond humanity! And since they oppose God's ordering, their necessary punishment is that life becomes a torment and burden to them. Even so, very few of them truly succeed in their resolve. For this reason Islam strictly forbade monasticism, for monasticism cannot be kept up, and if with a thousand hardships it is kept up, it is by making a person useless. It is no small matter of pride that no Muslim king has supported monasticism. Only the kings of Lucknow used to think of such absurd things. Nasir-ud-Din Haidar invented the *Achhutis* (the Untouched Ones) [TN: women of his household vowed to remain unmarried], and all Lucknow begged to be saved from the very name of those *Achhutis*. But although it is a great matter of pride that Muslim kings did not support monasticism, a far greater matter of shame and regret is that all the Muslims are partisans of monasticism and its supporters and helpers. What is a widow's not marrying again if not open monasticism? When we consider it, we are greatly astonished, since there is no dispute that second marriage is among human needs. In the Quran there is the clear and express command, '*And marry the unmarried among you*' (have your widows married), which leaves no room for any kind of special interpretation. Never mind the Prophet's hadith; there is his own practice: except for the lady Aisha, of all his wives he was the second husband of some and of some even more than the second. Of his three daughters, Fatima was married to Ali al-Murtaza and died while married to him; the remaining two daughters were each married twice. And the households of the whole family of the Prophet and of all the Companions were the same.

"Among those people a widow's second marriage was as necessary, as obligatory and as ordinary as a woman's first marriage is among us. And even now, nowhere, in Arabia, Syria, Turkey, Africa or Persia, do Muslims think there is any kind of disgrace in a widow's second marriage. It is the special misfortune of the Muslims of India alone that they have come to regard a widow's second marriage as shameful. This is the curse on them of living among the Hindus. The fools adopted the Hindus' custom, but did not understand that behind this custom they have lost everything, this world and religion and faith. Not to marry a widow is in itself a very grave sin, not one but two: one sin is the wrong done to the widow, a right of a creature; and disobedience to God is the second sin, a right of God. But not to do it, and not to let it be done, regarding it as shameful and counting it a disgrace, and to look with contempt on the one who does it, and to think the children of a second marriage inferior in nobility of descent: in our view this goes beyond polytheism, unbelief and apostasy, all of them; for it necessarily casts a slur on the honour of the Prophet's family and the saints of religion. What a fine Islam it is: with the mouth they say 'God bless him and give him peace', and 'God Most High be pleased with him', and 'God honour his face', and 'God's mercy be on him'; and in their hearts (God forbid, God forgive us; to repeat unbelief is not unbelief) they hurl foul abuse about wives and daughters at them. Abuse is not a stone, that bones should break or flesh tear or blood flow when it strikes. Wounding someone's honour is what is called abuse, and that is just the case here too."

Azadi trembled on reading this passage, and it was indeed a thing to tremble at. She repented much and begged God's forgiveness, but then began to say by way of excuse: "No doubt I was at fault so far as this, that I did not intend a second marriage and my mind did not turn that way; but not with the thought that a second marriage is shameless and dishonourable; rather I had thought staying a widow to be the way of fidelity and the condition of faithfulness, and that too was my misunderstanding. How should I understand fidelity and faithfulness better than the Prophet's daughters? I cannot equal the very shoes on their feet in anything."
