# Chapter 13: A Man Named Khwaja Mushtaq Sets His Heart on Azadi and Brings in a Go-Between

Among her father's acquaintances, and very likely among his distant relations too, there was a gentleman, Khwaja Mushtaq. Because they thought alike, the two were on very close terms. Although they were not of an age, since their natures were of one kind they were as free with each other as playmates. And people of English ideas care rather little for respect of rank; in their view the Indians' deference and regard belong to cowardice and hypocrisy. We for our part think it bad manners; but Azad was very taken with just this way of Mushtaq's, that, though of an age with Azad's sons, he would smoke a hookah, nay even a cheroot, before him without a thought, would not rise out of respect when he came or went, and took no care that "he is older than I; I must not turn my back on him or sit higher than he does." If a verse came to his lips, he had no scruple about reciting it either. Whistling was his habit. Many a time we saw people gathered, some older than he and some strangers, and if the fancy took him he would begin singing out of season, in time and tune, as if he were alone. Cutting short his elders, raising objections against them: in his view and in the view of his admirer Khwaja Azad, this was what freedom of opinion demanded. Finding the chance, we did check him two or four times, but whenever we checked him Khwaja Azad only egged him on the more. In any case Mushtaq was the one young man after Khwaja Azad's heart, and by holding him up as a model Khwaja Azad wanted to make his own sons just as unrestrained [TN: reading uncertain]. I do not know whether Mushtaq had no room in his own house, or whether he had any work in the world or not. Whenever we saw him, we saw him in Khwaja Azad's house. When there is so much coming and going, what *purdah* can remain? He knew the names of all the women, recognised each one's voice, and knew of all the family's squabbles. With such a man, what need to ask how many times he saw Azadi, or how often Azadi saw him? It would be no wonder if Mushtaq had caught a glimpse of Azadi even after her marriage. In short, the two knew each other well, but there was a great difference in their knowing. Azadi knew Mushtaq only so far as "he is one of Father's acquaintances and is often sitting with Father"; but Mushtaq's knowing was of another kind. From the beginning he had been infatuated with her beauty and cleverness, that is, with all her ways, and from the start he had had the idea of somehow marrying her. Now we cannot say whether Mushtaq came by this idea through coming and going so much, or whether he came and went so much because of this idea. Nor can we say whether the ideas for which Azad held him dear were his real ideas, or whether he had made himself of Khwaja Azad's way of thinking to get access to Azadi; but there is no doubt that if Khwaja Azad had had his way he would have shut his eyes and put Azadi's hand in his.

It would be no wonder either if the matter had been talked of face to face between Azad and Mushtaq and Azad had given Mushtaq hope. A man who wants his daughter to look about and marry by her own choice, why should he, like other foolish fathers, bristle at being called father-in-law, or feel it a disgrace that his daughter's hand should be asked of him directly? This is just where Mushtaq was badly deceived. From Khwaja Azad's courtesy and his special favour he took it that Azadi was already his. He did not know that Azad was free only in word. He was certainly Azadi's father, but he was helpless before his wife and the other members of the family. From his side there was neither note nor message, and there, all of a sudden, Azadi was betrothed. Hearing of the betrothal, Mushtaq was much put out, and turned the earth upside down to get the betrothal broken off somehow; but the betrothal was a betrothal in name, and in reality as binding as a marriage. Yes, if he had known even a little beforehand that talk of a betrothal was going on, he was so smart that he would not have let anyone's plan take root. Helpless, he sat down in patience and thought that this verse fitted his case:

Lost in the thought of the idols' tresses, Nasir, go on beating your breast;
The snake has slipped away, so go on beating the line it left.

But Mushtaq did not give up the thought of Azadi. Azadi can hardly have grieved as much over her husband's death as he grieved over Azadi's being married elsewhere. Seeing Mushtaq's frenzy and distraction, his parents and relations tried hard to bind him somewhere else, but he would not agree at all. They brought in his friends. They reasoned with him: "What is so special about one Azadi? The city is crammed with beauties each better than the last, that a man might sit and gaze at their faces. And if it is her prosperity that has caught you, what count is poor Khwaja Azad? Name any rich man's house you like and we will take your proposal there; and if we don't get you married, we are not who we are. If you are thinking of birth and family, Khwaja Azad is a gentleman of good family, granted; but you can have the daughter of a true Sayyid, noble on both sides. What do you take yourself for? You won't find anyone in the city today to whom your proposal could go without his mouth watering. Brother, the times have become so hard that daughters cannot find husbands. We have seen it among fine clans and good brotherhoods: young women are sitting at home, pure in looks and features, excellent in character and qualities, with no lack of gifts and dowry, and scores of match-makers are let loose; and no one, even falsely, says, 'We are looking for a girl.' We don't know much of the inside story, but the girl's mother's family are out-and-out mullahs. Her grandfather and uncles all live that way; and whatever thousand virtues such people may have, a certain meanness does come into the nature of anyone who has eaten the bread of the *maulvi*'s calling. How could the ways of her mother's family have failed to have an effect? Would you have got on with such a woman? There was some good purpose in that woman's not falling to your lot. You did well to think of her; let it go, put it away: 'the meat for the eater, the bone for the dog.' Look what matches we have thought of for you; when you hear them you will jump for joy." Mushtaq sat silent and listened to all this, and, as they say, the game cock has only one leg [TN: proverb: the stubborn man sticks to his one point], when he did speak he said: "If I married, I would have married her. Now she has slipped from my hands, and alas, through my own mistake and blunder the trapped prey has escaped. As long as I live I shall not forget this blow. And why do you urge me to marry? I know the state of Indian society well, since I too was born in this society and grew up in this society. I do not believe any Indian woman can give me the happiness I can expect as a husband. I find very little difference between the women of this country and animals, very little indeed. They have neither the capacity to be wives nor to be mothers. And where would they get the capacity? There is no education, no training. Don't call them wives: honoured slave girls, favoured maids. For what do they know besides the chores and service of the house (and even the chores and service only middling)? Their ability is such that if some luckless householder has to travel, then for as many days as he spends travelling the wife is a widow and the husband a widower. He cannot write his heart to her, nor she to him. The lady is called the wise woman of the whole neighbourhood; whatever the purchase, she does not trust it until she has settled the price at the door herself; but, bless her, she cannot tell you, if copper is seventeen and a half annas a seer, what is the price of three quarters of a seer and one and a half *chhatanks* plus two pice.

"Give her two months' grace to sit and reckon, but I swear you to it, she must not ask any man! The grocer and the butcher and the lace seller and the coppersmith and the cloth merchant and the goldsmith: in short, whoever she has dealings with cheats her, and this simple Begam knows nothing of it. Although she herself and her mother and grandmother, and perhaps great-grandmother too, were born under English rule, and, to crown it, all the men of the house are in service, one a head clerk, one a *munshi*, one a *tahsildar* (there is no government department in which there are not one or two employees of their house), what would she know of the Viceroy and the Lieutenant-Governor and the judges of the High Court and the Court? She does not know the difference between a Collector and a Magistrate. I swore oaths, and she would not believe that there is no magic in the telegraph and no puppet runs along it. How often I explained that the railway runs by the force of steam, but she just kept on giving me the lie. She probably heard from the sweeper woman of the neighbourhood that the heir to the throne of Russia was coming. By chance, God knows in what connection, it slipped from her mouth in front of me, and I said: 'Aha! So now you keep up with the great news of the world! Well then, tell me, where is Russia?' And what answer does she give? 'Hang it, wherever it is, let it go to the stove! Have you ever seen *kachnar* buds [TN: reading uncertain; an Indian vegetable] being sold there?' For some years the government has had registers of deaths and births made, and these registers have proved that out of every six children five die: one because we do not follow the rules of hygiene, one through the guesswork treatment of the Greek-medicine physicians, and three through the friendship of foolish friends, that is, the blunders of mothers, in whose view children have no illness but the evil eye and the influence of evil spirits. And the one hardy child who survives, being brought up under the care of an ignorant mother, grows up a dullard and a block of unhewn wood; and what else could he become? So if you ask the truth, these women have so ruined the race of Adam's children that hope of putting it right is now faint. Will a man with such ideas be drawn to any woman? Even that, I don't know how it was, that I was caught in the snare of one woman's love. You say the city is crammed with many women more beautiful than she. If I am not handsome myself, our family is counted among the handsome families of the city. I have seen many beauties, but the one I chose is something else altogether. I saw her from an age when she had not even the sense to understand what beauty means.

"Even then she had that God-given bloom which no dressing and adorning can attain. You people perhaps think a fair complexion and well-shaped features are beauty; in my view real beauty is that every gesture should be enchanting. That I have seen in no one but her. I have seen her weep, seen her sulk, seen her fight, seen her lose her temper, seen her laugh, seen her be coaxed, seen her coax, seen her blush. God has given her a wondrous kind of beauty that is unbecoming in no state. My belief is that God created her as a model of perfect beauty of the highest degree. And it is the rule that when the envelope is fine, what is enclosed is far finer. So, as her body is delicate, her heart must surely be pure. In short, don't even think that I shall so much as look at anyone but her. No doubt she is out of my reach now, but my love for her will last as long as my life."

Despairing of Azadi, Mushtaq wandered aimlessly about the graveyards for months; then he vanished for a long time; it was heard that he had gone off somewhere outside. After a long while there was news of him, and people went and brought him back. When he came here he fell so ill that at last the physicians gave him up. When he heard the news of Mustajab's death he came back to life with the hope: "Azadi is not one to stay a widow. She is young, has no children, is on bad terms with her in-laws; her mother's family are *maulvis*, so that if they do not press for a second marriage they cannot oppose one either; her father's opinion is known, he would probably not think even the *iddat* necessary. So Azadi will marry again, she certainly will. Such a chance has come from God; I must not let it slip from my hands." Now he began to think out schemes: "What plan shall I make? Though I have stopped going to Khwaja Azad's, God forbid, I did not quarrel with him or fight with him, and there was never any occasion to make a complaint of any kind to his face; and he is such a free and easy man in himself that perhaps he has not even noticed my absence; and if he has noticed, he must surely feel ashamed in his heart, for certainly it was not his wish to give his daughter to the mullahs. What happened, happened against his opinion, and this affair exposed his helplessness in household matters. And now, if such a chance comes, he will certainly support me to make up for the past. In short, in the first place he is not displeased with me, and there is no reason for him to be displeased; and if he is, it is no hard matter to win him over. And if he is displeased, it will be with the thought that I have stayed away out of pique with him; and that thought is to my advantage. I say, God grant he has thought so; and if he has not, let him think so now. If he thinks of my pique, its reason will certainly come to his mind along with it, and that is just what I want. It will be clear to him that my suit was not an ordinary suit but carried insistence and urgency with it. But well, that was an occasion for him to interfere. Now it is most likely that Khwaja Sahib will leave it entirely to his daughter's opinion. Even then he wanted it to be by his daughter's choice; but Indian society is not yet ready for such things. I think that once a woman has been married, the bonds of deference do not remain so strict on her. So now everything rests with Azadi herself; it is she who must be won over. Hadi Begam will try her hardest on her side to keep her daughter under her control; but Azadi's temper is very proud. I do not think that, getting thirty rupees a month from Bhopal, she would lie about living on her father's scraps. Besides, she is not on such very good terms with her brothers and their wives. In any case nothing will happen till the *iddat* is over. For the present all I need to do is to have some person of mine attached to Azadi, who will keep me informed day by day and from time to time of how things are there; and that person must be so tactful as to keep sounding the hearts of all of them, and, when it is seen that a second marriage is about to be proposed, not let anyone's word but mine make headway." Mushtaq had not drifted in from somewhere and settled in Delhi; his family had been in this city for generations, and he had so many relations near and far that, had he wished, he could have set scores of women on Azadi to hover over her at all times like the recording angels. But he thought: "This is the last stroke; it must not miss. Choose such a person as will tell me things plainly and frankly, give me advice, and win Azadi's confidence." At last it was decided that for this work there should be some match-maker, very cunning and very experienced.

In those days a go-between called Chhalawa (the Will-o'-the-Wisp) was very famous. Mushtaq had never had any need of such women; I don't know where he had heard the name Chhalawa. He sent for her, seated her with every courtesy, told her the whole story and held out inducements. When Mushtaq began to plead and beg too much, Chhalawa said: "Why, sir, is this any kind of task at all? Consider Azadi already at your service. If you tell me to, I will bring her and seat her beside you even within her *iddat*; but you want to make her your wife by a proper marriage, so what need is there for haste? Just set your mind at rest about this, and go and get your household things in order. Since your slave girl has taken on this task, be assured that Azadi will marry again, she certainly will, and marry you." After that Chhalawa did not show herself, but in her own way she kept ferreting out every little thing and passing it on to Mushtaq. The sermons, the mother staying with her daughter all the time, Azadi's restlessness, her constant remembering of Mustajab: hearing all this, Mushtaq kept losing hope: "Azadi's second marriage does not seem to be coming about. There have been so many sermons; every *maulvi* who came came preaching patience; not one, even by a slip of the tongue, said so much as that a widow is also enjoined to marry again. The kind of piety the *maulvis* have nowadays is known: rulings fixed in advance, points of law settled beforehand, sermons to order; that is what the *maulvi*'s calling has become. So as the *maulvis* are, so are their devotees; as the sermons, so the effect; as the rulings, so the dealings. And what good news is it for me that the mother clings to her daughter like her shadow? From one to a hundred thousand, she is not one to say a good word for me. She knows me well, and in a way she bristles at my very name; and it was this very lady who chose Mustajab. And since she is a *maulvi*'s daughter, there is great bigotry in her temper; day and night she is at odds with her husband. I have never seen so obstinate a woman, who is silenced again and again and still not convinced. God keep us from that blow under the arm. And when the very root of the matter, Azadi herself, is in such a state that she never forgets her first husband, then the kite has broken loose from the reel; what on earth will there be to fly against? In short, things look as if they have gone wrong. If Azadi were mine by fate, why would the first game have been spoiled?" Chhalawa heard this despairing speech of Mushtaq's just as we sometimes listen to the talk of foolish children, and began to say with a smile: "Truly, men are very simple, and you have not had even a breath of the world's air.

"My dear sir, never mind other things: Azadi's never forgetting her husband is itself the great sign that she is not one to stay a widow. It shows that her nature is one that forms attachments. People of that temper cannot live without being attached to someone or other. Think of those among your acquaintances whose wives die; it has been seen many a time that the one who was most attached to his wife is the one who marries again soonest. Why? Because having a woman is a condition of life for him. So far nothing has gone wrong at all; but if you make untimely haste and spoil it, I can't say. Sit patiently, and see what happens. This much I do say: if I don't bring Azadi and seat her in your house, let me not be called Chhalawa. I have told you already that Azadi is no matter at all; you should have given me some very hardest task and seen what this nobody can do. If I set about doing evil, I could have Azadi standing here by this very evening; but you do not want that, and I too say, well, there is no need."
