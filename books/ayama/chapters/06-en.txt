# Chapter 5: The Plan to Marry Azadi among the Maulvis, and an Account of the Maulvis

Azadi was fearfully clever. She knew her mother's ideas and was aware of her powers, and she had guessed beforehand that, just as her mother was herself, and as she wanted Father to be, so, with a difference of nineteen to twenty, she would dig out a son-in-law of exactly that kind from somewhere. And so it turned out. May God not make me tell a lie: proposals for Azadi came from some ten or twelve places, each better than the last, each finer than the other; but Hadi Begam, like soul like angels, fell for a mullah of her own stamp. Seeing how the times were going, people told her: "Look out for some go-ahead young man among these school people," and they even named several: "So-and-so's son passed the Entrance two years ago and draws a scholarship of ten rupees from such-and-such." But Hadi Begam did not soften a bit. She would cry out: "I cannot swallow a fly with my eyes open. All my life I have suffered the plague of that school. Am I Azadi's stepmother, to shut my eyes and push her into the pit? It is true the school people never lack for employment, and I don't deny that they are clever too; but they have this very grave fault, that their beliefs are not sound. So what is the use of a worldly good that harms one's religion? Let it go to the furnace! We are not to stay in the world for ever. Life is short; it will pass badly and it will pass well. I count it the great success of life that the end should be good, and that one should stand well with God when one goes there. With what difficulty I have kept my faith safe, God alone knows, and that too with the help of my parents' house, where they never let the Friday sermon be missed. At the least doubt I went at once and cleared it up. And this girl is green wood, and whose daughter is she? If she falls into the hands of someone with wrong beliefs, she will lose her religion the moment she goes and start reciting his creed. Why should I take that sin on my head? And the house I have found is, by God's grace, comfortable and well off. They are said to live on trust in God, but their standing is no lower than that of people in service. The ladies have gold jewellery, and good clothes too, the fine muslin we wear ourselves. They have not one maidservant but two. The house has a good name; God be praised, nobody among their in-laws has any complaint. The boy, God bless his life, all my family say he is a good, upright young man. He has finished the necessary books; for a little while now his father has set him to preaching, and those who heard him praised his discourse. So, my good people, I have agreed only after seeing that everything is good; for the rest it is her fate."

Azadi had grown rather sad on hearing of her betrothal among the *maulvis*. She too came of a *maulvi* family; not on her father's side, but on her mother's side the *maulvi*'s calling was still followed, in that they were nobody's servants or retainers: they taught students, preached, wrote *fatwas* (legal opinions), led prayers, and all was well. Worldly people served them at home and counted it a blessing. From their spending it was clear that they must have something fixed by way of salary from two or four places as well, but the *maulvis* would not let it be found out. In any case their way of life was: shabby outside, well stocked within. Dishes that we poor folk (and what count are we poor wretches?), dishes that even good rich men taste only now and then, God feeds the *maulvis* free, morning and evening, without trouble or toil, without "turmeric or alum" [TN: proverb: without spending anything]. The *maulvis* do not believe in unlucky days, and in truth the thing has no basis; all days are God's. But we see that, not many in a month, two or four unlucky days do pass even for the *maulvis*. Which are they? The days on which they have to light a fire in their own stove. What can I say? At the start I made such a mistake that I shall regret it all my life: I saw the outward look of people in service, well off, and I was taken in and thought there was no better way of earning a living. I took service, and, thanks be to God, with honour, with authority, with a large salary; but then when I compared it with the position of the *maulvis*, their side of the scale was the heavier. First, service, great or small, is obedience to another, whose other name is slavery; and in that one word slavery everything is included: dishonour, flattering one's fellow men, seeking to please them, toadying, constant vexation, toil and fear. Second, being far from home: out of a hundred people in service, ninety-nine are abroad, and perhaps one Alexander of fortune is in his own town. First of all [TN: reading uncertain], the *maulvis*: those whose servants they are, that is, from whom they get their bread, go about before and behind them instead. If some Englishman shakes hands[1] with us, we think he has bestowed a robe of honour on us; while the *maulvis* have devotees who kiss their feet. A gentleman used to relate of Maulvi Shah Ishaq that after the Friday prayer he would come and sit by the pulpit of the Jama Masjid, and people would crowd round to take his hand, and there was such pushing and shoving that people fell on top of one another. Then the Maulvi Sahib's servant would hang his shawl on a staff and swing it round on all sides, and those who could not reach to take his hand would touch the shawl to their eyes. One does not see that sight now, but there is no doubt that the *maulvis* are still honoured and respected in something close to that way:

O pious man, to each his fortune:
To you the tree of Paradise, to us the beloved's tall form.

As for being far from home: the *maulvis* will certainly have read in the books the rules for the traveller's prayer, but ask whether any of them has ever in all his life had occasion to say the traveller's prayer. Never. And these petty [TN: reading uncertain] *maulvis* who go about touring from village to village far and wide: what is their case? In the city they cut no ice; they have looked at two or four Urdu tracts and learnt a few great ignorances [TN: a pun on *mas'ala*, "point of law"] by heart, which is plenty for coaxing peasants. Whatever village they reach, they go and put up in the best house there, that is, the house of God. Word goes round the village. The poor villagers get nothing but bread of millet and sorghum, with lentils and greens; but for the Maulvi Sahib there are thin little cakes of fine flour [TN: reading uncertain], and a pot to drink water from, if there is one. A chicken in the morning, a chicken in the evening, wheaten chapatis; as much milk as he can drink, as much ghee as he can digest. Of the things in season, the green gram[2] or the sweet rice cooked in cane juice, there is no lack. He drinks milk and eats rich food; the fresh clear air of the village, the clean water that aids digestion, freedom from care and the villagers' warm welcome: the Maulvi Sahib, God be praised, came out with such a colour and gloss that when he got back the lady of the house did recognise him, but only after a while. And then there is a tour of ours, a curse on its twists and turns: the sky is pouring down rain, it is so cold that one does not want to put one's face out of the quilt, but a double march is hanging over one's head. Make the stage less than fifteen miles and one is roasted alive. On the way three boundary[3] cases are to be decided. The tour has gone on for too many days; in the quarterly return an excuse for the delay has to be invented, and one order after another comes from the Collector Sahib. Helpless, I ate two biscuits, drank a cup of tea on top of them, and mounted, shivering and shaking. I thought it would certainly be late for a meal, but never mind, by two o'clock I would be in my tent. Here midday came in the very first case. I frightened them and threatened them, but the wretched landholders will give their lives for every inch of land. I told the agents: "Take them aside and talk them round."

Agent: Very well, as Your Honour commands. Your Honour has been put to great trouble. Our side is willing; the other side is being obstinate for nothing. If Your Honour will wait a little, I will have a deed of compromise written by my people and bring it at once.

He said this and went, and when he had gone aside, God knows what lesson the villain taught them, but it was his own side that flew into a rage. The blow to my spirit was beyond description. They were denying plain facts [TN: reading uncertain]; one gets angry and has to hold it in. The mistake was mine: had I given a judge's decision, it would never have taken so long. I wanted a settlement by consent, so that they could not appeal. In short the whole day melted away over one case. At high noon people stopped for a moment to catch their breath, munch some parched grain and drink water, and I too sat down in the shade of a tree. People said to me: "There is nothing fit for Your Honour to be had just now; if you order it, we will bring milk." I thought: "They will not take payment for it, and if I drink free milk I shall have to bring up all the milk I have drunk since my sixth day." I refused. Now how could I tell them that I was so hungry I could not stand, and that sparks were dancing before my eyes? I could not make out a flag at a distance. I was worn out with the whole day's toil, but glad at heart that a very tangled and quarrelsome case had been settled. Now, at the camp, I would stretch out on the carpet and sleep. When I got near the village it was growing dusk, and I looked, and at the place I had chosen by the roadside there was no sign of a tent. Good God, what has happened? Where have all these servants of mine gone to ruin? Have they mistaken the village? I looked about, and in the dimness I could see nobody to ask. I turned towards the village; at the sound of my horse's hoofs the dogs came and surrounded me. The horse had been saddled all day, it was his feeding time, he was ringed by dogs, and he began to rear. At last I got down and went into the village, and it was deserted. After a long while a watchman came out, and he told me that the camp had halted in the little grove[4] by the boundary, about a *kos* (some two miles) away. That one *kos* was a whole stage in itself. When I reached the place I found that the tents were not yet up. At the news my heart simply sank. The landholders, the grocers and the shopkeepers had all fled on hearing that the camp was coming; not a sparrow was left in the village. Firewood, grass, flour, lentils: no one could get anything. The animals were standing shivering, and the men sat huddled, shivering. People must have managed something for themselves. My men did me at least this one act of loyalty in all their lives: they had a little rice by them and quickly boiled plain rice. In my ravenous hunger that tasteless rice was so good that to this day I have never happened to eat so delicious a meal. So I filled the hell of my belly and fell asleep under a tree as dead to the world as if a snake had sniffed me; but the misfortune was not over yet. It was the season of the rotting [TN: reading uncertain] spring; suddenly in the night clouds came up, hail fell, and it rained so hard that the water stood knee-deep in the grove. At daybreak, with a thousand difficulties, I went and took shelter in the village. Everyone crowded in wherever his horns would fit. People chose the mosque for me, and in truth there was no better place in the whole village. It was in that mosque that I saw the Maulvi Sahib I mentioned above. My teeth were chattering, and there was the Maulvi Sahib with a fire blazing in his cell, sitting at his ease in a single-layer shirt. Yes indeed, how could he fail to be warm with all those chickens inside him? Looking at the *maulvis*, I always remember in spite of myself the quatrain of Shaikh Ibrahim Zauq:

Qit'a (a verse fragment)

The worldly man makes offerings to the self-denying;
How great is the indifference of my Lord!
How truly someone said this, O Zauq:
"The wealth of the oppressor is the lot of the holy warrior."

Whether Khwaja Azad envied the position of the *maulvis*, or it was the effect of the education he had received, I do not know; but there is no doubt that he was very ill-disposed towards the *maulvis*. And this was the cause of the fighting between husband and wife, the disagreement, the quarrelling, the bad feeling. Since Khwaja Azad spoke like a shopkeeper [TN: reading uncertain], Hadi Begam would flare up, and this was a want of tact on Khwaja Azad's part as well. If speaking ill of *maulvis* is made a sore point with a *maulvi*'s daughter, granddaughter and great-granddaughter, how can she not take it ill? The result was that the more Khwaja Azad provoked her, the more Hadi Begam's obstinacy grew. Hadi Begam was very proud of her family's trust in God, and Khwaja Azad, to tease and provoke her, would say:

Azad: Begging, call it begging. What you call trust in God is to my mind worse even than begging. If a beggar begs, perhaps he is crippled, disabled; he has starved his belly; he goes from door to door, here he is pushed about, there he is scolded, and somewhere he gets a crust. These men are hale and hearty, strapping, well fed; they are not blind or crippled or lame. What right have they to live on others' earnings? And then with such high-handedness and arrogance that they sit about as revered masters?

Hadi Begam: You are not crippled either. What are you paid a salary for?

Azad: I work. I give my time in return for the salary.

Hadi: The *maulvis* work too. They preach, they teach students, they tell people points of religious law. Is their time not spent on these things?

Azad: Then people would not serve the *maulvis* thinking it a meritorious act. When we pay anyone wages we certainly do not expect a heavenly reward for it. What merit is there in giving someone his due?

Hadi: Then the reward is for this, that our money was spent on a good work. If the *maulvis* are free from worry about a living, they will guide the people.

Azad: It is one and the same reward, which the *maulvis* claim on their side and those who serve the *maulvis* hope for on theirs.

Hadi: Does God lack anything? He is very generous; it is in His power to grant as much reward as He likes to whom He likes for a single act of worship. But why would you have looked into such things, and if you had, why would you remember them? In the noble hadith it is said (may God forgive me if I am out by a word or two) that the Prophet gave a parable of the Jews, the Christians and his own community: a man hired some labourers at a fixed wage and had them work from morning till midday, and at midday he paid them their wages in full. From the last part of the afternoon till evening he had other labourers work at double wages, and at sunset he paid them double. Can the labourers who worked first complain that they got less? Not at all. He gave this parable to show that the community of our Prophet has a short life, little worship and much reward.

Khwaja Azad: That hadith has nothing to do with the point. If God wishes to scatter His reward as carelessly as Indian noblemen scatter their wealth, then certainly no one can stay His hand; but I am not arguing that now. I say that in your view the *maulvis* and those who serve them both deserve the reward; but in my view the weakness of Islam, the ruin of the Muslims, their wretchedness, poverty, low spirit and incompetence, the guilt of all of it lies on the necks of the *maulvis*. God has made the English rulers of the age because they deserved it, and has set them over us by the power of intelligence. You take your authority from the hadith, and I cite the Quran: "And We have written in the Psalms, after the Reminder, that the earth shall be inherited by My righteous servants."[5] Well, is that a verse of the Quran or not? Very well, go to your parents' house and find out: the commentators have written that "the righteous" means those who are fit to govern. We cannot match the English in anything: not in bravery, not in hard work, not in endurance, not in ability, not in cleverness. And if we had that in us, how would the English have been able to come five thousand *kos* to India and rule here? So we have become subjects, and if the days and nights go on like this, with the strong man's thumb on our heads, we shall have to stay subjects for hundreds of years to come. There is no good for us in sulking and quarrelling with them. Living in the river and feuding with the crocodile? But such stones have fallen on the *maulvis*' wits that even so plain a thing does not enter their understanding. What on earth can they understand of the *Hidaya* and the *Durr-i Mukhtar* [TN: standard books of Hanafi law]? Why, for one's need one must call even a donkey father; and the English are People of the Book, whom God has praised in many places in the Quran. Say it with your mouth: has He or not? And apart from their being People of the Book, under their rule there is so much peace and comfort and justice and care for the subjects that India has never known such security. The *maulvis* have made unbelief cheaper than radishes and carrots, and at every turn they declare people infidels and polytheists; but here, since their own foot is in the middle of it, they never, not even by mistake, look at "He who does not thank people does not thank God."[6]

Whereas not to value English rule is plain ingratitude for God's bounty.

Hadi Begam: Why are you after the *maulvis*? What have the poor things taken from you? These poor men have nothing to do with anyone, taking or giving; they have no train or army; they sit in the mosques and pray to God. If someone comes to study, they teach him; if someone asks a point of law, they tell him; or the one who preaches teaches matters of prayer and fasting to a few Muslims who care about religion and come and sit with him. Do the *maulvis* do anything besides? You have taken up a feud with them for no reason. The trouble of the stable falls on the monkey's head; everything is laid on the *maulvis*. "The poor man's wife is everyone's sister-in-law." Just think, how many people turn to the *maulvis*? Hardly one in a hundred. And there is no reason why many should turn to the *maulvis*. The *maulvis* will only say: say your prayers, keep the fast of Ramazan, give *zakat* (the alms tax) if you can afford it, make the pilgrimage, do not take intoxicants, do not go to fairs, do not watch dancing and shows, do not look on a strange woman with evil eyes, do not steal, do not lie, do not fight and quarrel, do not harass anyone, live in harmony and friendliness, do not swear oaths, do not speak ill of anyone behind his back, be kind to the young and respectful to the old, do not let the wealth God has given you go to waste, and be ready at all times to die and go before God Most High to answer for your deeds. How many people are there who will want to listen gladly to such things? The same, hardly one in a hundred. So if the *maulvis* really do lead people astray, put up with the one, and you take on the job of reforming the ninety-nine. What a topsy-turvy idea, to blame the *maulvis* for people going wrong! People have gone wrong through their own irreligion, through disobedience to God and the Prophet. People do not have the truthfulness of old, nor honest dealing, nor lawful earnings, nor the same blessing. If there were Muslim rule today, how could such irreligion spread among the people? Everyone does whatever he likes, and there is no one to stop him or forbid him. The voice of a parakeet in the drum-house: who listens to the *maulvis*? And you have started sneering at the *maulvis* at a time when they hardly exist any more!

Up to the time of the Mutiny it was so that, leaving aside the colleges and the Sufi lodges, no mosque was without its *maulvi*, and *maulvis* not in name only, but men of such learning that all India acknowledged their mastery and their *fatwas* were current as far as Arabia. And they were so honoured that even the king came to their houses. Akbar Shah sent for Maulvi Isma'il again and again to preach, and the Maulvi Sahib told him plainly: "Divorce your harem women and concubines, and I will come." Akbar Shah died, and Maulvi Isma'il never set foot inside the Fort, never. It is a well-known story that when Maulvi Shah Ishaq emigrated, his library and goods went to his four daughters, and a hundred thousand rupees fell to each daughter's share. That Mutiny was a Day of Judgement for the *maulvis*: many were killed and wiped out, many were exiled; a few here and there survived, but the great houses that used to support the *maulvis* were no more. The *maulvis* began to go begging from door to door, and in truth what you see is begging. At last, driven by want, the *maulvis* gave up the calling altogether. You will find hundreds of men in the city whose forefathers were famous *maulvis*, and these men do not know their A from their B. And of those who still follow the *maulvi*'s calling, there may perhaps be one who has held out; otherwise all their children are learning English, if not openly then on the sly. So the *maulvi*'s calling is finished in any case. These few old graybeards who keep up their old ways: as soon as their eyes close, the sky will be clear. "Surely we belong to God, and to Him we return." In short, the blame on the *maulvis* is entirely unjust: the fault is our own, and we plaster it on others.

Khwaja Azad: It is true that very few fish are seen caught in the *maulvis*' net; but just as one match is enough to burn a whole city, one *maulvi* can influence the Muslims of a whole country. One listens, and a thousand learn from him. There are many people who outwardly have no connection of any kind with the *maulvis*; perhaps they do not say their prayers at all, so as to go to the mosque and pray behind the Maulvi Sahib or hear his sermon. They are Muslims in name only, because they were born in Muslim houses. They have nothing to do with religion and faith, no care for God's commands, no concern with the sayings of the Prophet; they have taken up a way of life in which it is hard to tell them apart from ordinary animals. Yet with all that, even these people are not free of the *maulvis*' rule. Otherwise, tell me: this general hatred, this general aversion, this general estrangement that Muslims have from the English, which has ruined the Muslims and will go on to ruin what is left of them, where does it come from? Where did this curse come from? Certainly this idea arose from the *maulvis* and spread among ordinary Muslims. The Hindus are open idol-worshippers, and yet there is such mingling with them that Hindus and Muslims are as close as bodice and skirt. Muslims join their fairs and follow their customs, and they keep so much aloof from the People of the Book!

Hadi Begam: Living in one place, you cannot get on without mixing.

Khwaja Azad: Bless you! Then it is all the more necessary to be on close terms with the English. The Hindus and we are neighbours, and the English and we are rulers and ruled. A neighbour can get along without a neighbour; the ruled cannot get along for a moment without the ruler.

Hadi Begam: Close terms can only be between equals. Why would the English have anything to do with Indians?

Azad: Yes, that is true too; but what I mean is that we should not keep up ill feeling on our side.

Hadi: Well, can such a thing happen?

Khwaja Azad: You ask whether it can happen? Why, that is the great kink in the matter. The *maulvis* have made religion an excuse and frightened people so much that even now nobody learns English, or learns the sciences of the English, with an open heart.

Hadi: Why do you wrongly pile up the *maulvis*' sins for no reason? Do the *maulvis* go about after people with a whip? You must be raving about the *maulvis* like this before the officials too.

Khwaja Azad: The officials? I am ready to complain of them before God Himself.

Hadi: And you will surely get a hearing. Well, what sort of complaints?

Azad: If there were one complaint I would tell it. There are dozens, scores. Number one is the same: they have no right to live on other people's earnings. Second, they teach others renunciation, and themselves do not leave even a dead man's prayer-mat. Many of them, idle as they were themselves, have trained up a whole regiment of idlers, that is, their pupils, who have eaten up the whole country and not even belched. Fourth, hypocrisy: they put on a show of goodness for people to see, and how good they really are is known. Fifth, they know such tricks for cheating and deceiving people that even the most cunning thief is nothing to them. They collect subscriptions in the name of building and repairing mosques and spend them freely on themselves, as if they were mother's milk or a father's legacy. They make raids on Calcutta and Bombay and Dhaka and Patna and Hyderabad and Madras and far-off cities: "We have started an Islamic college in Delhi. Muslims should certainly help in this good work, each as much as he can, for the teachers' salaries, the students' stipends and the books. 'Surely God does not let the reward of those who do good be lost.'"[7] People see a holy-looking man in a long shirt and gown, an Arab turban on his head, a callus on his forehead from prostration, a long beard and short trousers, slipshod shoes, blinking his eyes and nodding his head, asking help for a good cause; and Delhi is the home of Islam, and the Muslims there have been left without means since the Mutiny. To give to this college is to place one's money in God's hand and buy Paradise. They give, and give with open hearts. Who comes so far to find out whether the college exists at all, or, if it does, whether even half a pice is spent on it? Whatever he gets, cash and goods, the Maulvi Sahib brings and hands over to his wife: "Here, my good woman. 'As long as fools are left in the world, no one stays poor.' Eat and dress, but in such a way that no one's evil eye falls on it; 'the evil eye is real.'"[8] Many people have the habit, when they have to give *zakat*, or quilts in winter, or whatever is to be given in God's name, of bringing it all together and presenting it to the Maulvi Sahib: "Please pass it on in fitting measure to whomever you think deserving." This much we will grant, that the Maulvi Sahib did think of everyone, widows and orphans and the poor and the needy and the helpless and travellers, and along with all of them of himself and his family too. Now it is an error of judgement[9] that he thought himself the most deserving. Suspicion is a bad thing, "surely some suspicion is a sin,"[10] especially about a man of learning; but to this day the riddle has not been solved why the Maulvi Sahib has so many floor-sheets made of coarse cloth. Well, all right, each to his own taste; but how is it that every floor-sheet has exactly the seams of a quilt cover?

About the Maulvi Sahib's intentions anyone may say what he likes; one cannot hold the tongue of a speaker. But in interpretation this man is a king, of acknowledged authority. He thinks and brings forth from his brain such a lawful dodge that none of the four Imams can catch him out. Though he owns many times the *nisab*[11], he considers it lawful to take alms. One day some unbeliever like us spoke up: "But you are rich." He replied: "God calls me poor: 'And God is the Rich, and you are the poor.' 'You' is the pronoun of the second person plural, and you make me rich. Now I don't know which of you two is right." The Prophet's arrangements were such that he forbade *zakat* to his own descendants, so that they should not become, like the *maulvis*, lazy, ease-loving and dependent on others. The Maulvi Sahib's difficulty is that he must also be a Sayyid, for noble descent too is a means of honour, and yet he cannot let go of the *zakat* that has come before him. God gives and His servant takes. You must have solved many riddles, perhaps untangled the hardest puzzles too; but I shall believe in you when you find a way by which neither his Sayyid descent nor his *maulvi*-ship is stained and the *zakat* is not lost either. Well, what can you tell? I too beat my head over it long enough; at last I gave up, had no choice, and turned to the Maulvi Sahib himself, and it turned out that to him it is nothing at all; he has hundreds of such knacks at his fingertips. One small dodge is to give it to a deserving person and then, on some pretext, have him make it over to oneself as a gift: "For you it is alms, and for us a present."[12] I saw a friend practise a dodge of this kind; he must surely have learnt it from some *maulvi*, for why else would anyone's mind turn that way? A silver betel box is filled with rolled betel leaves. He took a betel roll out of the box and laid it for a moment on the bolster, and from the bolster put it in his mouth. "Why, sir, what is this?" What brazenness! He says the use of silver is forbidden. If you set about looking for such pretexts, there is no command left and no prohibition.

Well, the efforts the *maulvis* make to earn their living, all these difficulties for the sake of eating, are the falseness of their own hearts. What makes me angry is their stupidity, that these enemies of reason are hollowing out the roots of the very tree they have climbed to pick the fruit. Every *maulvi*, for the sake of his own fame, invents some notion or other that makes Muslims start when they hear it. The result is that new sects keep springing up, and they fight one another so foully that it would not be right to fight even infidels with such unseemliness and indecency. The fall of the empire had already weakened Islam; Shah Madar strikes the dying man [TN: proverb: one blow on top of another]; these foolish friends will not let it recover.

God knows how long Khwaja Azad had been full of spite against the *maulvis*, for he strung together a whole chain of complaints about them. In the end Hadi Begam got fed up and rose and left, and if she had not, Azad would have gone on for hours. Azad even tried to stop her as she went: "Just listen a little more, just listen a little more"; but Hadi Begam rose and went off saying, "Enough, I have heard plenty."

Hadi Begam: The *maulvis* are the heirs of the prophets, and this is the abuse they get! The five fingers are not equal; there may be some among them like that, and you have started driving them all with one stick. But this much I will still say: cunning or not, deceitful or not, whatever is left of the religion of Islam is thanks to them. If all Muslims were like you, not even the sound of the call to prayer would reach our ears.
