# Chapter 11: Azadi's Mourning, and Her Thoughts at the Beginning of Her Widowhood

In the days when the late Mustajab lost heart and began to preach less, his father had said to him by way of advice: "Your preaching is like children playing tag; there was our preaching, when we drew out one part of the Quran over a whole year and preached for a watch and a quarter or a watch and a half at a stretch." We took that for self-praise and exaggeration: how could one part be drawn out over a whole year? Now, having heard Maulvi Muqtada's sermon, we believe that God has given some *maulvis* just such eloquence and flow that, never mind a part, they could, if they wished, draw out a single section over a whole year. Maulvi Muqtada spent a good full watch on one verse, and even then, mindful of the shortness of time, left out many things that had come to his lips. There was a gathering of five or six hundred people, men and women together; all were moved each in their own way, and it must surely have had a special effect on Azadi, for throughout the sermon the Maulvi Sahib's words were addressed to her. But are such great griefs, such heavy blows, to be forgotten? Cheerfulness was nowhere to be found; throughout her *iddat* she remained more or less silent. This much difference there certainly was: God knows whether they were prayers she had missed in her life or voluntary ones, but she began to pray a great deal; and whereas her practice had been to recite a part or a part and a quarter of the Quran, she now took up the recitation of the whole Quran in a week [TN: *fa-mi bi-shawq*, a mnemonic for the seven divisions of the Quran], and that with careful chanting too. Her food was not yet a full meal, but neither was it as before, when three or four meals in a row went untouched. In short, she was beginning to recover a little now and then, when her *iddat* came to an end. At this point the poor girl had another shock, like a knock on a fresh wound, but it did not last so long. There are only two ways of forgetting grief: one, that there should be nothing outside to remind the mind of it; the other, that thoughts of grief should not be allowed to arise in the heart. Hadi Begam tried both kinds of means for her daughter: as far as possible she did not let the condolers come near Azadi, since they would come and renew her grief; and she had her hear the *maulvis*' sermons, so that her heart might be drawn to God and the grief go from it. Besides this, she herself clung to her daughter so resolutely that she never left her for a moment. It was one woman's husband who died, and, as is the habit, the widows [TN: reading uncertain] are two. At first we all thought that when her daughter's *iddat* was over she would begin to look after the work of the house; but the *iddat* is over and done with, and the mother will not leave her daughter; and since she will not, let her own house go to the stove, she does not look after it. The daughter became a widow because her husband died, and this one has made herself a widow in her husband's lifetime. From the moment the telegram of her son-in-law's death came, from that day to this, this servant of God has not looked at Khwaja Azad even once to see who he is, and whether she has any kind of concern or business with him or not. God knows what was in Hadi's heart; but outwardly it seemed either that she was somehow ashamed of herself, what would people say with a young widowed daughter in the house, or that she wanted to show off her love for her daughter. Seeing his wife behave like this, Azad was at first rather put out, and then began to talk in a quarrelsome way. Azadi, for her part, had grown weary of her mother always sitting on her chest. Her nature sought solitude, so that, getting away from her mother for an hour or two, she might think out what she would have to do in future; but the difficulty was that she could not say it outright to her mother: "Why are you always pushing into my lap?" At last, just then, an invitation came from Azadi's in-laws: "This was our daughter-in-law's home; well, God did not will it, and we too know that the tie went with the one who made it, and we have no claim left; but if, as people we know, she would come and stay here for a week or ten days to pay a visit, her heart will hardly be diverted, but the soul of the dead will be pleased. For the rest, those who come and those who send have the choice; we on our side will go on clinging to the last breath." The Indians' way of social life has been spoiled so thoroughly that between mothers-in-law and daughters-in-law, between sisters-in-law, that is, between the two families joined by marriage, very little openness remains after the marriage; and even what we call openness means only that there is no outward quarrelling and scandal; otherwise there are heaps of grudges and piles of complaints in their hearts.

As was the rule, there had never been any fighting or quarrelling between Azadi and her in-laws either, but there was no friendship or love; and in this the fault was Azadi's. As soon as she went there she brought her husband so completely under her control that the late man would not break a straw without her wish. On top of that came his giving up preaching, turning away from the *maulvi*'s calling, taking service, and going to Bhopal to die. Although Azadi had no hand of any kind in these things, and neither misled her husband nor taught him, what remedy is there for suspicion? The in-laws had written down all these sins in her account of deeds. In the very laments that Azadi's mother-in-law made over her son's death there was complaint of her. Now that they had invited her, there was no scent of sympathy in the invitation either; but Azadi got a chance to escape from her mother's grip. For how many days she had been thinking of this! She often used to reflect: "Mother never moves away from me for a moment. I don't say she has set a guard over me; rather, in her own idea, she wants to divert my mind: 'If she sits alone she will think of her home and her heart will ache; better not to give her any such chance.' In her mother's love she cannot see that her clinging to me all the time pains me almost as much as the thought of my own state; and this state God has brought on me anyway. I do not become a married woman again by not thinking, and how long shall I go without thinking? My in-laws were always cross with me of their own accord even in his lifetime, and now it was I who forcibly pushed him off abroad; he would not have gone abroad, nor lost his life for nothing. Seeing me, the blood rises into their eyes; and when do they think of me as one who will stay a widow? They lay bets among themselves and swear that she will marry again, see if she doesn't. As for my parents' house, there (may God keep my mother alive) I can make every kind of claim. My brothers' wives sometimes grumble, but such is the relation between us; and with Mother there, what do I care for brothers and their wives? But what a snag has come in: because of me a quarrel has arisen between Mother and Father, and it goes on growing. What a calamity that Mother does not understand at all, and is ruining her lifelong home for my sake! And the Nawab, in his lifetime, has made me an allowance of thirty rupees too; alas, alas, taking me for a widow!

"God forbid, I am not going to take it. The man whose widow I would be in taking the allowance hated income of that kind. Never mind, I will take in sewing; I will eat not twice a day but once. It is plenty for my single self, and what do I need now anyway? Wear something coarse, eat something plain and dry, and God be praised, all is well. But the Nawab did not write in the letter that he was making me an allowance as a widow. Was he such a simpleton that there was a shortage of widows in his own country, so he came to fix an allowance for me here in Delhi? And what was special about me even in Delhi? This allowance is compensation for a life. I'll take it from the Nawab with a good grace, and if he does not give it, I'll go and stand before the Resident. I am not going to lay my burden on my parents; they married me off once, gave me what was in my fate and sent me off; they did not take a contract for my whole life, that I should come and fall on their heads again. Say what you like, I cannot live in that house with the same honour as I had in his lifetime. And then there is one great drawback, that I shall have to cut my horns and join the calves. The kind of independence I gained by being married will gradually be lost altogether. I can see the end: in that house my standing will be what a maid's or a slave girl's is. Never mind; I will take opium and go to sleep for good. I have never heard the taunts of brothers and their wives, nor will I hear them in future." The fact is that from grief Azadi had also become touchy, and her suspicion had grown so much that she thought of aspects of things that would never have entered anyone's mind but hers. She invented causes for things that had no basis, drew wrong conclusions from words. But no doubt she had thought rightly that "neither in my parents' house nor in my in-laws' can I get on anywhere." Azadi was sitting full of these thoughts when the maid from her in-laws came with the invitation. Hadi Begam made a sign to the maid to sit in the verandah, thinking, "What need is there to talk with her in front of Azadi? I'll take her aside and make some excuse." But Azadi was looking for a pretext to leave her parents' house and an excuse to get out. She said to her mother rather sharply: "Why, lady, let her come!" The maid gave the invitation, and Hadi Begam began to make excuses. Azadi spoke up: "Yes, yes, I will go." Her mother did not think it proper to argue with her daughter, and did well. Azadi went to her in-laws, stayed a few days and saw how things were there.

As she had expected, she found people more reserved towards her than ever. They went on talking for form's sake, and she stayed four days and took her leave. People took it that she was going to her parents' house, and she went and got down at her own separate house, in which she used to live in her husband's lifetime. Her own family too learnt of it only the next day, when the maid went as usual to ask after her welfare. As soon as she heard, Hadi Begam sent for a palanquin; just then Azad came in. Seeing his wife getting ready to go, he asked: "Where are you going at this hour?"

Hadi: Yesterday Azadi came back from her in-laws and got down at her own separate house.

Khwaja Azad: Well?

Hadi: I am going to put her in a palanquin and bring her straight back.

Khwaja Azad: And if she does not come?

Hadi: Then I shall stay there; I cannot leave her alone.

Azad: It is for just such a day that they say a wise enemy is better than a foolish friend. All the measures you have taken I have been watching from the start, and I do not agree with your opinion; but the matter was delicate, and I could not say anything.

Hadi: You go on beating that drum of measures of yours. No measure works against fate; what measure can poor I take? The one who had to die has died; everyone has sat down in patience and thankfulness. He was bound to die; if he had stayed here he would have died all the same; but then I would have had to have a hand in the medicines and treatment willy-nilly, and you would not have left me alive, and would have thrown this very reproach at me: "Your bungling killed him."

Azad: I still say that your bungling killed him. I have learnt now that he had palpitations of the heart, and used to have attacks in the hot weather, and sometimes, from eating heating food, his heart would begin to pound even in winter. Had I known, I would never have let him go. The hot weather, a railway journey, the hot wind of the Deccan, the blow of separation from home, and on top of all this the depression that must have arisen willy-nilly from the affairs of an Indian state: in such a state I would not have insured his life for a pice. But well, what is the use of talking of it? What was to happen has happened. I am speaking of the bungling you are doing in Azadi's case. From the start you have taken care that she should not grieve, should not weep, and for this purpose you cling to her all the time, and for this purpose you have had her listen to the *maulvis*' sermons; but understand at least this much, that grief and weeping are demands of nature and cannot be stopped by anyone's stopping them. Just as pain goes with a blow, so grief goes with a blow to the spirit; and just as, as time passes, the feeling of pain from a blow lessens of itself, exactly so, as time passes, grief too diminishes and lessens of itself. In calamity the best way of sympathy is to leave the afflicted person to themselves up to a certain time. The comfort that is gained in the end in this way, being in accordance with the law of nature, will be all the more beneficial. You wanted to comfort her before the time and thought your measure had worked, but that was your misunderstanding. The result was that, upset and weary, she gave up your treatment. Even now the wise course is not to interfere at all in her affairs, and to let her follow her own judgement. God has created man free, and every human being wants to remain free. If freedom[1] is harshly restrained, it bursts out in other, bad forms. It is like this: there is a seed in the ground, and it is known for certain that in its season that seed will sprout and split within the ground, a shoot will come from it, it will grow and spread, until it becomes a good-sized tree. Now if, before the shoot comes out, we put a great heavy stone on it and do not let it rise, the fibres will find another way; the branches of that tree may be crooked, they may be stunted, but it cannot be that they will not be. This is the reason why bad character is so often found in slaves and people of low degree: they have not full freedom. So if you want your daughter's well-being and safety, my opinion is that you should let her live by her own choice. You have no right now to set up your rule over her. She too is a woman like you; she understands her own gain and loss well and has the sense to distinguish between what is proper and improper. My great fear is that your unreasonable pressure may lead to some bad result. Her going from her in-laws to her own house, not asking your leave and not telling you: take it that she does not wish to live under your supervision. "*For the wise a hint is enough.*" What Khwaja Azad said was true and reasonable through and through. Hadi Begam had the palanquin sent back, and did not even send her daughter a complaint: "What is this you have done?"
