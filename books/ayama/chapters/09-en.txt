# Chapter 8: Maulvi Mustajab Takes Service and Goes to Bhopal; What the Service Was Like

In short, Mustajab took the post at the press; and since he had taken it not at his father's bidding but against his father's opinion, he carried it out as well as it deserved to be carried out, and six months had not passed before the Maulvi Sahib's pay became fifty. After some time things went so far that the Nawab Sahib would, as it were, set up a skeleton, and Maulvi Mustajab would breathe life into it with his annotation[1]. Many of the Nawab Sahib's books were to be seen in which Maulvi Mustajab's commendatory preface was the gist and the very substance of the book, and his small-print notes were interesting while the main text was so-so. In the third year of Maulvi Mustajab's service the Nawab Sahib resolved to write a book of great moment, and wished that none of the great questions, such as following a school of law, the imamate, raising the hands, saying *Amin* aloud, the Sufi way, the oath of discipleship, holy war, usury, imitating the Christians, eating with the Christians, "those in authority", and so on and so forth, should be left out of it, and that the book should be so comprehensive that while it existed the Muslims of the fourteenth century [TN: of the Hijra] would have no need of any other book. This was so great an undertaking that it was rather difficult for the Nawab Sahib to complete the book alone, and finding a better helper than Maulvi Mustajab was more difficult still. At last a telegram came summoning the Maulvi Sahib, and on the heels of the telegram a letter came running after it, setting out in detail that "the purpose of summoning you is this: a hundred rupees' pay, with board and lodging along with His Highness." Several people also wrote from Bhopal, and they must have written after seeing and understanding something, that "His Highness is so much inclined to advance you that it would be no wonder if, as soon as you arrive, he appointed you to the office of Chief Qazi; and that is the opinion of certain special people here too." All these inducements were on one side, and separation from his wife on the other. Maulvi Mustajab was just about to refuse when people reasoned with him: "There is a railway from here all the way to Bhopal, only a journey of a day and a night; it is a Muslim state; the husband of the ruling Begam is the absolute master of black and white; he sends for you so eagerly, and you hesitate to go? We cannot understand it.

"Such chances do not always come. If you are thinking of being alone, take your household with you, or send for them after you have seen how things are there. Who knows whom God means to benefit through you, that He has arranged all this by His power? Go, and go without hesitation, and go by all means, and go soon." If the whole world had cried "Go, go," Maulvi Mustajab would not have agreed to leave home; but he took his wife's advice, and for him, in truth, hers was the only advice that counted. Azadi said: "In my heart of hearts I am not willing; but men and women, our own people and strangers, all of them have been plaguing my life: 'Do send him off.' I don't know what such hurry people are in. And what such luck will you bring all Delhi the moment you go, that everyone who comes comes asking just this: 'Hasn't he gone yet? When, God willing, will he set out? God forbid, you haven't made him refuse? The plan to go is settled, isn't it? This week then, or on Thursday?' In such a state of things, when the whole world is saying 'Go, go' with one voice, how can I alone say against everyone, 'Don't go'? Never mind the others; even the Maulvi Sahib and Mother[2] themselves all suspect that it was I who made you give up preaching, and I who talked you into taking service. Thank God nothing unlucky happened in the service; otherwise people would have made my life a misery with their taunts. No, sir; whatever grief your going is to my heart, I cannot let any word come out of my mouth that would leave me no ground to swear by tomorrow. And I well understand that you will not be able to hold back either; such an opportunity has come. Go; God be your friend, I give you into God's keeping; may God be your protector and helper. I have read of the rights of husband and wife in books and heard them in sermons. All told, I have only one right over you, my *mahr*, and that is all; and I say it aloud, that I have gladly and willingly forgiven you my *mahr*. But you have many rights over me, such as no creature has over any other creature, and I know very well that I am certainly in debt for your rights. I must have spoken ill of you behind your back at my parents' house, and what woman does not? In some matter, perhaps unknowingly, something may have happened against your wishes, and I thought ill of you.

"Perhaps I made some unreasonable demand and you could not fulfil it, and I was displeased for nothing. You brought me something and I was not grateful. I fell short in serving you. At some time I did not obey you, did not show you respect. There are hundreds and thousands of such things. In short, I am your sinner to every hair of my head, and now with joined hands I say: for God's sake forgive me. There is no trusting life; who knows whether, parting now, we shall ever meet again in this world? I must not die after you have gone with your rights still on my neck. Have you forgiven me? Say with your mouth that you have forgiven me, and forgive me with your heart." When Azadi made her farewell speech so movingly, Maulvi Mustajab too could not control himself. He wept, and wept so much that he sobbed and sobbed. Azadi, for her part, was in such a state that however much she pressed her lips together, comforted her heart and turned her thoughts elsewhere (she got up and drank water too, and moved from one place to another), her feelings would not come under control in any way, and cry after cry broke from her in spite of herself. At last, after a long while, it was Maulvi Mustajab who recovered first, and began to say: "Why are you so distraught? I would take you with me, but then it is a strange city, and although I have had a great deal of correspondence with the Nawab Sahib, I have never had occasion to meet him; he has not seen me, and I have not had the honour of waiting on him. God Most High willing, as soon as I get there I shall arrange a house and so on and send for you. After all, before I took service I used to stay in the villages for a week, or two weeks at a time, for preaching; think that I have gone to preach this time too. And if, God forbid, there is no chance of sending for you, then on the eighth day you will see me standing right here. What service, and what employment? Even if they give me the state of Bhopal, I am not going to stay."

Azadi: That much I know, that once you are settled you will certainly send for me; and what would I do staying here anyway? The moment you set out I shall start gathering up my bits and pieces, pack the things and get ready to go. But God knows, just then some such thought came to me that my heart grew restless; and since your heart too filled up on seeing me weep, look, I won't weep now. You too set out happy and cheerful, and may God bring the day when, just as you are showing me your back as you go, you come back and show me your face.

She had said, for the saying of it, that she would not weep now; but as soon as Mustajab, setting off, said "*Astawdi'ukum Allah*"[3], gave his last greeting and recited the prayer for departure, "*O God, turn me towards good; O God, You are the companion on the journey and the guardian of property and family*"[4], Azadi was in the same state again, or worse. She followed him to the door, stood watching as far as the corner of the lane, and when she came back into the house she fell face down on the floor. She did not understand herself why her heart kept welling up so from within. And the way of love is seen to be something like this: it quietly makes a place for itself in the heart, and a person is not aware of their own infatuation, enchantment and devotion. Before this Azadi had not known that she had so strong a tie with Mustajab. Husband and wife had lived together contentedly and happily; in all that time there had never once been a quarrel or dispute over anything. She always sought to please him, he always kept her wishes in view. She praised him, he sang her praises, less to the face and more behind the back. She was overjoyed to see him, he was delighted to be with her. When they were parted she knew that they had not been merely husband and wife, but lovers devoted to each other like Laila and Majnun. When separation came it turned out that they had not been only man and wife, but like Shirin and Farhad, she ready to die for him and he to lay down his life for her. All of us worldly people are caught in just such a mistake, and trapped in the same kind of illusion. Living in the world we form all kinds of ties, and do not know that we have made them binding on ourselves. We attach every sort and kind of entanglement to ourselves, and do not understand that we have taken this needless burden on our heads. Maulvi Mustajab set out at nightfall. That night Azadi neither ate nor drank anything; she wept a little and slept a little. When she rose in the morning she began reckoning to herself: "Now there is only one more day to go. God keeping him safe, he will say the afternoon prayer in Bhopal, and as soon as he gets down he will send a one-rupee telegram; God willing, I shall have said the sunset prayer, or be saying it, when the receipt arrives. What a mistake I made not to keep a fast today; I shall have to wait for the evening anyway, and would have had to anyway."

God has spoken truly: "*And man is ever hasty*": man is very hasty indeed. From the morning Azadi began to long for the evening, and that day became a mountain to her that would not be got through in any way. Today all the routines of the world seemed to her to have changed. In her preoccupation she did not realise that it was she who was mistaken, and she blamed others for getting the times wrong. Once she flared up at the maids of the house: "You haven't the sense to see that since last night not a crumb has flown into my mouth, and you sit at your ease rattling off nonsense. Look how long it is since morning; does anyone care about the pot and the stove? There are many days still before Ramazan!" The maids were flustered at this, and one threw a *burqa* over her head and ran off to the meat and vegetable market, and came back at once: "From one end to the other not a single shop has opened." For the noon prayer she sent several times to ask at the mosque whether the muezzin had fallen asleep. Azadi had, to be sure, a special reason for her haste, in that she was waiting for her husband's telegram of arrival; it was right to wait for it and fair to be impatient for it. But no: man's nature is somehow made like this, that he wants every task that would happen in three days to happen the day after tomorrow, what would happen the day after tomorrow to happen tomorrow, what would happen tomorrow to happen today, and what would happen today to happen now, this very moment. The student is anxious for the day when he will finish his studies and the turban of graduation will be tied on his head. The Middle boy is in a hurry for the Entrance, the Entrance boy is impatient for the F.A. The young man says: "When shall I grow up, when shall I be married?" He has let the hair grow on his head, and several times a day he takes it between his fingers to see how much it has grown. The merchant is restless for his goods to sell, the moneylender impatient for his instalment of interest. In short, His Excellency Man is a strange creature: the things that cannot happen he wants to happen, and about the one thing that is certain to happen he is as unconcerned as if it will not happen, that is, death. Asadullah Khan Ghalib has put this very idea most finely:

I do not wait for death, for it will surely come;
I long for the one who, if she does not come, cannot be summoned.

Seeing Azadi's restlessness and distress, the thought kept coming to us that where there are no railways and no telegraph, the people must have no love or affection; and if they have love and affection, they must never have to travel; and if they have to travel, people like Azadi must not survive. It was lucky that just as the call to the sunset prayer was being given, the telegram of safe arrival came, so that Azadi said her prayer in peace. From the third day letters began to come one after another, at least one every day and sometimes two. Maulvi Mustajab wrote: "The Nawab Sahib has received me with such honour and regard that I had not imagined a tenth or a hundredth of it. He has lodged me in his own private quarters, and in conversation and in sitting and rising I am treated with courtesy as an equal. And since the Nawab Sahib is himself a very great scholar and a patron of learning, *maulvis* are gathered here in such numbers that merely being a *maulvi* is no distinction here. This story is true: some Agent, by bringing his pressure to bear, had a Parsi appointed as the head of some court. When the first order of his court came before the Nawab Sahib, his name bore the title Maulvi. On inquiry it turned out that the clerk who wrote the orders was an ignorant and incompetent sort of man; he saw that all the officers and heads of departments were written down as Maulvi, and even the highly paid staff, and he took it that Maulvi was an official title that went with the post. The poor fellow wrote down the Parsi head too as Maulvi. For a long time people laughed about it, and it was a laughing matter too; for the Parsis, by religion, are at root idol-worshippers, though with a Hindu cast, and in manner and way of life English. A wit said of them, quite rightly, that if you subtract the Englishness from a Parsi, what remains will be a Hindu. In any case a Parsi's outside resembles the English and his inside the Hindus; what has he to do with being a Maulvi, which is the religious title of the Muslims? But when I heard that the English government has always handed out the title of Khan Bahadur to Parsis, and that ever since the title of *Shams-ul-Ulama* (Sun of the Learned) was invented several Parsis have been made *Shams-ul-Ulama*, I said to myself that the clerk's Maulvi is no more ill-matched than the English *Shams-ul-Ulama*. Well, that was a digression. I am not a *maulvi*, but people call me Maulvi too, not only here but in your Delhi as well. It does not become me to say this, but what can I do? My real thought is that so great a crowd of *maulvis* is a cause for anxiety and a place of danger. The immediate result is that the colour of bigotry shows in the administration. God grant that the Nawab Sahib has sent for me for the purpose of helping him in writing and compiling; but at times he also says things from which it appears that it would be no wonder if he appointed me to some post in the courts.

"For the present no honourable post has been heard of as vacant; but if he means to advance me, creating a new office for me is much easier than writing a book. People tell many instances where first the pay was named, and then, when there was no room in the ordinary posts, some special work was devised. Here there is not much concern with work; whatever pay anyone is given goes on, work or no work. But I grow uneasy sitting idle; either I shall ask and get some work, or, after the custom here, keep to my pay. But until one or the other of these is settled, I do not think it wise to send for you; indeed, when I get the chance, I shall bring it to the Nawab Sahib's ear."

In this shilly-shallying six months passed: neither was the book begun, nor was any work in the courts given him. Twice a day enjoy delicacies at your ease along with His Highness, draw your pay on account, and lie about idle and stretch yourself. Mustajab grew weary of asking, Azadi grew tired of waiting, but the Nawab Sahib never tired of promising. Whenever he petitioned: "God willing, tomorrow"; whenever he reminded him: "If God wills, the day after." How could poor Mustajab know, how could poor Azadi be aware, that in Indian courts the morrow of the Day of Judgement is called tomorrow and years are called the day after? The joy that the rise in pay, the companionship and the hopes for the future had brought to Mustajab and Azadi and the other relations, and to the would-be hangers-on who willy-nilly forced themselves in, was turned by the daily putting off and the constant breaking of promises into displeasure. In place of hope came despair, and in place of gratitude complaint. The truth was that the Nawab Sahib was everything: very learned, very able, very experienced, a great statesman, a great administrator; and his powers too were so wide that he was, as it were, an independent ruler in his own right. But the state's affairs had been so disordered, not just lately but from the beginning, that the administration could not be put straight by any device. Hundreds of plots, thousands of intrigues, as many tales as there were mouths. God knows whether it was the effect of that company, or whether the Nawab Sahib's nature was made that way, that he would wish and could not do, resolve and hold back. Suspicion had so taken hold of his temper that he had doubts about everything and misgivings about every word: "*He doubts, and then he doubts that he doubts*."[5] Mustajab was much distressed to see things like this, but he was caught: "No foot to go, no face to stay." He did write to Azadi about how things were there, but in guarded words, for he knew very well that if he wrote it all out plainly, what would come of it except that she would look with contempt even on a vizier's post? No value was left in his heart for post or pay or favour; but the difficulty was that he could not leave either. "One comes by one's own wish, and goes by leave"; and if he were to ask leave, what could he say to ask it?
