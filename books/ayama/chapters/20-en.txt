# Chapter 19: Azadi Begam's Last Bequest and the End

Elders, brothers, dear ones! Peace be upon you. In India, and if not in India then in this city, this is perhaps the first occasion on which a woman who keeps purdah is addressing men, some of whom are strangers too. A woman, of whatever people, whatever religion, whatever country, whatever age and whatever condition she may be, always has a little modesty in her nature. From this it is clear that purdah, by her very creation, suits a woman's temperament. On the face of the earth the Muslims are the only people in whose religion there is a command of purdah; and people of other religions, especially the Christians, open their mouths very wide against it; but this is sheer obstinacy on their part. They are not well acquainted with our state, and on the strength of guesswork they object to a custom that up to now has certainly proved most beneficial. I know nothing at all of the domestic life of the English; but as far as I have learned from books and newspapers, they too have to suffer the bad results of going without purdah; yet the freedom and self-will which women have by the custom of the country, far from taking it away, even checking and lessening it is hard, nay, impossible. Among Muslims too the custom of purdah did not come in at once; rather for a long time everyone's daughters and daughters-in-law went out and walked about according to the old custom, until, through the teaching of Islam, people's breasts were filled with the fear of God and piety and good works, and they themselves came to dislike going without purdah, and, thinking freedom [illegible] and dangerous for themselves, they wished to bind themselves to purdah. When the command of purdah came down, for many days there was such ease in it that women joined the congregation in the mosques, went to the wars and performed whatever service they could for the fighters of the faith. As the blessed age of the Prophet, God bless him and give him peace, grew more distant, the purity of hearts and the cleanness of thoughts grew less, and by necessity strictness came into purdah, until the shape of purdah now is what you gentlemen all see. This purdah is far beyond the measure of the law, but still less than the need and the expediency of the time. Although I am a very strict supporter of purdah, a cause just as strong has come about, so that I have dared to let men hear my voice; and in all my life this is the first, and God willing the last, occasion on which I have gone against, not the purdah of the law, but the purdah of custom.

The daughter of the Messenger of God, God bless him, the lady Fatima, may God be pleased with her, more than once made a very long speech addressing the Emigrants and the Helpers, and that speech is still found written in books in the form of a sermon. People used to come in great numbers to ask questions of law of the Mother of the Faithful, the lady Aisha, may God be pleased with her, and she would answer from behind the curtain. In short, for this talk that I am giving I hold authorities for its lawfulness too. Besides this, I am myself amazed at where such strength has come to me at this time. After several years so much voice has come out; otherwise, look at my state, and weakness and wasting have gone beyond all bounds. No one denies, and there is no room to deny, that at no time and in no state is there any trusting in life; but not to deny and not to be able to deny is one thing, and to prove by deeds that we really hold in our hearts as firm a conviction as we say with our mouths is quite another thing, and between the two there is a great and very great difference. I do not speak of these last few days; but before this I saw and heard of my elders, of those on my right hand, of those younger than myself, some dying after being ill for long periods and some suddenly; not one or two but hundreds; nay, two or three times I myself fell so ill that there was nothing at all lacking for me to die; and yet before this I was never for one moment uneasy about my own life. It was as if my idea was that I was exempt from the general decree of death, or as if God had given me His word that I would not die short of a hundred and twenty-five years of age. No one but God knows the state of another's heart; but as far as can be understood from people's conduct, I think that all men, save whom God wills, are caught in just such heedlessness as that in which I wasted my whole life. If not now, then at some other time, go to your own place and think at leisure: if the people of the world trusted life only as much as by reason they ought, that is, went to sleep at night with this thought in their hearts, "Let us see whether we shall be granted waking or not," and rose in the morning with this idea before their eyes, "God knows whether we shall even reach the evening or not," then you will surely grant that the world could not keep its present shape.

Why would anyone have solid buildings put up, for what would anyone lay out a garden, to what end would anyone make any kind of arrangement for the future? Expectation would be a word without meaning, hope an empty fancy.

The breeze of heedlessness is blowing; the slumbers of disaster are surging;
The sleepers sleep so soundly they are sworn not to wake till the Day of Judgment.

Alas that I came to know my error and to be roused from heedlessness at such a time, when making good the loss is beyond my power and amends for the past are outside my ability. The length of the illness has changed my body, and the certainty of death my thoughts, so much that it is as if this were my second birth. The thought I wished to fix in my mind and that would not settle there is now the very thought that never leaves my heart. Things that all my life stayed doubtful and difficult now appear clear and self-evident as broad day, needing neither argument nor proof. I find one verse of the Quran fits this state of mine very closely: "*And every soul will come, with it a driver and a witness. You were in heedlessness of this; now We have removed from you your covering, and your sight today is sharp.*" If I had the thoughts I had before, or why should I not say the thoughts of my whole life, it would have been hard for me even to talk with you gentlemen, let alone such talk as needs [illegible]. But this thought, that I am not to remain in the world, has made my heart strong, my nature firm and my courage bold. It is only that people will call me forward and shameless; but one day there will be neither those who taunt (and this perhaps will be somewhat late) nor she whom they taunt (and this is going to be very soon). In a little while I shall say to you gentlemen things of such a kind that on hearing them you will be even more astonished than at this. Well, the verse I recited just now gives the state of the Day of Resurrection: that every living soul will be brought before the Lord of the worlds in such a fashion that, for one thing, there will be a driver with him, just as in the world a government constable stays with a criminal under arrest and drags him along into the presence of the magistrate; and there will be a witness, who will bear witness against him to complete the proof. In short, every person will be presented before God Most High, glorious is His state, in the guise and form of a criminal; then God Most High will say: "You were heedless of this state," that is, in your life you took no thought that I must go into God's presence for ever, and there will be constables there from whose grip I shall not be able to flee, and witnesses whom I shall not be able to give the lie.

So the veil of heedlessness that lay over your eyes, today We have lifted that veil; now your sight is so sharp that all the things about which you had doubt are now plainly visible to you with the eyes of your head. Very nearly this is my state. The religious ideas of my parents were so opposed to one another that there was always squabbling between the two of them; and this was the reason that the need arose for me to reflect on religious matters at so small an age, when the girls of my years had no thought of anything but dolls. In argument Father always got the better; but Mother was so firm in her belief that she would be silenced and yet stand fast by what she said. And sometimes it also happened that if some doubt came into her heart she went to her father and, by talking it over, cleared it up. For me it was hard: the age of childhood, the days of games and play, religious ideas, and among them disagreement too, and no ability to decide. The scales of an unskilled weigher: now this pan would dip and now that. When I looked to my mother, there was prayer, and fasting, and recitation of the Quran, and eagerness to go to sermons. When my father's words came to mind, then where was prayer and whose fast? For weeks on end even God's name did not come to my tongue. But I will certainly say this much, that my mother's influence on me prevailed. I said my prayers by fits and starts, and let many lapse too, but I never let a whole month pass empty of prayer. I broke fasts too, and kept them half-heartedly too; but it never happened that I let a whole Ramazan go by dry. But now that understanding has come (and when did it come? why, these fifteen or twenty days), I have learned that in all my life it never once happened that I performed any act of worship with that sincerity which is the condition of its acceptance. So such worship done and not done are the same. And where would sincerity have come from? Here even religion itself, from the start, was an imitation of my mother and her family, and that too [illegible] by my father's correction. The greater part of life passed in such a way that not once did the thought of God come from the heart; and when I did not hide it from God, what will come of hiding it from His servants? For days and days on end I stayed bewildered over this, whether God is really anything at all or not; and if He is, what does He want of us? He created us without being asked, and fastened all sorts of needs upon us. What more can we do than get through the days of life? On top of that the toil of worship, the bonds of religion, dying, and after dying the eternity that is ours: its meaning is that, having created us, He has cast us for no reason into a calamity.

What kind of justice is this, and what sort of mercy? In the world there are people of every kind, good and bad, religious and irreligious; in no one is any such distinction found by which God's pleasure and displeasure could be traced. How many religions there are, and the follower of each religion calls the other astray; but no one religion can give so much proof of any one thing as to show that in the world this preference belongs to it and not to another. Look at anyone, and it is a reference to the hereafter. This riddle has not been solved to this day, and there is no hope of its being solved till the Resurrection. In truth I used to be very troubled when the thought came that in this country we Muslims, compared with the followers of other religions, are like salt in flour; then has God created so many people only to fling them into Hell in the end? I know several Hindu women, and I am acquainted with two Memsahibs too; and reason too bears witness that among the followers of other religions too there is goodness, knowledge of God, fear of God and worship of God; and if they are not Muslims, it is not from groundless obstinacy but only for this reason, that they do not know the state of the religion of Islam, or that they believe with a true heart in the truth of the very religion they are in, and their hearts find comfort in that religion. How hard it is to pass the judgment on such people that there is no salvation for them! In short, about religion I had idle thoughts of some such kind, scattered and wavering. At times my mind would get tangled up too; but there was no such passion for religion in me; a little weight fell on my mind, I put the thought aside and busied myself with some other work. Now I have two kinds of regret: one, for living a life heedless and irreligious and worse than the beasts; and the other, for not regretting such an idle and useless life. Since I have become fully certain that I cannot survive this illness, my understanding has of itself become something of another kind. Now I see God and only God on every side, and I say just this: when the end of life is death, then a man, even if he were the king of the face of the earth, has no reality at all. It only waits for the breath to go, and then it is a heap of dust. But there is a great difference between certainty and certainty. I too always had the certainty of dying, and everyone has it; but that certainty was of one kind, and this certainty is of another kind. That was the copy, this is the original. That was the figure, this is the reality. It is written in the life of some saint that he had dug a grave in his house and made it binding on himself to go and lie in it for a little while every single day, so that the remembrance of death should stay fresh.

I have not dug a grave, but I am fully certain that from the bed on which I lie I shall now be lifted down only to go into the grave. In short, certainty of death to this degree has driven away all my doubts. It has now been well proved to me that I am a most helpless and lowly creature. No sooner had I come to know my own reality than God began to come into view. All my life passed in hearing "*He who knows himself knows his Lord*" and in reading it in books, but only now has its meaning come into my understanding: that by knowing oneself God is known in this way. Then, when I looked on God's greatness and His majesty, I was so utterly confounded that I began to be ashamed to call my existence existence. This thought brought me very great relief, because I used to be caught up in such cares in which to let my little reason meddle was folly and insolence of the last degree. On the one hand I came to know God, and on the other I looked at myself, and His favours to me are so many that they have no bound or end. So I thought that not to repay the favours of such a Benefactor is against the way of humanity; but His Being is beyond need, so much so that He does not care even for our thanks and gratitude. So be it; to treat one's fellow creatures well as far as one can is itself our thanks to Him, and this is the repayment of His kindnesses; because we see His dealings with His servants: the nurture of all, the upbringing of every one. So if we behave with kindness and consideration towards His servants, that will surely be a cause of His pleasure and satisfaction. But alas, a thousand times alas, when did these things come to my mind? When I have no respite to stay in the world; and if I do have the respite of a day or two days, or perhaps half a day, or perhaps an hour or two, there is no ease from the illness, no rest. But even so, as long as there is breath in the body, something or other must be done. At last one thing came into my understanding, for which I have put you gentlemen to trouble, and that is to consider the state of widowed women. You will surely grant that whatever I say about widowed women will be far more worthy of trust than what you are thinking in your own hearts; because, in the first place, I am a woman, and no man, however near and dear he may be, can know a woman's state as well as I can by being of the same sex. Secondly, I have tasted the bitterness of widowhood to the full and suffered its misfortune thoroughly.

Thirdly, when I am ready to die and sit with my feet hanging in the grave, there can be no share of any personal interest of mine in whatever I say. My time has now come when I have turned my eyes completely away from all worldly interests. The very body I brought with me from my mother's womb, and which kept me company all my life, my bond with it is about to break; then what other bonds can remain? And it was this very thought that gave me the courage to gather people together and tell them of the misfortune of widows; otherwise how many of God's handmaids, stifling and wasting away within themselves, have passed from behind the curtain of the world, and no one knew how sorely widowhood tormented them and how much it made them weep. I understand very well that what I am doing no woman has done to this day. There will be talk of this strange thing for a long time. People will have all sorts of evil suspicions, they will curse me and speak ill of me; but I am going to such a place that the voices of here cannot reach there. My dealings are going to be with God; and whether I am bad or good, whatever I am, He alone knows; and He is not a judge of the world's sort, who cannot form any opinion of his own without jury and assessors and witnesses. So what can I care what people say of me after I die? Most of you gentlemen know my circumstances, and I see no need to relate them. So, as I go, I will tell you gentlemen the story of my widowhood. I do not claim that my widowhood was a widowhood of any special kind. No doubt I was widowed in youth, but widowhood does not depend on any condition or any age: old women, young women, girls, women with children, women without, rich, poor, noble, low, nowhere is there any lack of widows. Nay, my state was far better compared with that of a great many widows. I had no burden of children, so that I should have had the care of rearing and bringing them up, of having them taught to read and write, of their marriages. The Nawab Sahib, God give him a good reward, had fixed a salary for me on which I could live at ease, and a hundred thousand thanks to God that I have lived at ease up to this moment. I often heard women say, and at the very beginning I thought the same, that God had relieved me of the care of a livelihood; if I were to intend a second marriage, it would be a most improper and ugly and unlawful thing. But not many days had been allowed to pass before my opinion changed entirely, and I understood that if the aim and end of the marriage bond were only this, that the man earns and the woman dresses and eats, then there is no delight at all in this bond, nor is this bond a thing of any great worth.

And if that were so, the rich would never even mention marrying their daughters. No; rather the real aim of this bond is that man and woman should enjoy each other's love; and all the other blessings that arise from keeping house, and the benefits that come to one from the other, are all branches of this same comforting and joy-giving and soothing love. There is no fixing a person's opinion either. I remember well that at the beginning of my widowhood, nay, until several years after it, my state was such that, never mind anyone having the nerve to say it to my face, if anyone had so much as mentioned a second marriage even by a hint or an allusion, I would surely have seized him and scratched his face. And if ever the thought came to me even of itself, I took it for a whisper of Satan and put it off as soon as it came. Or here am I, the same woman, who now, even in such a state, knowing that it is utterly useless and fruitless and cannot even happen, still have this as my heart's desire: to die as somebody's wedded wife, and to be raised at the Resurrection among the wedded wives. By one reckoning, all through my widowhood I kept up a sort of refusal of marriage; and if there had been no refusal I would have gone and done it; and once I had set my mind to doing it, when would I have been stopped by anyone's stopping? But the heartfelt refusal, which ought to be called true refusal, in fact lasted until the *iddat*: until then the memory of the late Maulvi Sahib was still fresh, and I thought it faithlessness and heartlessness of the last degree to forget so soon his companionship of so many days. In the time fixed by the law until the *iddat*, whatever other purposes there may be, this is my own experience, that after that much time has passed such a great change comes over grief that, unless the sorrow is kept fresh by effort, it is as if this were the natural lifespan of sorrow. Why should the thought not have come even within the *iddat*? But after the *iddat* was completed I pressed my mind harder, that one way or the other some settlement of this matter must certainly be made. I left no side of thinking and reflecting untouched: what religion said, what reason said; whether my particular state wholly and entirely called for marriage, and whether custom stood in the way. And custom too is not so fierce as it is among the Hindus, but only so far that a widow who remarries does not keep the same kind and degree of honour, and people begin to look down on her. And, well, who is there in the world whom someone in a better state does not look down on? But by remarrying a widow is looked down on in that particular matter which wounds the heart most of all, and for whose protection she married: that is, her honour.

I always used to look on custom and usage with great contempt, and say in my heart that there are two touchstones for knowing good and bad: religion and reason. Nay, if you ask the truth, there is only one touchstone, that is, religion, because man's reason is not to be trusted at all. One man thinks a thing good and another thinks the same thing bad, and it is from this that there are disagreements in the world. We think that there can be no deed in the world more cruel and savage than killing innocent children; but those people among whom it is the custom to kill daughters, like the Rajputs of our country, have this saying, that to keep an unmarried girl at home, or to be called father-in-law and become brother-in-law, is a most shameful thing; no deed can be more worthy of praise than this, that life may go but honour must not. I have given this as one example; in the same way there are hundreds and thousands of things in which people's opinions differ. Nay, leave aside the disagreement of man with man: one and the same man thinks a thing bad at one time, and then the same man begins to call the same thing good at another time; and this has happened to me myself. And consider, and you will see that no human being is free of this wavering. The things of childhood seem bad in youth, and those of youth in old age. In short, good is only what religion calls good, and bad only what religion pronounces bad. So I used to say in my heart: when man's reason has not the skill to tell good from bad, what is this custom and usage? And they are great fools who follow it. But when I myself had to contend with custom and usage, I learned that in all God's creation there is nothing more mighty. Whenever the thought of marriage came, then the intention formed; and whenever the intention formed, custom and usage overturned all my plans. I would say: "Oh dear, oh dear! People will call me the woman of two husbands; ladies of my own standing will look on me with contempt, nudge one another, smile; all the respectable wives of old will eat of the Lady's dish of offering and I shall sit and stare at their faces. Because of me my family will be pointed at. No, no, I cannot bear this dishonour. Let fire take a wifehood that brings a stain on my good name, makes me hear people's taunts and suffer their abuse." Then sometimes the thought would come: what sort of sense is it to let one's own house burn so that others may warm themselves? Those who know God and the Prophet and are mindful of faith and religion may think in their hearts whatever they please, but with their mouths they cannot utter any base word to one's face or behind one's back; nay, even to think it in their hearts is harm to their faith.

But how many such people are there? Hardly one in a hundred; then what account is he? And then the dealing is going to be with women, who are generally without a share in religion, without a portion of faith. These poor things, in the rare case that they are on good terms with their husbands, it is through their own dressing and adorning; and if they are on bad terms, as mostly happens, then with quarrels and strife night and day, the management of the household, the rearing of the children, backbiting this one, speaking ill of that one, your jealousy, my envy, when do they get leisure to turn towards religion? They are such ladies that if the lady Mary herself came before them, they would wink at her too at least once. But if I marry, why [illegible] with the ladies at all? If there were one or two, they could be left alone; here, by God's grace, the whole kiln's batch is spoiled, the whole tribe is crooked. Willy-nilly, some time or other, somewhere or other, there will be an encounter with them; and when there is an encounter, these wretches will surely tease too; and when they tease, the heart will certainly be hurt. At such a time, if I had had someone to lend support and to keep up my courage, my second marriage too would by now have been fifteen or twenty years old. But my kin, my relations, my dear ones, though in every other way they showed sympathy and care, not one of them even falsely mentioned this. They too were constrained by the same custom by which I was helpless. And besides custom they also had this thought: "It is not her wish to make a second marriage; and after all she is one of us; certainly it will not be; then even to speak of it before her would be like rubbing chillies on a wound." My mother went so far as, out of thought for my widowhood, to renounce worldly life while her husband was alive, and in a way she ruined her own home. My grandfather preached such sermons on the virtues of patience that, never mind a husband, if I had died myself I would not have mourned even for myself. In short I am grateful to them all; none of them fell short with me. No doubt they ought to have moved the question of a second marriage and encouraged it; but they did not, because I ought to have shown my intention of a second marriage, and I did not. I tried ever so many means of busying myself in some occupation so that this thought should not come into my heart at all. For one whole year I tried keeping the fast of David, because I had read in a book that fasting, and fasting like that of the Prophet David, peace be upon him, on alternate days, is most useful for mortifying the self.

For months I did not wash my head, for weeks I did not comb my hair, I did not change my clothes; I swore off good food and vowed off good clothes; but I learned that nothing was at fault; my very being called for marriage. One day, as was my habit, I was sunk in this thought, and as I lay there it occurred to me: am I the only one so restless in myself, or is this the state of other widows too? You people will be amazed that this is a woman of a strange whim indeed; but for this very purpose I made the acquaintance of fifty-two, that is, two over fifty, widows, on which several years of mine were spent. Women; and among women, widows; and among widows, unripe ones. On top of that, in finding out their real and heartfelt thoughts such difficulties came my way that any other light-headed woman would not have taken so much headache. Whoever's state I wished to find out, first I drew closer to her, mixed with her and made her my friend; but the thing I was searching out was a matter so private that playmate would be ashamed to tell playmate, friend would hide it from friend. Many of women's affairs are things to be hidden; and those who were deep in their bellies hid a great deal too; but I too, once I was after it, did ask it out in one way or another. Yes, marriage was such a matter that no sooner did its name come to the tongue than the listener flew off the handle. At last, beaten, I used this trick: I took upon myself all the false slanders of faults that I was not to commit and had not committed; only then did those women open up and give the secrets of their hearts. To search out people's circumstances in this way is a bad thing and is forbidden in the law, and in the Quran there is the prohibition "*Do not spy*"; but I say it calling God to witness, there was never this in my intention, to tear away people's veils or to look down on them. What faults shall I search out in anyone, when every hair of me is a sinner against God and God's servants? If others have sinned, they still have respite for repentance; it may be that God grants them the grace of repentance, the repentance is accepted and all their sins are forgiven. The misfortune is mine, wretched and ill-fated as I am, that even the time for repentance is not left: "*Repentance with God is only for those who do evil in ignorance and then repent soon after; it is they to whom God turns; and God is All-Knowing, All-Wise. And repentance is not for those who go on doing evil deeds until, when death comes to one of them, he says, 'Now I repent'; nor for those who die while they are unbelievers. For them We have prepared a painful punishment.*"

Repentance is for those who did some bad deed unknowingly and then quickly repented; the repentance of such God too accepts, and God is the Knower, the Wise; and the repentance of those is no warrant who went on doing bad deeds until, when death came and was present, they began to say, "Now I have repented"; nor is the repentance of those a warrant who died unbelievers; for such We have prepared a painful punishment. So, gentlemen, I am in "*when death comes to one of them*," and I have no confidence even that my repentance will be accepted. Nay, my purpose in searching out people's circumstances was only this much, to see whether it is only to me that widowhood is so galling, or whether this is the state of others too. And the state of some is not fit to be told; with them it is the same as the saying: "*Choosing shame over the Fire*": constrained by the shame of the world, they consent to go to Hell. But it is God's great favour that, I do not speak of the rich, but among the women of the middling and the poor such corruptions are very few indeed, nay, as good as non-existent; and these are all the blessings of purdah. And it is a pity that the English-educated of these days are bent on this, that it should be broken by any means possible; but remember this one word of mine too at this time: that as much as purdah lessens, so much will corruption certainly increase. Unless God Most High brings angels to settle on the earth in place of men; then nothing can be said. I met several women of this kind who in reality refused marriage; but when I looked into it, their refusal was of the same sort as that of the fox who passed beneath the vines, saw the bunches of grapes, and her mouth watered; she leapt, she jumped a great deal, but the grapes were high and she could not reach them; at last, saying "The grapes are sour," she tucked her tail and went off. So among those who refused, some had no good looks, some were past their age, some burdened with children, so that a man would first have to build a coop for four or five of them before he married her. Among so many women I saw a true refusal in one, for whom nothing stood in the way. But a person of that sort, a godly man or woman, has never to this day come before my eyes; and if she had not taken a promise from me, I would surely have told everyone her name and whereabouts, because I count a visit to such saintly people as part of worship. I think it my great good fortune that by chance I happened to come to her; and I tell the truth, that if I have any hope of my salvation, it is only from this, that I made a request to her and she promised to pray for me.

She will surely fulfil her promise, nay, what else could be; and when she fulfils her promise, God too will surely fulfil His promise, nay, what else could be. He does not reject the word of His good servants. You too, for God's sake, pray that for the sake of His good servants God may pass over my sins; and I too pray the same for all of you; may God accept it. What a delightful subtlety it is that with the very mouth with which we tell lies, backbite, swear oaths, curse, speak ill, babble nonsense, with that same foul and unclean mouth we ask in prayer; then what earthly chance is there of such a prayer being accepted! But if one servant prays for another servant, that prayer is nearer to acceptance, because if my mouth is sinful, it is so for me, not for others. That lady I spoke of, when I went and saw her, God simply came to mind. I did not know that in these times and in our own city such saintly people lie hidden; and in truth, we know our own state; it is these people by whose blessing earth and heaven stand. The greatest thing of all is that she has hidden herself in such a way that no one has the faintest whisper of her state. She is the daughter of a good, well-off family, and was married into the house of some great noble outside the city. While her husband was alive there was nothing religious about her; her widowhood and mine are somewhat alike. She too was widowed in youth, in the third year of her marriage. Her husband was riding along on horseback; God knows what happened; the horse shied, he fell, and with the fall his life went out. She too had no children. The effect of her husband's death on her was that she gave up the world. If this is the outcome of widowhood, I say: O God, make the women of the whole world widows! When she came to the city, she did not stay at her mother's house, for fear of noise and bustle. There is an aunt of some distant relation, and she lives in that lady's house in a separate upper room. She does not care to meet anyone; not that she despises people and shuns them, no; but the tasks she has made binding on herself truly leave her no leisure. I too unknowingly broke in on her hours. I had seen a great deal of humbug of this kind, and I went and boldly planted myself there. Pretence has quite another look; when I saw her, my heart was simply overcome. By the time the day was three or four *gharis* (a *ghari* is some twenty-four minutes) advanced, she had finished her morning devotions and stood up to unwind silk; she goes on unwinding the silk and at the same time goes on reciting the Quran by heart.

She has worked out such a measure that just as the recitation of the section from *Alif Lam* to *Fil*[1] is finished, so too her day's wage of five pice is earned. And this is her means of livelihood. Then when she sits down with her sewing, it is not sewing for hire: travellers, students, or those who have not the means to pay for sewing leave cloth with her, and she sews it for nothing. This I have seen with my own eyes, that she puts this service of the poor before her voluntary prayers. The whole day's fast, and this toil, and then at night, look when you will, she is there on the prayer mat. She had my bed spread beside her prayer platform; in between her voluntary prayers, for as long as she recited her litanies, she kept fanning me. And I, by a trick, pretended to be asleep and unaware, thinking: good; for a little while let me at least taste the breeze of Paradise in this world. I have seen a great many prayers, but a prayer of such relish and longing, so regular, so reverent, so dignified, never came before my eyes. When I saw her state I had not the courage to ask, but at last I steeled my heart and did ask: "Why do you not now make a second marriage?" She said: "I could make one; but who would, for no reason, take on her neck the burden of a husband's rights? It has come in the noble hadith that after God comes the rank of the husband; and if the prostration of one servant to another had been allowed, the wife would have been commanded to prostrate herself to her husband. But our people's ways have become so corrupted that it is hard to keep a husband's rights. Then it has also come in the hadith that women will go to Hell in great numbers, because they do not acknowledge their husbands' kindness. So, my dear, my heart is afraid of making a second marriage; and now much has passed, and only a little is left; that too God will see me through, if He will." So that lady's refusal was a true and proper refusal; the rest, whoever I saw, said no, no with their mouths and yes in their hearts. And if widowed women think so, what is there to blame them for? It is the husbands of these poor women who have died, not the need for which marriages take place all over the world and for which their own first marriages took place. And if anyone dislikes it, he should go and quarrel with God for making women so. Of man God has said: "*Man was created weak*," man has been made weak. And weakness is of two kinds: bodily and of the reason. Man's bodily weakness is plain from this, that leave aside the lion and the leopard and the wolf and the elephant and the snake and the scorpion and so on and so forth, a single pimple and a single mosquito, if they set about it, can plague him to death.

As for weakness of reason: while his reason is overpowered by doubts and fancies and company and upbringing, by many things, he is ruled by the opinion of others to such a degree that if he were called the slave of others' opinion it would not be wrong. Every person wants people to think him good, and for that he is ready at every moment to labour at the hardest labour and to bear the very greatest hardship. When the English forbade the widows among the Hindus who used to become *satis* (widows burned on the husband's pyre), they made a great inquiry, and it was proved that in people's minds the *sati* has such grandeur that they think of her as of the rank of a goddess. Some foolish women, led on by the Brahmins, blurt out with their mouths, "I will become *sati*"; from that very moment they begin to be honoured; then they are held to their word, so that right up to the moment of sitting on the heap of wood no fear or agitation of any kind is to be shown. My purpose in giving this example is that being held to one's word is so bad a thing that thousands of women have lost their lives for its sake; then if, held to their word in this way, they declare a refusal of marriage at the start, and then, to keep up that refusal, stay all their lives in the misfortune of widowhood, what is there to wonder at in that? The question is whether such a refusal can be trusted at all or not. I say it cannot, and ought not to be. Whether you trust it or not, this Quran is in my hand; if you like, have some boy or woman look behind the curtain; and knowing God to be present and watching, I say that not I but my God has kept my honour safe to a hair. But I am not proud of it, I am not pleased with it; and how can I be proud and how pleased, when I find in God's Word this terrible verse: "*Whether you show what is in yourselves or hide it, God will call you to account for it; then He will forgive whom He wills and punish whom He wills; and God has power over all things*": and whether you show the thing in your heart or hide it, God will take account of it from you; then whom He wills He will forgive and whom He wills He will punish; and God has power over everything. Over my body I had power, and I guarded it. My eye was not allowed to fall on a man outside the forbidden degrees, I did not let my tongue speak a sinful word, my foot did not walk the wrong way, my hand did not move amiss; but over my heart I had no control. How was I to stop the whisperings, how was I to put off the thoughts? So my body is entirely sinless; but the heart, I neither think it sinless nor call it sinless. The body is a thing of the world; it is with me only up to here, and here I shall leave it behind.

The one of whom an account is to be asked in God's court is the heart; and alas, it is not fit to be presented before God. What good can it do me to show myself sinless in people's eyes, when I know very well that in God's court no one has any say, no one can take anyone to Paradise and no one can save anyone from Hell? Nay, to assure people of my sinlessness would be to tell a lie and to deceive, and I do not wish, as I go, to take one more sin on my head. There was a time I went through, not days, not weeks, not months, but years, when a man's voice sounded pleasant to my ears. When the watchman called out at night, or the hawkers cried their wares by day, I would listen with my ear pressed close; nay, once, losing control, I went and stood in the entrance passage, and then for months I kept reproaching myself. In my illness people have done me such services as only my own heart knows, and I cannot repay their kindnesses in any way; and yet I never found such comfort as came from the late Maulvi Sahib, God forgive him, asking in passing, "How are you feeling now?" I have come across a great many women too who were never married at all. I never felt pity for their state; but having become a widow I learned the worth of widowhood: that there is no torment in the world greater than this. Whoever believes what I say, may God give him a good reward; and whoever does not, what more can I say of him than this: God grant that he too become a woman like us, God grant that he too be married, and God grant that he too live on in the world as a widow. When I became certain that thousands and hundreds of thousands of women are caught in the harsh misfortune of widowhood, I thought that if I could help them a little there is no deed of merit greater than that. There is a reward for freeing a slave girl and having slaves set free; then why should there not be one for delivering widows? They too are servants of God; they too suffer the pains of prisoners and the hurts of slaves, nay, more. They too feel grief and comfort. First it came into my heart to gather women together and make men of them; but I saw that women are utterly helpless. The men have stuck out their leg in such a way that they do not let them stir at all. In truth the doings are the men's, and the women are blamed for nothing. I was in this perplexity when one day, while I was reading the Quran, this came to mind: that all the commands that are special to women, God Most High has sent down addressing women themselves, as "*And divorced women shall wait by themselves*"; "*And mothers shall suckle their children*"; "*they shall lower their gaze and guard their private parts and not display their adornment except to their husbands*," and so on; "*and that they should refrain is better for them*."

And in the Quran there are many more examples of this kind; but widowed women have not been given the command of a second marriage in this way; rather He has said, addressing the men: "*And marry off the widows among you*." There is surely some reason or other for this, and it is none other than that very power and authority of the men which, against the command of God and the Prophet, they have taken over women by force and highhandedness. Now if men say that a widow ought to arrange her own marriage herself, that it is her business, that she will make the offer and acceptance and not we, she will become the wife and not we, then this will mean that God made them the ones addressed in "*marry off the widows*" for nothing, God forbid; either He does not know the real state of things, or He did the men an injustice in wanting a work done by them that was not theirs to do. If God is anything at all and the Quran is His Word, then "*marry off the widows*" is a command of very hard trial for men. In the first place, having power, one cannot refuse God; and suppose that in some rare case there is lack of power, then in such a case, would it be God's purpose in commanding the men that you should force your widows to marry? But rather than my explaining to the men in what way "*marry off the widows*" must be carried out, they ought themselves to think and understand that "*marry off the widows*" is surely not a meaningless command; and if it is not meaningless, what does it mean? And whatever meaning is understood, what is the way to be discharged of this duty? My work was only this much, to lay the real state of widows before the men with truth and good intention, and to make the men aware of their duty; perhaps by doing so the misfortune of widows will be lightened and the men's answerability before God lessened. This I have done, and done purely for God. O God, if what I have said is in keeping with Thy holy will, grant my words effect and grant people the grace to act on them. O Lord, may the widows of the whole world have ease; and, not as the reward of this worthless effort of mine but only of Thy grace and kindness, for their sake grant me deliverance, with a good end, from this pain of the world which I can no longer bear. They say, and it is borne out by hadith too, that in every day and night there is some hour or other in which whatever a person utters comes to pass. When Azadi made this prayer it must surely have been just such an hour of acceptance: for on the one side all the men there had resolved in their hearts that none of them would suffer a widow to stay unmarried (and so it happened), and on the other, the prayer had only to end when Azadi suddenly gave a single hiccup and was gone.

The End
