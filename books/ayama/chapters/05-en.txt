# Chapter 4: Talk of Azadi's Marriage, and Her Own Thoughts about It

Meanwhile proposals of marriage for Azadi began to arrive. Whenever a note or a spoken message came from somewhere, the others would discuss it and argue it back and forth freely, but Azadi would sit so silent and apart that it seemed she had nothing to do with it. Leave aside asking her or taking her advice: if Azadi had listened attentively to people's talk, even that much listening would have been counted shameless. The ladies began to make signs to one another out of the corners of their eyes, so her mother had her nurse explain to the daughter: "When there is talk of your marriage and wedding, you must slip away, or if there is no chance to slip away, lower your eyes and bow your head. The women are watching for it; let no one go and carry tales to your in-laws-to-be. And mind, be very careful about this among your friends too, or some mischievous girls will dig and dig with their own questions and then go round the neighbourhood themselves beating the drum about it." Azadi did as her mother said and her nurse explained, but in her heart she kept thinking: "There is one woman, Mary, who found her own husband and made her own marriage; and here am I, of the same kind, forbidden not only to speak but even to listen. Will these people really hand me over to a stranger whose very face I do not know, to say nothing of his habits and temper? Such an outrage? Such darkness? If I dislike him, or he and I do not agree, I am dead while still alive. It is true that these people have more sense than I; they have seen much of the ups and downs of the world, and they wish me well with all their hearts (can anyone have more tenderness than for her own child?), and they will certainly not fall short in any way in finding the best possible house for me. But be that as it may, it is I who will have to pay. My parents, having married me off, will be free of their duty; the guests will eat and drink, sing and play, and go their way; nobody burns in another's fire. It will be I alone, and this mountain of a life. If my fate is good and I am matched with someone good, I am across; otherwise every moment will be hard. I know one plain fact: Mother is very fond of sweets, and sweets are always lying about the house, but I never even taste them, not even by mistake.

"So just as in food and drink my taste is one thing and hers another, who knows whether in this matter too her opinion may be one thing and mine another? Those two, husband and wife, are forever arguing over every little thing, and I listen. I cannot speak between them, but as far as I have considered, Father's opinion is usually right and reasonable, and Mother makes a point of honour of her word out of mere stubbornness. Though it is not my place to make peace between them, this much I understand: their ideas are so different from each other that if one says night the other says day. And that is why Father takes part in my affairs only half-heartedly, and that too when Mother starts forcing it on him. One can see plainly from his face that he does not like these things and agrees with her because he must. Very well, then, suppose Mother finds a son-in-law to her own way of thinking and her own liking; and what is there to suppose? That is just how it seems to be going, and just how it will go. Poor Father has never had his way and never will. Then this same squabbling will go on there, with me. These two quarrel in the morning and make it up by evening, but my temper is of another kind. I cannot bear anyone crossing me at all. When anyone contradicts me in the smallest thing, I want in spite of myself to bite him. With such a man I could hardly get through a single day. Far better to stay unmarried a thousand times than a joyless marriage. Well, mere thinking does no good. It must not happen that I go on thinking and thinking where I am while these people go ahead and do it. What shall I do? Whom shall I tell? None of my friends is fit for it; they are leaky and shallow. To tell them would be to disgrace myself in the whole city. My nurse is a mere woman, a good soul [TN: reading uncertain], but she has not even the skill to talk sensibly. And then, if I do tell anyone, what will they think in their hearts? I shall fall in their eyes for ever. Among so many people I can think of only one person to whom I could say something about this, who would not look on me with contempt, into whose mind no suspicion of me would come, and who would support me as well: my father.

"But making up one's mind is easy; to say it face to face needs courage. He loves me very much, and I am rather bold with him, and because of his love nothing I say offends him; but after all he is a man; how could I bring myself to speak out to him in my own affair? No, no, however I steel my heart, I shall not be able to say a single word before him. Why not write it and give it to him? In writing I can put my meaning well, and no one at all will know what I wrote and what he read. But the trouble is that if it were in his power he would do it himself without my telling him, and since he can do nothing, telling him would only grieve him for no purpose. Rather than go about looking for this person's or that person's support, why should I not speak to Mother myself? She is the one who will do it; others (even if it is Father) will at the most drop a word in her ear as advice. And what a great advantage it is that I do not stand in awe of her as I do of Father. If I am bold with Father, I am free and easy with Mother. She is a woman, and so am I. There is no reason to be so shy of her. All the other plans are useless; the only right thing is for me to be my own envoy and go and talk to Mother myself."

After this Azadi was free for a little while from the weaving and unweaving of her thoughts, and she was looking for a chance, when she found her mother free from the work of the house, to tell her tale. Then it occurred to her again: "I have thought out a great many objections, but if she asks me what, after all, ought to be done, what answer shall I give? Mary's case is Mary's. Among those people there is no custom of purdah, and there is no shame in meeting men and talking with them. We are people who keep purdah; how can we compete with them? With purdah kept, all that is possible is that the elders make enquiries, and they are doing so. At the most I could somehow steal a glimpse of him from a distance, and that only perhaps. And Father is right when he tells my brothers that looks are a passing shadow, or moonlight for four days; a man's great skill is his ability; it is with that one has to deal, and that is what counts. So if I were to make myself a byword for so little, could anyone be more foolish than I? As for the other things, habits, temper and so on, they become known, if at all, only when one has happened to live together for years. Shaikh Sa'di put it very rightly:

"In one day you may learn from a man's ways
How far he has risen in the ranks of learning;

"But do not feel safe from his inner self, and do not be deceived,
For a soul's wickedness is not found out in years.

"And then, what is a man's temper worth? I have seen with my own eyes how people change colour like a chameleon. Better, then, to say nothing to anyone and hear nothing, to sit silent, content with my fate: whatever happens, God does it. After all, apart from Englishwomen there are hundreds of thousands and millions of women in the world, and they have been married in just this way. Here and there, now and then, there is some disagreement, and that happens among the English too; Mary herself told of several such cases. If there were discord everywhere, how would the world's work go on? And then, when one has to get along with one person, what is so hard about shaping one's own temper to his liking? And why should one who cannot change her own temper blame another?" Thinking things over in this way by herself, Azadi stopped short on her way to her mother. And it was just as well that she did not reach her, for her mother had such a dislike of this sort of talk that she would have given her a scolding she would long remember. A woman so firm in her own opinion that she had spent her whole life with her husband trying to make her see sense, and still stood on the same one leg of her cockerel, what weight would she have given to the word of this slip of a girl? Hardly a day passed without her quarrelling with her husband, and the quarrel was over one thing only: Khwaja Azad would praise the English from every side, their wisdom, their good management, their customs, their manners, in short everything about them, and even, not openly, but covertly, by hint and allusion, their religion. And Hadi Begam was the daughter of a very famous family of *maulvis*. Women's religious beliefs are very firm in any case, because of their simplicity and tender hearts, and in this Hadi Begam went somewhat further even than ordinary women. Azadi's provocations had made her more bigoted still. Whatever she heard here she would go to her parents' house and learn four crushing answers to each point.

To spite her husband she carded the cotton of English society so thoroughly that you would have thought she knew *The Mysteries of London*[1] by heart. She had never liked Mary's coming to the house at all, but her daughter had put her foot down so firmly that she could do nothing. And of the things Mary said that turned Azadi's head, not even Hadi Begam's angels knew anything. The girls would sit together, heads close, and hold their consultations; what business had an old woman like Hadi Begam to break in on their councils? So if Azadi had gone to her mother and quoted Mary as her authority, her mother would have so exposed the English that Azadi would perhaps never have cared to look at Mary's face again for the rest of her life. But God meant to keep Mary's honour veiled: Azadi never made up her mind to go to her mother.

Although Azadi had decided at that time that to say anything to anyone about her betrothal and marriage was useless and pointless, and that the matter was of such a kind that, willy-nilly, it must be left to fate, still, if it were a trivial thing, one could put the thought of it out of one's heart. She wanted to turn her mind to other things, but again and again, of its own accord, the thought would come to her: "Let us see to what kind of man it is written in my fate that I shall be tied. Oh dear, rather than this anxiety it would be better if they did not marry me at all. Why are they so set on my marriage? Have I demanded it, or asked for it?" As soon as this came into her heart, she looked round among the people she knew and could not see a single woman who had stayed unmarried. Then she began to think about the reason: "Perhaps the desire for marriage comes when one grows up; but God knows, the thought of it has not entered my heart till now, and the very name of marriage frightens me. And those who are married, what great happiness do they have? Silly girls may perhaps be happy for a few days at becoming a bride, wearing fine clothes, jewellery, flowers and bangles, putting on henna and rubbing on perfume; after that, everyone I have seen I have seen crying over her troubles. The husband's ill temper, the taunts of mother-in-law and sisters-in-law, bearing children, rearing them, the drudgery of the house and the tangles of housekeeping: it is one long misery. I don't know why women buy this trouble. It must be that this wretched belly makes them do it all. Women are useless and unskilled; they cannot earn anything; if they did not lean on men, where would they get their food and clothes? Parents cannot carry the burden for long; daughters became fit for marriage, and the parents fastened them on somebody's head. Well, but I am not so unskilled. If I work, though it strains the eyes, can I not earn even four or five annas a day by sewing? Well and comfortably. But then, wage-work is wage-work by name; one will get by somehow, scrimping and pinching, but how great the disgrace! Khwaja Azad's daughter, Maulvi Muqtada's granddaughter, living by sewing? Oh dear, God forbid that I should bring a stain on the name of my elders. What a silly idea this is! By God's grace and kindness both my father's family and my mother's family are well off; can they not do even this much for me alone, that I may sit at home with the honour that befits their name? They will, they surely will; but nobody's parents have lived for ever. And what would my brothers and their wives care about me? My brothers earn nothing as yet; I do not speak to anyone, I know no one; and even so my sisters-in-law and I do not get on; they will not let me stay a single moment. Well, why don't I at least mention it to Mother and sound her out? There is nothing so disrespectful in saying I do not want to marry. Till now I have only been making things up in my own heart: she will say this, she will say that. Who knows what she will say? Perhaps something will come up in the talk that will clear my worry and not displease Mother either. It is true that unmarried girls do not utter a word, good or bad, in talk of their own weddings, or even of other people's; but I do not count such shyness as shame. When a man is ashamed of stealing, it means that stealing is bad, and that he too thinks stealing bad and hides it; and if his shame is true, one day or other he will certainly give up stealing. What kind of shame is this, when the marriage is drummed about the whole world, there is an uproar of congratulations in both families, the girl is happy to be a bride and the boy pleased to be called the bridegroom? A man has two eyes; is one to be shy while the other gives orders? If Mother is obstinate with Father, it is because her pride is caught up in the argument; otherwise Mother is a very reasonable person.

"Why should she be angry with me when I have done nothing wrong? At the most she will gently make me understand."

So Azadi once again made ready to go to her mother. Seeing her at her prayers, she sat down and began to consider in her heart how to begin. "Am I just to say: 'You are talking of my betrothal; I do not want to be married'? That is not a way of talking; that is like throwing a stone. And if, when she hears it, she scolds me and says, 'Get away with you! The singing-woman you made free with sings any nonsense [TN: a proverb for a familiar person taking liberties]. Who asked you to meddle? Go and sit in your place,' then it will come to nothing; I shall be in disgrace and my purpose lost as well. If only it would happen that someone brought the matter up and I sat listening and spoke up when I got the chance! But it has not happened and it will not. Mother has already sent word by the nurse forbidding it: when there is talk of this kind, I must slip away. So this much is settled: either I do not speak of it at all, or, if I do, I speak myself and in private. Very well, let me think a little more; if I do speak, I must speak in such a way that my words are not wasted."

From the time such ideas arose in Azadi's heart, there was no end to them. Weary and anxious, Azadi tried hard to turn her mind elsewhere, but these thoughts had put all her senses out of action, and like someone possessed, who keeps repeating one thing, walking and moving, rising and sitting, she was taken up with that alone. She would make a plan herself and spoil it herself, form an opinion herself and change it herself. Her final thought was: "To refuse marriage is not right. To think that people marry their daughters for food and clothing is plainly wrong; otherwise all who can afford it would keep their daughters at home. A reasonable provision for my living could easily be made too; but the great difficulty is honour, before which well-born people count neither life nor wealth as anything, and honour can be protected by no means but marriage. So it would be quite unfitting for me to utter such an unworthy thing before my elders. Why should such an idea have come into my heart at all? It only proves my foolishness and inexperience." In short, many urges rose in Azadi's heart, but in the end they all subsided, and, resigned to fate, she sat silently watching the show. She did nothing, and nothing could be done. Her father's position was much the same as his daughter's: "Bring this, send for that"; apart from that he too had no say. And how could he have a say? What he wanted, that his daughter should be married at eighteen, was impossible. His wish that his daughter should freely give her own opinion was out of the question.
