# Chapter 7: Azadi Is Married to Maulvi Mustajab, and in the End Weans Him from the Maulvi's Calling

God knows how it was that Azadi's betrothal took place first at all; otherwise what do *maulvi* people know of the fuss and bother of betrothals? Once the matter is settled, they go, have the marriage read, and bring the bride home. No band, no music, no drum or tabor, no decorations, no fireworks, no pomp or display. There was a time when, if there was a wedding in some well-to-do house in a neighbourhood, there was an uproar through the whole city. For weeks the neighbours round about could not get a night's sleep for the noise of the drum-house. Ten or twenty faces were scorched by the fireworks, and two or four heads were broken in the scramble to loot the decorations. At the time, in their vanity, people could see nothing; afterwards the bride's family would beg all their lives, and the groom's family would live by selling the bride's dowry. God bless the *maulvis*, that by preaching and preaching they broke this unbelief and put a stop to customs against the sacred law. Now if some friend of God were born who could somehow stop the expenses the *maulvis* have fastened on the Muslims for themselves and their useless schools, then we too, in our own lifetime, might be just a little glad to see Muslims eating their bread with lentils instead of chutney. Nowadays, in truth, people get married so quietly that only when their children come crawling on their knees into the lane does one learn that this is so-and-so's son. One day it happened that a wedding procession was going along a lane, and from the knees[1] of those with it one could tell that they were followers[2], not of Imam Abu Hanifa, but of some *maulvi*. Just then a gentleman came out of a side lane, stopped short at the crossing and began reciting the creed, and then said to himself: "There is no power nor strength but in God! What a mistake I have made; I thought they were with a funeral." There were two reasons why Azadi's wedding was plain and simple: on one side the *maulvis*' brick-and-a-half[3], with *maulvi* in-laws and a *maulvi* uncle; on the other side the father's English ideas. The *maulvis* wanted only that not a soul should hear of it, and that until he said "*Qabiltu*"[4] with his own mouth nobody should be able to tell which of them was the bridegroom. The father's opinion (though he did not express it) was: "The bride and groom have not seen each other, there is no acquaintance, they have never met; is not such a marriage against reason and far from good sense?" In short, Azadi's wedding did take place, but as cheerless as the *maulvis* could make it. If Azadi had been a doll today, but a doll belonging to the granddaughter of some Nawab's old nurse, then we should have seen a wedding! What was there left in poor Nawab Sahib's possession? His forefathers' villages, gardens, market rows, market towns, rent-free lands and shops had all been mismanaged and gone to the state long ago. Even the women's palace he lives in is mortgaged. The Begam Sahib has not a thread of jewellery left. There was a perpetual English pension of a hundred or a hundred and twenty-five rupees a month, and it has been made over into the moneylender's hands, for generation after generation and descendant after descendant. Out of bundles of fine shawls, two pieces were left, an antiquated brocade gown and a four-garden-pattern kerchief, darned in scores of places; and then such a tangle came about that last summer he flew kites against Agha Wang [TN: reading uncertain], and those two pieces too were forced on somebody for a song. Well, if two old rags went, they went; at least the match stayed in Nawab Sahib's hands. To all appearances Nawab Sahib has not a cowrie of income from anywhere; but the grocer goes on giving him credit, and grocers are a very shrewd tribe; he must have seen something to lean on. Aha! It is the third year since he pawned gold coins worth five hundred for two hundred for the Flower-sellers' Fair [TN: the *Phul-walon ki Sair*, a Delhi festival]; perhaps he means to square that account. So put the Lala on his guard: those gold coins are from the time of Nawab Sahib's grandfather. He got them out of Her Highness the elder Begam Sahib with great difficulty, after much quarrelling, and only by swearing on his youth that he would not sell them. Well, that is the very thing in old-family nobles we are mad about: however straitened, nay, however empty-handed they become, their natural largeness of heart and inborn generosity remain what they are. If a noble is a noble by birth and blood, a pinch of dust is nothing in his eyes, even if it is the philosopher's stone.

Let Nawab Sahib only hear that his grandfather's granddaughter is fretting for a doll's wedding, and then even if he has to pay four for one, nay, a hundred or a thousand for one, he will pay it; how could he bear that his grandfather's heart should be troubled? And, God keep him, he is so lively of spirit too that he will drop all his other pastimes and throw himself into the doll's wedding. "A madman needs only a shout"; he goes about looking for just such excuses.

Azadi was a woman of such heart and mind that a husband to her taste was rather hard to find. God knows at what lucky hour some prayer of Maulvi Mustajab's had been accepted at the court of God, that a wife like Azadi fell to his lot, each better than the other in looks and character. How the Maulvi Sahib could have seemed worthy in Azadi's eyes, who can say; but Azadi did not complain of anything or protest about any want of attention. Whatever was lacking in him was well made up for by the fact that the Maulvi Sahib had become his wife's wife, as it were, and set her shoes straight for her. If Azadi said "Get up," he got up without fail; if she said "Sit down," he sat down without excuse. Azadi, for her part, was not so foolish as to put on airs at seeing her husband obedient and to hold herself aloof. She always regarded her husband as her husband, and took great care that the place she had in his heart today should not one day change in any way and his eyes turn away from her. By degrees such love grew between them that even the *maulvi* women began to envy Azadi. If there was a wedding anywhere among their acquaintance, they would have Azadi recite the Surah of Sincerity[5] for luck and breathe it over the faces of the bride and groom, so that the love and sincerity between this husband and wife might, God willing, be between the bride and groom too. Maulvi Mustajab's life was of the ordinary kind, like a conjugation table recited by rote [TN: reading uncertain]. If he ever told of his own doings, it was only so far as: "In so many days I learnt the Quran by heart, then I studied Persian, then began Arabic. Of the Six Authentic Collections, the second half of Bukhari is left; when that is done, I shall take my certificate in hadith. I write the *fatwas* on inheritance myself; if some troublesome case of successive inheritances comes up, I show it to someone to be safe. By God's grace I have the matters of the *Fatawa-i Alamgiri* at my fingertips; it is a great help in writing answers to queries. In preaching I had great difficulty; I kept putting it off so as not to take up this work, but my father would not agree, so on his advice I began preaching first among the women of my own house. Women, in the first place, have little learning, or rather none, and in the second place they listen to religious things with great devotion. By degrees the women of the neighbourhood began to gather. When I had lost my shyness among the women, I began going round to the villages about the city to preach, such as Sabzi Mandi and Paharganj; and now, by God's grace, I preach every Friday in the middle arch of the Jama Masjid, and people have begun to invite me to their homes too. There are also six or seven lessons in grammar, syntax, logic and law. In short, things are coming along." What else had Mustajab to tell Azadi? But Azadi's tale was very long: she would describe her parents' disagreements for hours on end, and keep Mustajab occupied with her own thoughts for hours together. So much so that, hearing Azadi's talk, there arose of itself in Mustajab's heart a kind of aversion, not to the *maulvi*'s learning but to the *maulvi*'s calling. If this was Azadi's aim, she had indeed hit on a very fine plan. She did not take up the post of adviser, which breeds obstinacy and opposition in the listener; she said what she had to say in the way of stories and tales, and did not hurt his feelings. Aversion bred listlessness, and listlessness brought a change in his routine. His preaching began to be missed and his lessons to become irregular. Once he had been so particular about invitations to meals that it was as if, of all the rights of God's creatures, this was the one right that could not lapse at any time or in any state; now his mind began to look for excuses and pretexts. Just as elegy-reciters have weepers attached to them, so that when the reciter begins to croon, the weepers begin to wail aloud, so preachers too have a few trained special disciples to warm up the gathering. The Maulvi Sahib takes his seat on the pulpit like a king on the throne of empire, and of these one is waving a fan, one is driving away the flies, one sits respectfully in front with his eyes shut, swaying, and now and then rolls his bloodshot eyes and cries "Praise be to God!" in season and out of it; one has the spittoon, another has charge of the water flask, a third has a kerchief under his arm. At first one suspected it was some book, then it turned out that no, it was His Reverence's sandals.

And there is a fourth as well, who stands behind near the pulpit holding His Reverence's staff with the air of Aaron holding the rod of Moses. If few people have come to the sermon, there is nothing to worry about; there are plenty of guests from the house itself. The thread of the story is broken, but I have remembered an amusing tale about weepers, and I cannot keep from telling it. In Johad [TN: reading uncertain] there was a Mir Sahib, a landholder, but two or four harvests in a row went bad; the government collected its arrears on the spot, not a farthing came from the cultivators; landholding is a calamity, and he was so broken that he never recovered. What will a dying man not do? He had a good voice, and took up the reciting of elegies. Every Muharram he got something from those who kept *ta'ziyas* (Muharram shrines), and on that he scraped along in hardship till the next year. He had some land in the town, on which he had settled weavers as his tenants. Mir Sahib roped in two or four of the weavers and made them weepers, and coached them: "What do you do lying about at home all night? Come with me to the gatherings; whenever you hear me reciting, start weeping. The weeper's share is double." Regard for what the landholder said, and greed for a double share: the weavers gladly agreed. They went with Mir Sahib to the gatherings and wept and wailed, and comfortably brought home double shares tied up in a cloth. One day it happened that they reached a gathering; the people called Mir Sahib inside, since he was the reciter, and the weeping weavers were outside in the courtyard with the others. For some reason the gathering was late in beginning. The weavers had no patience to hold back, and began to consult one another: "What is the matter today?" At last one of them wanted to go and ask Mir Sahib himself, but there was no way to reach him. Standing there it struck him that perhaps today it was to be a silent gathering, so he calls out to Mir Sahib: "Mianji, shall I weep?" Although it was a gathering of mourning, and everyone knew about the weepers too (weeping for show can hardly be hidden), on hearing the weaver's "Shall I weep?" everyone in the gathering burst out laughing in spite of themselves, Mir Sahib included. To cover his embarrassment he said this much to his fellow reciters: "As I am a quack reciter, so are my weepers bunglers." In short, from the day Maulvi Mustajab began to preach among men, his father Maulvi Nasih attached several such trained wolves of his own to him. Even before his sermons began to be missed, Mustajab's sermons had grown slack.

The wolves were wonderfully sharp at spotting things, and began to say to the Maulvi Sahib: "Since the marriage your son seems somehow out of sorts." Meanwhile Maulvi Nasih also saw, contrary to custom, that food was being cooked in the house for Mustajab every second or third day. He grew uneasy, and asked his son: "Why, sir, what way is this? These are not the ways of setting up shop. The Jama Masjid, and in the Jama Masjid the middle arch at that, is today the very summit for a preacher. I handed over to you my pitch, settled over years, and because you got it without any trouble, you have valued it so that two Fridays in a row you are missing. And even when you go, what sort of going is it? I know that in that time not even a quarter of a section of the translation can be properly finished. My boy, for eighteen years I preached in that very place for two watches on end; I took breath for just a moment in between for the afternoon prayer, and then preached again. My practice was to begin a part of the Quran in Ramazan and to draw it out so far that, push as I might, one part was finished by the next Ramazan. What is your preaching? Children playing tag. You arrive at who knows how late an hour, and still there is a long while before the afternoon prayer, and in that time you have gone, come back, prayed and preached too. Have you learnt the art of folding up the tongue [TN: a play on *tay al-ard*, the saint's miraculous folding up of distance]? I see that you are even rather shy of going to invitations. Could there be any greater ingratitude than that God gives you such blessings to eat free, without trouble or toil, and you kick them away with such disregard? By this you have lost fifteen or twenty meals, and so be it; but I fear you may do yourself a lasting loss. My boy, winning people's hearts is no easy thing; it needs hard work, steadiness, self-control, conciliation and the winning over of hearts. You have already adopted a manner such that if I were to begin treating people with indifference as you do, in a few days no one would come near me. And what are you, that you have already become a saint [TN: alluding to the proverb "You have not yet become a man, and already you are a pir"]? You are no longer a child. Look at the colour of the age, consider the state of the times, what difficulties we *maulvis* are facing. The rule of the Christians: these people do not openly interfere with anyone's faith and religion, but they have devised such a manner of education that people of themselves come to hate religion. Outwardly there is no compulsion on anyone, no force; but what greater compulsion could there be than that no one gets employment without passing the school examination? Willy-nilly one has to enter the school. And though nothing against religion has been seen in the school books, God knows what effect it is that, first, someone educated at school does not remain firm in religion, and if one in a thousand does, I can swear he does not stay in the *maulvis*' control.

"Look at anyone: suspicious of the *maulvis*, bearing the *maulvis* a gratuitous enmity, set on rooting out the *maulvis*. If you look carefully, the gist of the government's arrangements is that we *maulvis* should not be allowed to remain on the face of the earth; but, as the line goes, 'What can the enemy do, when the Friend is kind?' In between, the *maulvis* had indeed lost some of their standing: they were accused of Wahhabism and the English were made angry with them, the English-educated Muslims raised their heads high, and the missionaries made great efforts; but seeing attacks from all sides, religious zeal arose among people of itself. Societies were founded in place after place under the names of the Support of Islam, the Aid of Islam, the Care of Islam and the Defence of Islam, and the societies' preachers began to go on rounds and tours through towns and cities. Volume upon volume of religious articles and lectures were printed in newspapers and journals. Many new books suited to the times were made for children, so that along with worldly knowledge they should also learn the commandments of religion. Beginner boys, ignorant of religion, unacquainted with faith, who cram history and geography all day, and stay tangled in arithmetic till a watch of the night has passed, and through all their years of study never hear a whisper of the articles of belief: it would be a wonder if they did not become irreligious, a marvel if they did not become unbelievers. All this stir that you see and hear is a good omen for us *maulvis*. It certainly proves this much, that the *maulvis* may once again be valued and respected; and even now that they are not, still there is no better means of livelihood. People are greatly deceived about these school folk; a whole world is caught in the delusion that there can be no comfort without service, and no service without a school education. Not yet in these districts of ours, but in Madras, where the rule is oldest, there really are some B.A.s who are table-servants, nay, grooms. In Bombay and Calcutta men who have passed the Entrance wander about at their wits' end for posts of ten rupees and cannot get them. How worthless and neglected the Middle [TN: the Middle School examination] is here, you probably know yourself; and do you know anything of their hardships? From working and working, the Middle men often become dazed, the Entrance men cranky, the B.A.s deaf, and the M.A.s worn out.

"That is the great merit of the *maulvi*'s calling: little trouble, or if you ask the truth none at all, and success assured. If a man has wits enough just to read Urdu, even if he cannot write, he too has no lack of bread in this calling; and not bread of sorghum and millet either, but *shirmal* and *baqarkhani* (rich sweet breads)."

Maulvi Nasih was a very old and practised preacher, and said himself that he drew out one part of the Quran over a whole year. For such a man one pretext is enough to talk, and once he has begun, what is to stop him? Nasih would not have fallen silent even by the night prayer, but just then the muezzin called: "Your Reverence, the congregation is ready." Nasih went off for the prayer, and Mustajab went and repeated the whole story before Azadi Begam, his teacher as well as his wife, and today, in the talk between husband and wife, the question of the *maulvi*'s calling was decided. Hearing her father-in-law's speech, Azadi said: "The Maulvi Sahib has said nothing unreasonable. There is no doubt that the studies in the schools are very hard work. You know my mother's cousin, who passed some examination in English and got the very highest title; there is not a scrap of flesh on his body, and he is ill all the year round. From poring over books his brain has become so weak that he never has a day free of headache. At his age he is the right hand [TN: reading uncertain] of his elder brother, yet the hair on his head has turned grizzled, and perhaps two or three of his molars are loose too. His sight is so weak that he wears his spectacles even in his sleep. For the post of deputy, it seems, another examination is needed; the poor man cannot read a book, yet for two years running he went to Lahore, and failed both times. Other posts are offered, but he does not like them because of the work, or the low pay, or being away from home. Having no choice, he has taken a post of forty or fifty here in the school. Seeing his state, the people of my mother's family have become so frightened that now no one there even mentions the school. And really, what is the use of such an education but to throw it into the stove, that a man should wear his life out and in the end get just this forty or fifty? The *maulvis* earn this much, and more, sleeping or sitting; and if your income were reckoned up, I know you too must be getting about that much or a little less; and how long have you been at it?"

Mustajab: All this is true, but I do not like these earnings. Ever since I heard your father's objections from your lips, my heart has so turned against this calling that I cannot preach, and I do not want to go to invitations. Outwardly people show me respect, but in my own eyes I grow smaller. It was some five or six days ago that I went with my father to an invitation; the students were invited too. Everyone ate, and the two of us, father and son, kept standing and strolling among the students as usual. Then I saw some people sitting outside in the courtyard talking among themselves and looking our way. I listened carefully, and one man was saying: "What rudeness! They did not even ask the Maulvi Sahib, and they have started feeding the students." Another answered: "Brother, you don't know; *maulvi* folk do not eat at invitations; their share goes to their house." Hearing this, all who were sitting there kept looking at us out of the corners of their eyes and laughing. What could be a more dishonourable living than that? It is true that when they saw us leaving, even those who had been laughing at us stood up out of respect. But you can understand what pleasure there can be in such false and hypocritical respect. In my view, earning a living through the *maulvi*'s calling, in whatever way and in whatever form, is dishonourable in itself. Taking by asking, and taking by putting on the look of asking, is taking all the same; I see no difference between the two. Those who give to us are human beings and we are human beings. The same two hands and two feet as theirs, the same two hands and two feet ours. Why should we look to anyone's hand? Why should we lay our burden on others?

Azadi: That is the very objection Father used to make; but Father gives this answer to it: just as there are other kinds of service, so the *maulvi*'s work is a kind of service too; and if they did no other work, they gave people religious teaching and instruction. If there is no dishonour in service, no dishonour in a trade, no dishonour in labour and toil, why should there be any in the *maulvi*'s calling?

Mustajab: That is the *maulvis*' way of fooling themselves. The whole world knows, and the *maulvis* themselves know too, that whatever is given to the *maulvis* is given as charity and alms and for God's sake; and I cannot accept that disgrace.

Azadi: Of course, that is the snag that has come in; otherwise the calling was a very noble one.

Mustajab: There is no question about the nobility of learning; but there are two great flaws in this calling. First, hypocrisy, which is such an evil thing that the thought of it comes to a man in any case; and once one is in the *maulvi*'s calling, it is as hard to escape hypocrisy as to live in a coal shop and not get one's face black. Second, one's eye falls on other people's faults (preaching and fault-finding go hand in hand), and from that is born conceit, so that a man begins to think himself better and chosen. So perhaps he tries to put others right, but goes wrong himself. I can say nothing against the dignity of the learned, but what can I do? I am not sure of my own self.

Azadi: People are guided, they keep from disobeying God, those who have strayed come back to the straight path; is that a work of small reward?

Mustajab: True; but my own soul too has a right over me, for which I shall be called to account. Why should I not spend the very time I spend on reforming others on putting myself right? And if I do this work as a meritorious act, then praise be to God, that is a fine thing; but that means precisely that I should not make it a means of livelihood, not make it a trade.

Azadi: Have you told the Maulvi Sahib these thoughts of yours too?

Mustajab: The Maulvi Sahib never gave me a chance to speak. Once he began, he kept up a steady stream; then came the summons to prayer, and he got up and went straight off.

Azadi: He must suspect that you cannot work hard; and it would be no wonder if he lumped me in with you too.

Mustajab: An idea that has no basis at all cannot last long. What I do next will certainly show him that his idea was wrong.

Azadi: Will it show him, or will it fix the idea even more firmly? You will be lying about the house day and night; seeing you as he comes and goes, he will certainly say to himself: "So these are the joys of lying about at home." And let me tell you plainly, it would be no wonder if he came to find even our expenses a burden. There is an age for being a burden on one's parents too. So if you have made up your mind to give up the *maulvi*'s calling, then understand this too, that the Maulvi Sahib is not going to put up with us; and there is no blame in that either; after all, how long could he?

Mustajab: I understand very well; but just think why it is that I shrink from the *maulvi*'s calling. Only because my nature cannot bear to be under obligation to people. So how could I bear to be under this obligation to my father, that he should bear my expenses, especially now that he has married me off? By his own lights he taught me a craft by which to earn my bread, and, if you ask the truth, set me to the means of bread. Now if I do not want to earn my bread by that means, it is my fault, not his. From the moment the dislike of this calling arose in my heart, I made up my mind to separate; and if I have hesitated all these days, it was because of you, to see what your opinion would be. The owner of the Muhammadi Press and I are old acquaintances, more than mere acquaintances, and I know that Nawab Siddiq Hasan Khan of Bhopal has his books printed at that very press, and that they need a man who knows Arabic there for annotating and correcting. For the present the pay is thirty rupees, but it may rise by degrees to fifty. In my own estimation I can do this work well; all sorts of books will pass before my eyes, and my learning will grow too. If you advise it, I will take this post. You think out the running of the household.

Azadi: What great big household is there, that it will be hard to run? Don't even give it a thought. As for the post, to all appearances it is good in every way. The work suits your nature, it is a means of connection with a great court, and with a view to future advancement the pay is not so small either. The only thing is that it means being under a friend; in friendship there is a claim to equality, and in service that claim cannot remain.

Mustajab: The owner of the press has no say in the work I shall have to do; being under him is only in name; the real connection will be with the Nawab Sahib. And even if it does mean being under a friend, that is many degrees better than being obliged to every Tom, Dick and Harry for a pice or two, or for a meal or two.

Azadi: There is one thought more, the Maulvi Sahib's displeasure. Your taking service may perhaps be against his liking.

Mustajab: Why "perhaps"? Service will certainly displease him, but there is no help for it. Settling on a means of livelihood for myself is my business. Only this much is necessary, that it should not be against the sacred law and should not stain the family's honour, and neither of these is the case. And then, what of a father's displeasure? After being displeased for a few days he will turn kind of himself; and when he learns my reasons, it would be no wonder if in his heart he were convinced as well.

[2] This is said in irony: they think it wrong to follow Abu Hanifa, and are followers of the *maulvis* of this age.

[3] An allusion to the well-known proverb: "a separate mosque of a brick and a half" [TN: said of those who split off into a little sect of their own].

[4] At the marriage the man has to say this word, which means "I have accepted."

[5] That is, *Qul huwa Allah* [TN: the 112th surah of the Quran].
