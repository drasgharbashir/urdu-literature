# Chapter 10: Maulvi Muqtada Preaches a Sermon on Patience

He took this verse of the Surah of the Cow: "*And We shall surely test you with something of fear and hunger, and loss of wealth and lives and fruits; and give good tidings to the patient, who, when a calamity strikes them, say, 'Surely we belong to God, and to Him we return.' Upon them are blessings from their Lord, and mercy; and it is they who are rightly guided*." [TN: The Quran, 2:155–157.] What the Maulvi Sahib said about the lexical meanings, the grammatical construction, the eloquence and rhetoric, the occasion of revelation, the variant readings and the connection with what comes before and after, was excellent in itself but out of place in a sermon to women; leaving that much aside, we set down the rest.

The Maulvi Sahib began in this way: God Most High, glorious is His majesty, addressing the Muslims who were the Prophet's contemporaries, says: "*La-nabluwannakum*," We shall surely, surely test you. *La-nabluwanna* is one word in which there is the *lam* of emphasis and the *nun* of emphasis: not one emphasis but a double one. By this He means to warn those addressed: "Do not take Our word as something casual and passing; what We say will certainly come to pass." The mountains may move from their places, the sun and moon and stars may change their courses, the rivers that flow from west to east may begin to flow backwards; in short, the world and all that is in it may change, but not one word of God, nay not one letter, nay not one dot or one flourish, can change. In another place too He has said: "*There is no changing the words of God*": there is no altering of God's words. Then, although those addressed in *la-nabluwannakum* were the Muslims of the Prophet's time, this address is directed to all Muslims till the Day of Resurrection; that is, all the Muslims who will be on the face of the earth until the Day of Resurrection will be tested, and all will be examined. So this is a general examination from which no Muslim is exempt; whoever dies must die having sat the examination. Those who are alive now are under examination at all times, and those who will come hereafter will have to sit this examination too. In worldly examinations it is announced beforehand that the examination will be in such-and-such an art, such-and-such a science, such-and-such a book, so that the candidate may study and learn and get ready. You must have heard the word "course" from the schoolboys; the course means the particular books, and particular parts of the books, in which the examination is held. If a course were not fixed, the student would be in great difficulty. There are many books in a science, and one person cannot take in all of them in a limited time, so he must choose some. But if no course were named, it is possible that he would choose one book and the examiner would set questions from another; and that would not be an examination but a way of making him fail. When worldly examiners, seeing this unfairness, fix a course beforehand, God Most High, glorious is His majesty, is a most just and most merciful examiner; and in another place He says: "*Surely God does not wrong anyone by the weight of an atom; and if it is a good deed He multiplies it, and gives from Himself a great reward*": God does not do even a grain's weight of injustice, and if there is a good deed, He multiplies it many times over and grants a great reward from Himself.

So God too fixed the course of His examination and announced it to all the believers: "*With something of fear and hunger, and loss of wealth and lives and fruits*." In what shall We examine you? A little fear, a little hunger, a little loss. What kindness of the Lord of the worlds! The very name of an examination is a dreadful thing, and on hearing it a person's hands and feet swell up with fright. There is an Arabic proverb: "*At the examination a man is honoured or disgraced*": here, at the time of the examination, a man's honour is lost or kept. So God Most High, considering the weakness of His servants, declared: "Do not be alarmed at hearing the name of the examination, do not be afraid; We do not set a hard examination, not much: We shall only send a little of three things each, and see." First, fear: the Muslims of that time were always in fear of the unbelievers. In that country government was not of the kind where there is one king who keeps order for the peace and comfort of his subjects, and all fear him and all obey his command. The whole country was lawless, and every man was his own ruler. Those people had worshipped idols from their fathers, grandfathers and great-grandfathers onwards; besides the idols in their houses and tribes, they had brought hundreds of idols and filled the Ka'ba with them. When the Prophet began the call to Islam, that is, called people to the worship of the One God, those people were furious. And since this new religion entailed the uprooting of idol worship, the dishonouring of the idols and contempt for their forefathers, the whole country agreed not to let this new religion spread and flourish. It is the rule that when a man is left without an answer in an argument, he takes to fighting to defend his words; and those people were quarrelsome too. They began to pick all sorts of quarrels with the Prophet and his companions, whose number was then very small. Those who belonged to a clan and a party were somewhat protected; apart from them there was no Muslim soul who did not suffer all kinds of torments at the unbelievers' hands. The unbelievers cut off their food and drink, broke off ties of kinship with them, and if they found one or two alone, beat them and dishonoured them. The Muslims dared not give the call to prayer aloud or go to the Ka'ba to worship God.

Helpless, the Muslims left house and home and went abroad for fear for their religion; and those who could not go met with such afflictions that if they were told in detail your hair would stand on end to hear them. The unbelievers did not let the Muslims rest in peace even abroad; they made raid after raid on them, and beat and harassed those who gave them shelter. All these efforts had this aim, that the religion of Islam should not spread; but who can prevail against one whose helper is God? "*They want to put out the light of God with their mouths, but God refuses except to perfect His light, even though the unbelievers dislike it*": the unbelievers are bent on blowing out the light of God, and God does not allow it; He will make His light shine and spread, and if the unbelievers dislike it, let them. And so it came about. Those Muslims who were fleeing and hiding in fear of their lives, when God helped them, conquered countries and became the kings of the age. In "*something of fear*" there is a hint at this early state of the Muslims. It is a matter for great thanks that God has not tested us by keeping us in such a state of fear; but we ought to think for ourselves and take account of our own souls, how far we could stand firm in our religion and faith if our state were like that. Brothers, the way I see the Muslims, for fear of the brotherhood, for fear of people's taunts, for fear of the world's ill opinion, they do many things against good sense and against the sacred law, and say, "We are helpless." Then how could those whose hearts are so weak and whose faith is so feeble bear the fear with which God tested the Muslims of the first age? Warding off harm and seeking benefit are two things, and in moral philosophy these two have been held to be the source and end of all human qualities; but as far as I can see, the two are one thing, and if there is a difference it is one of viewpoint and wording, for the loss of a benefit is also a kind of harm. But this discussion is rather intricate, and this is not the occasion for setting out such subtle questions. My purpose in saying this is that the moralists have made fear and greed two, and I wished to prove that they are one. If we fear a thing, that fear is of the harm of losing some benefit; so why should it not be called greed? The upshot is that I hold greed too to be a kind of fear, and wish to show that just as the coin of our faith is not fit to be tested in the fire of fear, so it is not fit to be rubbed on the touchstone of greed either. In the first place, when a man lets fear enter into religion, what assurance can there be that he will not let greed enter too?

What difference is there if, suppose, one man accepts Islam from fear and another from greed? Both are equally weak of heart: the one could not resist fear, the other could not resist temptation. In the same way, suppose we know of a thing that by religion it is right or permissible to do it, and we do not do it for fear of people; that is a sign that if, instead of fear, we had been offered an inducement not to do it, then just as we held back from doing it out of fear, so we would have held back from doing it out of greed. Now see how many opponents Islam has in this country. First, and most of all, the Hindus, among whom we must perforce live. About them we are quite assured that by frightening us or holding out temptations they cannot in any way bring us into their religion. We do not know which of our customs the Hindus have adopted; we, by living with them, have certainly become half Hindus. No fear, no temptation, and we have become half Hindus; then with fear and temptation it would have been no wonder if we had begun openly worshipping idols. And hidden idol worship there is a great deal of among the Muslims even now. Perhaps this saying of mine seems an exaggeration to you; but come with me to Sultan Ji Sahib, Qutb Sahib, the Qadam Sharif, the Asar Sharif, Sayyid Hasan Rasul-numa, Khwaja Baqi Billah and the other places of pilgrimage, and see: the common people worship openly, and the select, who have heard something from us *maulvis* [TN: reading uncertain], do worship too, but under cover of veneration:

Your excuse may pass with us,
But it will not pass with the Lord who knows the unseen.

The polytheists too made just such excuses: "*These are our intercessors with God*": those whom you say we take for gods are not gods; they are our means of approach at God's court. "*We worship them only so that they may bring us near to God*": we only worship them so that they may bring us close to God. Brothers, when God did not accept this excuse from the polytheists, why should He accept such an excuse, worse than the sin, from you, who are the community of the Prophet who was sent only to make the Unity of God pure and firm? And suppose that you understand it as you say and believe it in your hearts too; still not everyone is learned and accomplished like you. In the Prophet's community there are many unlettered people who cannot even recite the *Alhamd* properly; they cannot make the distinction between veneration and worship. They bribe the head clerk, not thinking that he will speak to the officer and get their business done, but believing that the officer is a ruler in name only and the head clerk is the general master of black and white. When they had any need, they did not look to God and the Prophet at all, but would seek out some *pir* (saint) or other, dead or living. Rain is sent by the *pir*, children are given by the *pir*, the sick are healed by the *pir*. In their imagination the *pir* is not a second God but God Himself. So, brothers, you who understand, have pity on the state of these Muslim brothers. It must not happen that you offer veneration and they begin to offer worship; that you take hold of the hem and they take off the cap. The second opposing religion is the religion of the Christians, who are also the rulers of the time. Well, it is another matter that they call Jesus the son of God and believe in the Trinity, which in our religion is a kind of unbelief too. God Most High says: "*The Jews say, 'Ezra is the son of God,' and the Christians say, 'The Messiah is the son of God.' That is the saying of their mouths; they imitate the saying of those who disbelieved before. God fight them! How they are turned away!*": the Jews say Ezra is God's son, and the Christians say the Messiah is God's son; this is the talk of their own mouths; they too have begun to make up the sayings of those who disbelieved before them; God's curse on them, where are they straying! But whatever may be the case elsewhere, the Christians who rule our country neither interfere with others' religion nor hold out any kind of temptation to bring people into their own. Leave aside the padres; they too are Christians, but I have made a thorough inquiry, and they have no say whatever in the government. Yet with neither threats nor inducements, there are still thousands of Muslims who want to make their outward appearance like the English; can one then expect that if the pressure of fear or greed were brought to bear, such people could stand firm in Islam? I do not think ill of my Muslim brothers, and thinking ill is a bad thing too: "*Surely some suspicion is sin*" (some suppositions are sins); nor am I praising myself in this guise: "*So do not claim purity for yourselves; He knows best who is God-fearing*" (do not proclaim your own innocence with your own mouths; God knows the God-fearing well). Rather I draw a conclusion from the outward state of things, and I think that just as they have imposed the condition of an examination for posts, if, God forbid, a condition of religion were also laid down, that for instance the post of *tahsildar* and higher posts were to be given to no one but Christians, I cannot say how many Muslims' faith would be shaken. In short, it is a pity that so far we have failed in the examination of fear; but the examinations have not come to an end. O brothers, strive so that you do not fail when it is set again.

The second thing in the examination is *ju'*, that is, hunger. By this perhaps famine is meant: the Prophet, worn out by the unbelievers, cursed them, praying: "O God, these people will not give up disobeying You; send down upon them such a famine as there was in the time of Joseph." And so for seven years on end there was such a famine that men ate men. Or perhaps there is a hint at the campaign of Tabuk: when the Prophet marched on Tabuk, the Muslim army was so ill equipped that the fighters lived by eating the leaves of trees. Good God, what faith was theirs, that they went to give their lives in God's way hungry and thirsty, with neither mount nor weapons! By God, before such people one is ashamed to call oneself a Muslim and to speak of faith. Is there anyone among us who would endure such hardships and face such calamities for the sake of faith? But the more fitting interpretation, and the one that appeals to the heart, is that by hunger the People of the Bench are meant. There is disagreement as to how many they were, but they may have been between sixty or seventy and a hundred; the People of the Bench were those who had emigrated from Mecca to Medina for fear of the unbelievers. In their own homes they had been well off and comfortable, but when they fled in secret they came with nothing but the nose on their face and their two ears, and could bring nothing with them. Here in Medina their Muslim brothers looked after them with bread and scraps as far as they could, but looking after people has its limits too. If there is one loaf, they can share half each; but if there is not even one in the house, or half a dozen want a share of half a loaf, then giver and taker, host and guest, both stay as hungry as ever. The result was that the People of the Bench went about with stones tied over their bellies; when they stood in prayer they grew dizzy, and when they sat down they could not stand up again for weakness. When their food was like this, what need to ask about their clothing? Only a small blanket: the same for covering, the same for bedding, the same for shirt, the same for trousers. And if only it had been big enough for them to wrap it round themselves properly! Besides being old and filthy, it was so small that if they held it with their hands, they would fall over in prostration. If some such ragged man, dirty and filthy and in tatters, came and sat among you, you would certainly feel disgust; but the Prophet of God, when he had leisure, would come and sit freely among them for hours. Poor outwardly and rich inwardly, beggars to look at and in reality the near ones of God. Of such people the Prophet said: "*Many a one with dishevelled hair and covered in dust, if he swore an oath by God, God would fulfil it.*" There are many people whose outside is unkempt and dusty, but God holds their wishes so dear that if they set their hearts on something, God cannot but fulfil their insistence.

It was also a purpose of God that He tested them with poverty and want. The fact is that those in whose eyes the world has value count its hardship as hardship and its comfort as comfort; but in God's sight, as has come in the noble hadith, the world has not the worth of a gnat's wing, not even the weight of a mosquito's feather; and that is why, as regards worldly comfort, there is no distinction or difference between believer and unbeliever. And so God Most High Himself has said: "*And were it not that mankind would become one community, We would have given to those who disbelieve in the Merciful roofs of silver for their houses, and stairways by which to climb, and doors for their houses, and couches on which to recline, and ornaments of gold. Yet all that is nothing but the enjoyment of the life of this world; and the hereafter, with your Lord, is for the God-fearing.*" He says: the world is so contemptible in Our sight that, were it not for the thought that the whole world would become unbelieving, We would give the unbelievers so much wealth that they would roof their houses with silver, climb silver stairways, fit silver doors, have silver thrones made, and lounge on carpets with bolsters; and silver apart, if We wished We would grant them just as much gold. But what would come of it? The people of the world are fools anyway; they would think that God likes unbelief, and that this is perhaps the reason He has made all the unbelievers comfortable, and they would all start disbelieving. For that reason We have not singled out the unbelievers in the world. This gold and silver may be things of worth and value only in their eyes; consider it and you will see that it is all the passing benefit of the few days' life of this world; the real and eternal benefits are those of the hereafter, and they are reserved for the God-fearing. Brothers, never be proud of worldly comfort, and never, never take it as a proof of God's pleasure. I tell you truly, worldly prosperity is a very dangerous thing. Have you not seen that verse of the Quran: "*And on the day when those who disbelieved are presented to the Fire: 'You used up your good things in your life of the world and took your enjoyment of them; so today you are recompensed with the punishment of humiliation, because you were arrogant in the earth without right, and because you were transgressing*'": remember the day when those who disbelieved will be brought before the Fire, and it will be said to them: "Whatever good was to reach you has reached you in the life of the world, and you took your fill of comfort in the world; now suffer the punishment of disgrace, in return for your wrongful boasting on the earth and in return for your evil deeds." There are only two things, this world and religion. Those whose life in this world was spent in grief and trouble, if they hope, "Well, the world went badly, it would be no wonder if God made it go well there," there is room for patience: He is most generous and merciful; if He did not give here, He will give there.

But those who enjoyed themselves in the world, ate the best of everything, wore the best of everything, and lived in comfort, what right have they to set such hopes? If they have made some such covenant and pact with God, let them produce it, showing that God picked up His other servants from the rubbish heap, while He made them with His own special hand and shows them special favour. God Most High has left nothing out of His Book; there is a verse in the Quran on this very theme: "*Or have you oaths from Us, binding until the Day of Resurrection, that you shall have whatever you judge?*": have you taken an oath from Us until the Day of Resurrection that whatever you go on ordering We shall go on carrying out? It is a property of human nature that, being born in the world and opening our eyes on the world alone, we think that this world is all there is, and that man's grief and comfort end in it. Such people judge God's pleasure and displeasure by worldly happiness and unhappiness: "*As for man, when his Lord tries him and honours him and blesses him, he says, 'My Lord has honoured me'; but when He tries him and restricts his provision, he says, 'My Lord has humiliated me'*": when worldly honour and favour came from God, they thought, "Yes, God has honoured us"; when there was straitness of provision, they took it that God's eye had turned away from them. But this is a sheer mistake; the world is the house of examination, and the result that is to follow from this examination will follow in the hereafter: "*A party in the Garden*" (O God, make us among them) "*and a party in the Blaze*" (O God, do not make us among them): one party in Paradise and one party in Hell. To warn His servants against this mistake God said: "*Do they think that in what We give them of wealth and sons We are hastening good things to them? No, but they do not perceive*": do they think that this help We are giving them with wealth and children is the reward for their good deeds, which they are getting for the present in the world? No, no, you have not understood its meaning at all. So, O brothers, fear God at all times; and if the end is good, and things go well with God when we get there, then the few days' hardships of the world are not at all worth caring about; and if, God forbid, things go badly there, what use can the fleeting pleasures of the world be? It would be like the descendants of Timur: leaving aside the question whether their descent is genuine or not, they are called *Sahib-i Alam* and princes, and yet they go about begging. "*So take warning, O you who have eyes!*"

What happiness can it give them to think that their forefathers really were kings? Indeed my own idea is that if there is any sensible man among them (and if they were sensible, why would they have come to this?), in any case if even a little sense has touched anyone, the thought of this princely rank is like an itch on top of leprosy. Such is worldly comfort compared with the suffering of the hereafter. Take confirmation of this from the Quran too: "*Have you considered: if We let them enjoy themselves for years, and then there comes to them what they were promised, of what avail to them will be the enjoyment they were given?*": just consider, if We did let them enjoy worldly benefits for a few years, and then at last the promised punishment arrived, what good would that past enjoyment do them? Yes, so God tested His Prophet's companions with hunger, that is, poverty and want, and they bore that calamity with patience, steadfastness, gratitude and good cheer, and God rewarded them both in this world and in religion: "*God is well pleased with them, and they are well pleased with Him*": God is pleased and happy with them, and they are pleased and happy with God. Among us too, whom God wishes (and perhaps there are many such) He tests with the hardship of hunger. How many servants of God there are with large families and small incomes, hard times, earnings that do not cover expenses; on them such days often pass that they make do with whatever thing of the season they see is cheap, guavas, water chestnuts, corn cobs, carrots, green gram, mangoes, melons. They sent for four pice worth of parched gram, everyone ate a handful each, drank on top of it a gourd of water or plain water, felt a weight in their stomachs, lay down and slept. Although men and women alike all work in their own places, what can they do? It somehow never stretches far enough. Do not think that God means to test only those who go hungry. God tests their patience, to see whether in hardship too they remember Us, and are grateful to Us, and hope for Our grace and favour, or not; and He examines the hearts of those who have means, to see how far they take pity on the poor, and, as far as they are able, consider it their duty to pass on something of what God has given them to the needy and to sympathise with the destitute. If fasting too is reckoned as part of the hunger I am speaking of, it would not be out of place. This too is in truth a great examination, that a man, though he has the means, should keep his mouth closed all day only for God's sake, neither eating nor drinking anything. By this a person comes to value his daily bread, to recognise the affliction of those who starve, and to see his own helplessness, what state he comes to in one day of hunger and thirst.

It is proved from the hadith that there is no better means than fasting for restraining the desires of the self. Now see how we bear this hunger. You know better than I how many people do not keep the fasts without any excuse; and of those who do keep them, the fast is kept as following custom, for fear of people's teasing, that if they do not keep it people will call them fast-breakers and make them out to be God's thieves. In the hot weather I have seen people with a water spray in hand from a watch after sunrise, pouring bucketfuls of water over themselves. No doubt there is some weakness, but not such that they should lose heart. If people would take the fast as God's command and carry it out gladly, I think there would not be as much complaining and wailing about Ramazan as is seen and heard. "*Deeds are judged by intentions*": the deed depends on the intention; this is a widely transmitted hadith; and the state of the intention God knows best of all, and after God the person who has it. We judge by the outside; why should a person bring anyone else in between himself and God? Why should he not take account of his own soul himself? With everyone there goes a natural *mufti* (giver of legal opinions); another *mufti* may make a mistake, but this *mufti* never errs: "*Ask your heart for a ruling*": when you are in doubt about anything, ask your own heart. Now there remains the third thing in God's examination, and the examination is over. What is it? "*And loss of wealth and lives and fruits*": loss in wealth, in lives and in fruits. The master of lexicography, the author of the *Qamus*, has written that among the Arabs the word *mal* (wealth) is applied only to livestock, such as camels, horses, sheep and goats, because those people have no other kind of wealth, or if they have it is rare. Just as in our country the chief food is bread, and people speak of it and say, "It is time for bread," or "He has gone to eat bread," although sometimes there is no bread at all but only *pulao* or something else in its place. The people of Lucknow do not care for this, and laugh at this idiom of ours. Loss of wealth, lives and fruits are losses of such a kind that in no age has any class of mankind, nay probably no single human being, been safe from them; and what need to ask about the Muslims of the first age? Those poor people were, at the very beginning, in loss after loss in every way as regards the world.

To start with, they had no assurance of life itself; they believed afterwards and resolved to die beforehand. They knew for certain that the moment they spoke the name of the One God their heads would be cut off. They knew well that if they swore allegiance at the Prophet's hand, they would have to leave everything: wife and children, brothers and kin, house and home, wealth and goods, relations and near ones, friends and acquaintances, helpers and supporters. A man who has committed murder, or robbery, or theft, or broken into someone's house, may escape by paying something, by entreaty and flattery, by bringing efforts and recommendations to bear; but to hope that these people would pardon the crime of believing in the One God was simply futile: "*And they took vengeance on them only because they believed in God, the Mighty, the Praiseworthy, to whom belongs the kingdom of the heavens and the earth*": the fault they proved against them was that they believed in God, who prevails over all, whom the whole world praises, and whose kingdom is in earth and heaven. I have been telling you from the start that God tried the companions of His Prophet, God bless him, in every way, tested them in every way, and found them of full standard. Then what was lacking at His court? The kingship of the world followed close at their heels. Sixty years is not such a long time either, and in that short span they set the stamp of Islam on the whole face of the earth. And as for their ranks in religion, God alone knows them: "*No soul knows what joy of the eyes is kept hidden for them, as a reward for what they used to do*": no one knows what coolness of the eyes God has kept ready for them, in return for their deeds. They spent their lives like this for the sake of religion; and the Prophet's community has acknowledged their merit in this way, that those among them who were the Prophet's chief counsellors and companions, and in one sense his benefactors, are the very ones they have begun to abuse and curse. Could there be any greater ingratitude than this? Now we return again to the subject of the examination. But one thing in this verse deserves consideration: in this verse there are two series of conjunctions, one "*of fear and hunger and loss*", and the other "*of wealth and lives and fruits*". There are three things in each series. The order in the first series rises from the lesser to the greater, for fear is a light and easy thing compared with hunger, and likewise hunger is a light and easy thing compared with loss; but in the second series this order seems on the face of it to be turned upside down, for life, the dearest of all things, has come in the middle, and fruits have come after everything. But there is a subtle point in this.

I have not seen it in any commentary; perhaps it has come under someone else's eye. The point is this: by fruits is meant provision, and that is the mainstay of life. Let someone ask its value of the landholders who grow grain. You are city people; most of you will never even have had occasion to see the trees of the countryside. The landholders are ready to fight and die for every span of land, because they regard the land as the means not only of their own livelihood but of the livelihood of their children and their children's children. So fruits are dearer even than life, and that is the reason why fruits come last of all. "*This is what my Lord has inspired in me.*" Well, the examination is over; now two things remain. One is the standard of success: just as in school examinations, for instance, a hundred marks are full marks, and to pass it is necessary to get at least a third of the marks, so for this divine examination too some standard of success must be fixed. The second is the reward of success: if one comes through the examination in full, what prize will be had from the government: a stipend, or a medal, or a title, books, or a post, or an honour? It would seem that, the standard of success and the reward of success being two things, they ought to be stated separately; but this is the very excellence of God's word, that the more you reflect, the more points of eloquence keep coming out. After sitting the examination students are in a hurry to know the result; that is why God Most High has stated the result and the reward together, and has indeed put the reward first: "*And give good tidings to the patient, who, when a calamity strikes them, say, 'Surely we belong to God, and to Him we return.' Upon them are blessings from their Lord, and mercy; and it is they who are rightly guided*": and, O Prophet, give the good news to those who are patient, who, when some calamity falls on them, say: "We belong to God, and to Him we must return." These are the ones upon whom are their Lord's favours and mercy, and these are the ones who walk on the right path. God Most High has stated the reward so excellently that there could be no better way. He did not say that they will receive gardens to dwell in for ever and ever, in which rivers of honey and milk and pure wine will be flowing, which will stay ever green and fruitful and in flower, through which autumn never passes. He did not say that in the world they were forbidden to adorn themselves, that being men they were not to wear gold and silver jewellery and silken clothes like women, and that now this ban will be lifted from them and they will be allowed to wear whatever they like.

He did not say that they will be given houris to serve them. He did not say that they will live in perfect comfort, joy, contentment, peace and full freedom. He did not say that they will see God. No, what He did say was: "O Prophet, give them good tidings." It is as if a king were to say to some servant: "If you carry out such-and-such a service, We shall make you rich." In the king's vagueness there is much greater scope than in specifying, "We shall give you a robe of honour of seven pieces, or an estate, or a title, or a rank." When the time comes to fulfil the promise, then, as the line goes, "Each man's thought is in the measure of his spirit": the king will give according to his own greatness of heart. When this is the case with the kings of the world, this promise is the promise of the Emperor of both worlds, at whose court nothing is lacking. Blessed is the lot of those to whom the Prophet of God, God bless him, gives good tidings from God; know that their lifelong poverty is over. And then, not bare good tidings, as if they were mere hopefuls [TN: reading uncertain]: no, along with the good tidings He also gave them the title of "the patient". Think of this world too as a sample of the hereafter; the very things that are here are there too, but these are the copy and those the original, these the figure and those the reality, these imperfect and those perfect, these perishing and those lasting. The kings of the world too, when they are pleased with someone, give titles according to rank, so that the holder of the title may have honour and esteem among his peers; but since the world itself is altogether absurd, nothing in it is free of absurdity. It is the same proverb: "Camel, O camel, which part of you is straight?" In titles there is no limit left to absurdity. I used to wonder at Pharaoh saying, "*I am your Lord Most High*"[1]: after all he was a man like us; he could not have borne hunger or endured thirst, just as we cannot; at a little pain he must have grown restless, just as we grow restless; without sleep he too could have had no rest, just as we have none; then with what face did he call himself "*I am your Lord Most High*"? The wretch might at least have understood: "I cannot create, I cannot bring the dead to life, I cannot send down water and rain, I cannot make grain grow from the earth; how can I be a lord, and a lord at that, 'your Lord Most High'?" But ever since I have heard titles such as "Sky-high in Worth", "Saturn in Dignity", "Solomon in Majesty", and others of the kind, I have understood that there is no end to man's vanity. No doubt that fool said "*I am your Lord Most High*," and to this day the birth of such fools who are ready to become "your Lord Most High" has not stopped.

Well, in short, from the court of God Most High, exalted and glorious is His majesty, the righteous also receive titles: "*And whoever obeys God and the Messenger, they are with those whom God has blessed: the prophets, the truthful, the martyrs and the righteous*"[2]; these are all divine titles, and likewise "the believers", "the God-fearing", "the doers of good" and so on; and among such titles one title is "the patient". Just as worldly titles differ in rank, so it is with religious titles. I cannot say of what degree the title "the patient" is; but this much is known, that this title is reserved for those near to the Lord, for God Most High has said: "*Surely God is with the patient*": truly God is with those who are patient. Being with someone implies nearness and companionship; so the perfection of servanthood is that the servant should be near to God, and the youths of Paradise and the Garden of Ridwan are nothing compared with nearness to God. Then He says that the people worthy and deserving of this honoured title are those who, when calamity falls, say, "*Surely we belong to God, and to Him we return*." By "*they say, 'Surely we belong to God, and to Him we return'*" is not meant that one says "Surely we belong to God, and to Him we return" with the tongue, out of habit, without understanding. Some people get into the habit of swearing, and at every word they blurt out "By God, by God!" In the terminology of the sacred law this is called a thoughtless oath. No expiation is due for such an oath, but to take God's name with such carelessness is a great disrespect and a want of manners: "*Will you not then desist?*": say with your mouth, will you stop even now or not? In the same way, among Muslims a sort of habit has grown up of saying "Surely we belong to God, and to Him we return"; and just as that "By God, by God" is a thoughtless oath, so this "Surely we belong to God" is an idle utterance, with neither expiation nor reward for it. One who merely says "Surely we belong to God" with his mouth out of habit is neither deserving of the good tidings, nor worthy of the title of the patient, nor fit for his Lord's blessings, nor eligible for His mercy. By "*they say, 'Surely we belong to God'*" is meant that the meaning and sense of "Surely we belong to God, and to Him we return" should be fixed in the heart like a carving in stone, so that even at the time of calamity one is certain of it, and instead of complaint, wailing and lament the same words come from the mouth too; then the reward of patience is received. Now understand what "Surely we belong to God, and to Him we return" means. It means: we belong to God, and to Him we must return. On the face of it, it seems a rather pointless saying; that is, its connection with calamity is not understood. At first sight there should have been some prayer of the kind: "O God, grant us the grace of patience," or "Forgive our sins," or "Have mercy on us," or "Forgive the dead and give patience to those left behind."

What is this, "We are Yours, and to You we must return"? You hear plenty of sermons, but these subtle points that come in this humble one's sermons you will not have heard anywhere: "*That is the bounty of God, which He gives to whom He wills; and God is the Lord of great bounty.*" But God's word cannot be pointless; pointless are we, and pointless our understanding. If God gives sound sense, one learns that in "Surely we belong to God, and to Him we return" there are two things, submission to His will and prayer. To say that we belong to God implies the special relation we have with God and God has with us; but alas, we do not understand it, and even if we understand it somewhat, we do not act on it; that is, it is not proved from our deeds that we have a special kind of relation with God. Now consider the special relations of the world, after which we wander about day and night, bewildered, at our wits' end and distressed. In the first place, since the world is a coming and going and a perishing inn, it is a mistake even to call any relation in the world, however close and strong, a special relation. The first, and perhaps the strongest and purest of all, is the relation the child has with its parents and the parents with the child; but all of us present here, men and women, have been children in our time, most of us have children, and there are examples in every house. When we or anyone have grown up, does the same childhood relation remain at all with our parents, or for our parents with us? Do parents whose children die before them die too along with their children? No. Does one whose parents die before him grow angry with the world and die along with his parents? No. Do any parents keep company with their children, or any children with their parents, in suffering, in pain, in sickness, in health? No. Mother or father or child, all are separate persons; each one's daily bread is separate, hunger separate, thirst separate, desire separate, lifespan separate, fate separate. It is a divine arrangement that one has gone on being born from another, until over the whole face of the earth the progeny of one Adam is spread, and in the same way, as long as God wills, one will go on being born from another. Between parents and children there is even a kind of being part of one another; when even among them one has no special relation with another, what is there to say of other kinships, friendships and loves?

All the ties there are in the world, think of them simply as the greetings of passers-by on the road; and when you want to make these greetings into special relations, then, apart from the fact that your making cannot make a special relation, it plainly means that you are secretly interfering with God's arrangement. And the answer to such a person is the one God has given in the Quran: "*Whoever thinks that God will not help him in this world and the hereafter, let him stretch a rope to the sky, then cut it off, and see whether his scheme takes away what enrages him*": if anyone thinks that God will not help him in this world and the next, let him hang a rope from the sky, hang himself and die, and see whether what he disliked is removed by this device or not. Man is so made that he cannot live in comfort in the world alone. For a single moment of his he needs so many requisites that it takes an army for His Lordship's business to go on: farmers, masons, blacksmiths, carpenters, goldsmiths, grain dealers, grocers, merchants of every kind, craftsmen of every sort, tailors, cobblers, barbers, washermen, sweepers, water carriers, tinners, coppersmiths, and who else and who else: a whole world is busy in his service, and it means nothing to him. The officer comes to the court at the proper time with his staff, in case anyone has a complaint to make. Four or five times in a day and night the railway is got ready, in case one wants to go somewhere or send off some letter or goods. The telegraph babu sits waiting: "Let me get the signal and I will fetch news from anywhere in the world this moment." The printers are printing books: who knows which book one may ask for? The teachers are present in the school, so that the young master of high fortune may come and do his lessons. The posts are running, the roads are being made, gardens are laid out for recreation. In short, it is a wondrous chain, in which every living soul is servant and served, needy and needed, commander and commanded, ruler and ruled, hireling and hirer, everything. In such a state of things he is not a human being who has not many ties. However proud and touchy a man may be, he must perforce take help from others; and this tie of giving and seeking help is the root of connection and the source of special relations. We are under obligation to parents, to relations, to kin, to friends, to the teacher, to the ruler of the time, and to God knows how many. The greater someone's kindness, the greater the heart's pull and the mind's inclination towards him. If one considers it, although the whole world is contriving our ease and comfort, the key to it is in someone else's hand; otherwise, if He wills, the whole machine is useless and the whole frame [TN: reading uncertain] at a standstill.

If any benefit reaches us from anyone, it is He who puts the thought of benefiting into his heart and gives him the power to benefit. So, if you ask the truth, the real Contriver and Benefactor is He, and these outward helpers are the means and instruments of His kindness:

Qit'a (a verse fragment)

If harm comes to you from people, do not grieve,
For neither comfort nor pain comes from people.
Know that the hostility of enemy and friend is from God,
For the hearts of both are in His control.

Then some small and light favours He bestows on us through these outward helpers, and the great and mighty things He has kept in His own hand of power, in which no human being has any say, and without which our life is impossible. Come, let us count up His favours in His own words: "*It is God who created the heavens and the earth, and sent down water from the sky, and brought forth with it fruits as provision for you; and He has subjected to you the ships, to run upon the sea by His command; and He has subjected to you the rivers; and He has subjected to you the sun and the moon, constant in their courses; and He has subjected to you the night and the day; and He has given you of all that you asked of Him. And if you count the blessings of God, you cannot number them. Surely man is very unjust, very ungrateful*": it is God who created the heavens and the earth, and rained water from the heavens, then by it brought out grain and fruits for your provision, and gave you power over boats so that they move on the river by His command, and set the sun and moon to work in your service, so that both are busy at their tasks night and day, and made night and day for your service, and provided for you in fitting measure all that you needed; and if you would count God's blessings, you could not take them all in. There is no doubt that man is most cruel and ungrateful. If I set about commenting on this verse, it would take months to finish, if then; and I too have to complete the verse I began to expound. So in this place I want to say only this much: of the few favours God has counted up in this verse, each one is something on which our life depends, and which lies outside man's power. Sa'di has borrowed from this very verse:

Qit'a (a verse fragment)

Cloud and wind and moon and sun and sky are all at work,
So that you may get a loaf in your hand and not eat it heedlessly.
All of them, for your sake, are spinning and obedient;
It is not the way of justice that you should not obey.

So if connection and special relation rest on benefit and kindness, and so indeed they do, then our closest special relation ought to be with God; but God is not a thing to be seen with the eye. We have no habit of thinking and reflecting, and the result is that in our hearts we have as it were no connection with God: we neither acknowledge His rights, nor follow His commands, nor love Him, nor fear His anger. But His connection with us is so necessary a connection that it cannot be severed or separated in any way. Our very existence is the proof of His connection. We were not born of ourselves. Our father brought us up and reared us, but by His command, in that He put love of us into our parents' hearts; and even the words bringing up and rearing are applied to parents figuratively; in reality it is God who creates and brings up and rears. Hundreds and thousands of people long for children and have none; and a mother's milk dries up and she cannot make it flow. A child falls ill and no remedy comes to mind, and if one does, it is by His command and by His will. In short, we were neither born by our own will nor by anyone else's, nor did we grow up so far by our own will or by anyone else's. Whatever was done, God did; whatever is happening, is happening by His doing; and whatever will happen, will happen by His doing. No doubt the greatest love in the world is that of parents, and that too, I think, in a particular state and up to a particular time. But take of the loves of parents one of the highest degree, for instance for an only son, and compare it with the love of God, and you will find no likeness at all between the two loves. That is perhaps involuntary, and this is by choice. In that there is perhaps some self-interest too, and in this there is no trace of self-interest. That is a reflection and this the original; that is only in name and this beyond dispute. Parents, at the very most, are those who contrive, and God Most High is the one who provides the means. For the love of parents, such as it is and in whatever measure, how much obedience is due to them, that God Most High says: "*And your Lord has decreed that you worship none but Him, and that you be good to your parents. If one or both of them reach old age with you, do not say to them 'Uff', nor rebuke them, but speak to them a noble word; and lower to them the wing of humility out of mercy, and say: 'My Lord, have mercy on them, as they brought me up when I was small'*": O Prophet, your Lord commands that you worship none but God, and that you go on doing good to your parents. If one or both of them reach old age in your lifetime, take care not even to say "Hm" before them, nor to scold them, and speak to them only with honour and respect, and out of respect stay bowed before them, and pray for them: "O Lord, have mercy on them, as they brought me up when I was small."

Just think of the father's rank, that God has commanded doing good to parents along with His own worship. When for so small a right of parents there is this respect for them, then of God's rights there is no counting; what respect must not be due to Him? But man is so unmindful of what is due that, as for parents, some dutiful children, and they too rare, do acknowledge some of their rights; but as for God, most of God's servants do not know even this much, what He has done for them, that they should worship Him and thank Him. So when, at the time of calamity, the servant has said "*Surely we belong to God*," it means that he has acknowledged God's rights, and that the connection and special relation that God has with him, and the kindness and mercy that are bestowed on his state at all times, and the relation of lordship and being nurtured, of ownership and being owned, that subsists between God and him: all these things have come back to his mind. And then what? Man has no sooner turned that way than his grief is forgotten. After that other thoughts will certainly come to him. He will feel remorse at his own shortcoming, he will be made aware of his mistake: "Why, being mortal, did I form such a tie with a mortal thing, and why did I give it so much room in my heart? I paid regard to a right of the lowest degree and wasted the rights of the highest degree. I let the snake get away and went on beating the line it left. I acknowledged the cook's kindness and did not thank the host. I left the river and ran towards the mirage." Then he will reproach himself: "Can there be anyone more ungrateful than I, that all my life I ate His blessings, enjoyed myself and took my ease, of which I had not a grain's worth of right to anything; and now, when some little thing has happened against my liking, whose reason I do not know and whose purpose I am not aware of, I begin to pull faces and grumble. All that He gave before is wasted, all that He fed me is lost. I am not the owner of this workshop of the world, not its master, not the owner's counsellor, not his adviser; who am I to meddle in what is beyond me? What right have I to ask why and wherefore? Look at me, and look at my finding fault with God's Godhead! What am I, and what is my worth? What is my reason, and what is its reality?"

It stands to reason that before such thoughts grief and sorrow cannot remain; and it has also been found by experience that if some kind of loss befalls, or some calamity comes, and one recites "Surely we belong to God, and to Him we return" with a sincere heart, then, if God so wills, the loss is made good and the lost thing is found; and if not, the effect of grief and regret certainly grows less in the heart. "*And to Him we return*": what lovely words! In two words two meanings: a prayer, and a consolation too. What is prayer? Expressing a wish to God; and in "*to Him we return*" there is a wish too, but understood, that is, not stated in words. It is a sentence of the kind as when two travellers are going along the road, and one complains, "Brother, I am tired," and his companion comforts him: "Why are you worried? We shall soon reach the stage," meaning that on reaching the stage there will be rest. So it is here: "Why do you grieve? We are going back to God," meaning that He will give rest for our trouble. This is the prayer, and with conviction and acceptance too; and it points to this noble verse: "*O man, you are toiling towards your Lord with great toil, and you shall meet Him*": O man, have you understood what this life of the world is? A hard journey; and where must you go? Towards your Lord; and then you are to meet Him. From "*to Him we return*" this too follows, that all of us have this same journey before us. If one has reached the stage first, he has been delivered early from the toil of the journey; and since we too are going along behind him, "*and we, if God wills, shall join you*": God willing, one day we shall certainly join him, and that meeting will be such that after it there is no parting and no separation. "*Upon them are blessings from their Lord, and mercy*": now this is the full reward of the examination of patience. If God's greatness, His power, His glory, His majesty and His perfection are fixed in the mind, one knows what favour and what degree of exaltation these words signify. Today, if a king of this world writes of someone as "the object of Our favours", the man cannot contain himself for pride; and this "object of favours" has been written at the dictation of the Master of all being and space, the Creator of earth and heaven, the Provider of men and jinn, the Ruler whose command is "*Be, and it was*." Praise be to God, what honour and esteem the patient have at God's court! So, O brothers who are sitting in front, and O sisters behind the screen, mark this much: the ordering of the world, which has gone on in one way for thousands of years, will go on in the same way as long as God wills.

What is the worth of you and me? The greatest prophets of resolve, the mightiest kings, the most skilful physicians cannot draw a breath before death. So in a place of such helplessness, why lose your reward too by useless lamenting and wailing? Rather than in the end, worn out and beaten, perforce being patient anyway (for that patience is wasted), why not be patient at the very time of calamity and so enter among those patient ones whose ranks I have told you? May God grant every afflicted person the grace of beautiful patience. Amen. "*Glory be to your Lord, the Lord of might, above what they describe; and peace be upon the messengers; and praise be to God, the Lord of the worlds.*" (A matter of the sacred law.)
