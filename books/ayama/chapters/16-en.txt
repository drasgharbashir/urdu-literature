# Chapter 15: The Go-Between Eggs Azadi On

Azadi's thoughts had got this far when Azmuda came to see her again. Azadi herself asked her: "Tell me, lady, what news? I hope you have won over your Begam Sahib's son-in-law?"

Azmuda: Oh, lady, don't call him a son-in-law; it brings abuse on her for nothing. That fellow has not agreed so far, and I don't think he will agree either.

Azadi: But why? Is he some madman, to kick away the wealth that has come to his door? Or is he so rich at home that he does not care for wealth? To my knowledge there is no one so rich in Delhi now. Or is there some drawback of some kind in your girl? And if he won't agree, a fig for him [TN: literally "let shoes fall on him"]! Just give a hint to some match-maker, and by evening she will have heaped up a pile of proposals. It is a matter of a far-off place, with no acquaintance and no knowing, so people will hesitate a little; but whatever it is, before wealth nobody can see anything. Alas, alas, that I was not a man! Otherwise I would shut my eyes and go along with you. But would the Begam Sahib take any notice of me?

Azmuda: That's a fine one you've said, that she would not take any notice of you! You don't know the worth of your own beauty. People of your looks are not to be found one in a hundred thousand; in a thousand there are hardly one or two. My whole life has been spent among such people, and in Lucknow I saw a time when wherever you lifted your eyes there was a red tulip in bloom. I am not saying this to flatter you to your face; truly, God bless you, there are few with your grace and air. One very fine thing in you is that you will keep this same beauty and bearing into old age. Although you have had no children, you have borne such a terrible blow that the most beautiful of women, coming under that shock, would have had all her thirty-two teeth knocked out [TN: i.e. would have been utterly ruined]; but, the evil eye be far, there is not the least change anywhere in your firmness. You have such a jasmine-like [TN: reading uncertain] colouring that it will never fade.

Azadi: Oh dear, may fire take this beauty! Never mind if I had been pitch black, dumpy, shapeless, a dried-up stick, or a fat buffalo, a dwarf, a span high, anything at all, but a married woman. Now am I to lick this wretched face, or make an amulet of it and hang it round my neck, or what am I to do?

Azmuda: When God gives anyone wealth of any kind, they ought to spend it and give comfort to others. Beauty too is great wealth, which today has hundreds of seekers and thousands of suitors. If you will, people will fall on you like moths on a candle, and by the grace of this beauty you will sit like a ruby worth hundreds of thousands. But yes, if you want to mix yourself into the dirt and dust with your own hands, there is no remedy for that. Listen, my dear: one is not to stay in the world for ever and ever, nor to be born again and come back. If God Himself sends some calamity, as when some disease fastens on one, that is helplessness; otherwise my religion is this: live four days, take your ease, enjoy yourself and drink the wine of pleasure. If one must die, one must die this way or that.

Azadi: Repent, repent! What sort of ideas are these of yours? After death must one not show one's face to God?

Azmuda: Why, my dear, who has seen it? These are all [illegible] talk. I have seen fine *maulvis*; say the word and I'll have the veil lifted from any one of them. These days there are so many *maulvis* in our Bhopal that they rain down from the sky like locusts and come swarming out of the ground like ants. Their piety and purity are an elephant's tusks, for show only; and if some old greybeard, after licking and sucking his fill, turns into a weeping beggar at the end of his life, that is no proof. At that age every old procuress takes to the night vigil, to cheat God.

Azadi: I say, don't take the *maulvis*' deeds as proof at all, whether of their old age or of their youth. Good and evil do not come from anyone's doing or not doing, but from the command of God and the Prophet.

Azmuda: It was God who made the pleasures of the world too; and then, what is everyone's lot is our lot. And if God meant to keep us longing like this, what need was there to create us at all? We can't understand how God should throw so many of His servants into Hell and sit watching the show Himself. What kind of God is that? A wretched Halaku! We will have nothing of the Godhead of such a God.

Azadi: For God's sake don't utter such words of unbelief in front of me at least. I used to think that with so much stir about the *maulvis*, there must be great piety in Bhopal.

Azmuda: Didn't I tell you that the great piety is the piety of want: of those who have not a farthing to their name, no bread for their belly, no clothes for their body, or who sit with their feet hanging in the grave? If they are not pious, what are they to be? What is special about Bhopal? The poor, the paupers, the destitute, the needy, the old and the useless are pious everywhere, and there too. And for those who have their own means, every day is the Feast and every night the Night of Forgiveness:

The night passes with the heart's delight,
The morning, on rising, passes with the cup.

God knows what news there is of the hereafter;
For now, life passes in ease.

Azadi: So is it your opinion that I too should begin to live that kind of life?

Azmuda: Certainly. From the time I heard your name, love for you arose of itself in my heart. Now that I have come and seen you, your face is so dear to me that if you had been a man, you might do what you liked, but I would not let you go. If you have never heard of a woman in love with you, here I sit; look at me.

Azadi: What sort of hints and innuendoes are these you are making? Have you taken me for one of those shameless women of Lucknow or Bhopal?

Azmuda: As they say, at the mere name of love the she-donkey gave up eating the field; you have started getting cross already! My lady, my dear, the scent of musk and the glow of beauty cannot be hidden even behind seven veils. Sitting behind the curtain you know nothing, and I go about ferreting out just such things. I know of fifteen or twenty places where they are sitting ready to take poison over you; and mark my words now: however much you wish it, people will not let you stay a widow.

Azadi: Who would dare? If anyone so much as looks at me, I'll have his eyes put out. Well, let me hear the name of one or two.

Azmuda: One is Khwaja Mushtaq himself, whom you know very well; there is no need for me to tell you. He has been dying for you from the first day, and it was for this purpose that he kept up such a close friendship with your father; indeed your father had even given his word. But it was Maulvi Mustajab's luck; he carried you off over everyone's head, and this poor fellow was left gaping. His heart was so shattered that for a long time he wandered about like a madman from country to country and jungle to jungle; then he fell so ill that no one thought he would live. When he heard of your husband's death he came to life all over again. This is the very man for whom I came from Bhopal to Delhi. When I made inquiries, the whole truth came out. When I talked to him myself, I learnt that he lives in hope of you. If today he despairs of you, giving up his life is no hard thing for him at all. Him I count your true lover, for in his passion for you he did not even listen properly to the proposal from Bhopal. And apart from him you have many other ordinary admirers too, and their messages will be coming to you any morning or evening. So if you wish to sit in peace, it is not to be had; and what is there to wonder at in that? When a tree bears fruit, stones are bound to come at it. In the days when you were unmarried, proposals for a match must have been coming, as they come to everyone; it is a turn of words, but the thing is the same.

Azadi: Well then, let me think it over by myself, and then I will take counsel with you.

Azmuda: What counsel will you take with me? My counsel is the same one, today's for today and tomorrow's for tomorrow: that you and Mushtaq both come with me to Bhopal, and so both homes will be settled; but you are not one to agree. It is a very bad custom among you people that husband and wife stick to each other like glue. But I am sure that living among us, neither will this manner of yours remain nor such ideas. I had meant to take my leave from today, since I too am about to go now; but since you say so, well, two or four days more.

Even before meeting Azmuda, Azadi's own opinion too had been that "I cannot remain in the state of widowhood", and that was also the ruling of reason and of religion. So in one sense she had, as it were, already resolved on a second marriage. If she hesitated, it was only on this point: "Although it is lawful, by marrying a second time one loses all standing in people's eyes. Well, in the world the rich look with contempt on the poor, those with children on the childless, the educated on the unlettered, the handsome on the ugly. But what a calamity is this, that you marry a second time to keep your chastity safe, and by that very quality are counted inferior in people's eyes! People may be fools, they may be irreligious; but where is a person to go, leaving them? It is the same saying: among ten noseless men, the man with a nose is the noseless one [TN: a proverb]. And then if it were one day's matter for both, a person might bear it; but what a stigma this is, that the mother is blackened and the children are abused."
