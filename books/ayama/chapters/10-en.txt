# Chapter 9: Maulvi Mustajab Dies in Bhopal; Azadi's Widowhood

It was the tenth month since Mustajab had come when, from the beginning of Sha'ban, preparations for Ramazan began in the state. It became known that all the offices would stay closed throughout Ramazan, and that general leave would be given to all the servants from outside: whoever wished could go and spend Ramazan at home. A list of prisoners was drawn up for release, twenty or twenty-five to be set free every day at the hour of breaking the fast; and with those who remained over, on one side the alms of the Feast would be distributed, and on the other the captives freed. Hearing this, Mustajab's spirit revived: "Now at last God has delivered me." Then he had a doubt, and how could a companion of the Nawab Sahib not have at least this much doubt, that perhaps "servants" meant holders of posts, and he held no post. He thought of writing a treatise on the meaning of the word "servant" and laying it before the Nawab Sahib for his perusal, proving by rational arguments and transmitted traditions that holding a post is not a condition for the word "servant" to apply; but then it occurred to him: "Why take so much trouble? The real aim is release, and that can be got by having my name written in the list of prisoners too." Seeing Maulvi Mustajab's name in the list of prisoners, the Nawab Sahib would certainly have started and taken warning; but what use would that have been? His way of doing things was natural to him, and if it came from helplessness, it was in any case not to be helped. But it never came to that; the Nawab Sahib himself one day asked Mustajab: "Tell me, Maulvi Sahib, where do you mean to spend Ramazan?" It came into Mustajab's heart that, just as the Nawab Sahib had a habit of giving a vague answer to everything, he too should give some such ambiguous answer that his staying or going should not be settled at least until the first of Shawwal; but he was burning to get home, and he blurted out: "*Ramazan at home, with family and friends*."[1] Mustajab at once wrote home, and people, hearing that Mustajab was coming, did express their joy; but Azadi was as low-spirited and sad as ever. Indeed some lady or other, God knows who, congratulated her and said: "Ramazan is coming, and there is news that the master of the house is coming too; you should have had the house whitewashed."

Azadi said: "Yes, there is news; a letter came yesterday towards evening. He writes, 'I shall certainly, certainly come by the night of the new moon; leave has been granted, and I have got my luggage ready.' But I don't know what it is, my heart is not in it. I sat down with the bolster covers and floor-sheets to open them up and look them over, and my heart just sank of itself; I did not even get as far as opening the bundle. Look, there it lies, tied up just as it was." They were still talking when the postman called out at the entrance hall outside: "A telegram has come." At the word telegram Azadi's colour went white and her heart began to pound. When the maid brought the telegram and gave it to her, her hand was not under her control. There was an English-educated boy living next door; they quickly called him and gave it him to read. As long as he was looking at the telegram, Azadi stood staring fixedly at his face in a strange agitation. When it took a long time, Azadi asked: "Brother, for God's sake say something. It is in my name, isn't it? Whom is it from? What does it say?" The boy said: "It is in your name, and the Nawab Sahib of Bhopal has sent it. The Maulvi Sahib's name can be read inside too; more than that I cannot make out. If you like, I'll go and get it read." The boy went to have the telegram read, and here the lady who had come earlier and was sitting talking with Azadi said: "Why are you so frightened? The Nawab Sahib must have sent word of the Maulvi Sahib's departure." Azadi did not feel like talking; and how could she bring out with her own lips, all at once, something so shattering? And the thought kept coming: "Ever since he set out, nothing but evil thoughts have kept coming into my heart. God knows what has happened to my heart; can this too be some kind of madness? Who knows, perhaps women who love their husbands are in just this state when separated from them. As for me, what great love did I have? Only this much, that he could not bear my being unhappy in any circumstances. If that is what is called love, it was on his side; on my side nothing of love was ever shown." In short, Azadi was forced to say just this to the lady: "Come, lady, do you take me for a little child? A telegram of departure, and from the Nawab Sahib? What would that mean? God knows what has come about that he could not even send a telegram himself."

Just then the boy's mother came with the telegram. She had come from home intending to tell her it was illness, as a pretext, and to send word of the death to her parents' house; but when her eyes fell on Azadi, well, she could not help it: she threw her arms round Azadi and began to weep. Then what was left? Azadi had been sitting choked up and full for ten months. She gave one scream, then fainted and fell from the bed so awkwardly that if her scarf had not caught on the bedpost, there was the floor, of brick, and her head could not have escaped being smashed to pieces. A young man's death, a sudden death, and far from home: a kind of uproar arose through the whole city, and a multitude flocked in to condole, so that there was no room to set down a sesame seed anywhere in the neighbourhood. Azadi's case, of course, was apart; she had a special tie with Mustajab, such that they were as it were one soul in two bodies, and because of this tie Azadi's heart had had some sort of foreknowledge of this calamity long before. When Maulvi Mustajab was taking leave from home, Azadi had spoken at that very time in a despairing way: "Who knows, parting now, whether we shall ever meet again in this world?" Then from the day Maulvi Mustajab left, not one day, not one moment of Azadi's passed in happiness: always sorrowful, always anxious; and the grief too was not of the kind that comes from being parted from loved ones, but much more than that, far greater. Then now at last the telegram came, and before it was even read her state changed of itself. In short, Azadi was fully convinced that the Maulvi Sahib, who kept saying he was coming home, had gone to his true home; but all the other people, their own relations, acquaintances, people they knew and strangers, raised all sorts of doubts about the telegram. One said: "It must certainly have been misread; a letter came only yesterday evening, and there is not even a hint of illness in it." Another said: "Well, there is nothing strange in falling ill suddenly; but news of the illness should certainly have come." A third: "There are plenty of men in the family; granted the house was separate, still the telegram should have come in some man's name." A fourth: "Why, sir, I can't believe it; it must certainly be some enemy's mischief." And although the Maulvi Sahib was not the kind of man to have any enemy, how long does it take to make enemies in Indian states? A man who speaks the truth, minds his work, is honest, and does not flatter falsely, has a thousand enemies: earth and sky are his enemies, door and wall are his enemies; and then the greatest cause of enmity was his closeness to the Nawab Sahib.

Seeing him share cup and morsel with the Nawab Sahib, God knows how many must have swallowed draughts like poison and held their peace. In short, that telegram went to the college Principal, it went to the schoolmasters, it went to the padre, and all read what was in it: that Maulvi Mustajab had died suddenly of heart disease today at two in the afternoon, and full particulars would be learnt from a letter to follow. Some had also advised that a reply-paid telegram be sent to confirm the news, but that idea was dropped again. But a friend of the Maulvi Sahib had, as soon as he fell ill, written a short letter and posted it, saying that at the Friday prayer the Maulvi Sahib was overcome with emotion, as often happened to him, and suddenly fell unconscious, and his soul departed: "Surely we belong to God, and to Him we return." On the third day after the telegram the Nawab Sahib wrote the same account in his own hand, and expressed great sorrow. And yes, that letter also said that the Maulvi Sahib's widow would receive thirty-two rupees a month until she married again. From the moment Azadi fainted, her whole body had gone cold as ice, and her teeth were clenched so tight that nothing could go down her throat. When the faint had lasted a long while, the *hakim* (physician) felt her pulse and said: "From grief the innate heat has been congested in the pericardium; it is a complete swoon, and there is danger and there is not. Let means be tried to bring her round; delay is dangerous." They made her smell a restorative, sprinkled rose water, waved fans of scented grass, bound her calves, rubbed her knees, palms and soles; but Azadi lay stiff like a piece of wood, and lay the whole night in the same way, with not the least relief and no consciousness at all. And how could they weep for the young man who had died far away, when here before their eyes they were in fear for this young woman's life? At last, when the cool of the morning came, Azadi sneezed, and opening her eyes found herself in her mother's lap. Now for fear of her nobody could let a sound out of their mouth; all sat holding their breath. Azadi did come to, but it was a strange kind of consciousness. She had been lying down; she sat up. She does not speak or say anything; she neither weeps herself nor lets others weep; like a statue, dumb and still, she goes on staring fixedly in one direction, neither blinking an eyelid nor telling anyone how she is. At last her mother said: "Daughter, you have not said the night prayer either, and now the time of the morning prayer is running short. Get up, do the ablution, say your prayer. There are many people down here; I have left a prayer-mat spread on the roof room; go up there."

Azadi went up, did the ablution, said the *sunnat* prayers, made her intention for the obligatory ones, and in the first *rak'at*, after the *Alhamd* [TN: the Fatiha], recited that passage of the Surah of Joseph where Joseph's brothers reproach their father that if he keeps remembering Joseph so much he will one day destroy himself, and Jacob answers his sons: "*I complain of my distress and my grief only to God*": I lay the plea of my distress only at God's court. The moment those words left her lips, it was as if the stopper of a fountain had been pulled out. The jet of tears flowed with such force that it seemed her whole body would turn to water and flow away by way of her eyes; and the cries burst out so violently that her breath could not stay in her body and her heart seemed to be coming up into her mouth. She meant to say one thing and something else came out. Ask the *maulvis* and they will say plainly that the prayer did not count; and we say that in all her life Azadi said just this one prayer. O God, shall we too ever be granted to say even half or a quarter of a *rak'at* with such sincerity and such presence of mind? Upstairs Azadi was praying, and downstairs a sound came like that of a railway boiler[2]. Hadi Begam had sent her up to pray for this very reason, so that in solitude she might weep her heart out and get out the pent-up pain inside her; otherwise, choking and choking, her breath would stop. Indeed, while this was going on, one or two women did make to go up, and Hadi Begam stopped them. When two full hours had passed, Hadi Begam went up herself and saw her daughter lying in prostration, weeping. She raised her up and pressed her to her breast, and fanned the burning fire higher still. As soon as the mother took the daughter in her arms, the daughter was like a pigeon whose throat has been cut and which is left half-slaughtered, writhing. The mother was terrified to see it. No doubt the great calamity had fallen on Azadi, but the mother's grief was no less. She wept for the dead, and the mother for the living; she mourned the departed, and the mother grieved for the wounded; she lamented a husband, and the mother sorrowed for the piece of her own heart. She could not bear to see the girl's distress, and no means of comfort came to mind. Her heart would not let her stay away, and her sitting near gave her daughter more pain; no one knew that secret. Having no choice, she made her heart a stone and her breast steel and stayed clinging to her daughter; otherwise, God knows what the girl would have done to herself, beating her head against the walls. Hadi Begam did a very wise thing in not leaving her daughter for a moment for three or four days and nights together; she neither went near the guests to receive condolences herself, nor let any of them come to her.

What a bad custom it is, that they call it condolence, which means giving comfort, and in place of comfort they bring to mind the good qualities of the dead, that is, instead of ointment they rub salt and pepper into the wound. Mustajab had been struck down by God, and Azadi was being struck down by these foolish condolers. The wretches have no sense of time and place. Coming to a house of death, and changing their clothes and dressing themselves up! We used to hear that women are very tender-hearted, but seeing Azadi's guests our idea was completely changed. Except that there was no dancing by the *domni* singing women, no difference was to be seen by which one could tell that this was a gathering of mourning and not of a wedding. The same carpets, the same yellow rice and betel, the same fittings, the same guests, the same show, the same pomp and pride, the same tales, the same stories. The news of a death arrived, and they began rummaging through their bundles of clothes, sent for the jewel box, searched out from the trunks the shoes worked with leafy borders or with spangles, dressed and adorned themselves, got into the palanquin and set off. All the way they kept reckoning in their minds: "I have had this meal cooked before coming; if I see it getting late there for the evening, I'll send money or send word to have it bought from the bazaar." In short, if on arriving there they found they had time to spare, then having changed their clothes in great haste, they put them right sitting in the palanquin and saw to their jewellery. Just then the bearers called out: "The lady may get down." The bearers turned their faces away and stood, the lady got down with great ceremony, and all the way to the entrance hall kept turning to look back at the palanquin to see that nothing had been left behind. The bearers are outside and the lady inside the entrance hall, and there is an argument about the fare; she threatened them, bullied them, cursed them, but would bearers ever budge? In the end she gave what they asked, but in a temper: "Well, I have come to a house of mourning, so I can say nothing now; but just see whether I don't drive your stand out of the neighbourhood!" The moment she set foot inside the house she began peering about to see where the mourners receiving condolences were, and reached them by stepping over the others. Up to that moment there is no grief and no sorrow, no care for anyone's pain and no thought of the calamity; but God knows what kind of art it is, that she has hardly sat down before she lets seers of water pour from her eyes like this. I have seen old men of a hundred die, poor souls who had had their fill of life and were braver than anyone about dying, and who left, God be praised, a family of sons, grandsons, great-grandsons, daughters' sons and their sons, so large that they were second Father Adams in themselves; yet when the women set about weeping, they raised a whole mourning over their deaths too.

And here the account of the calamity was itself so terrible that one's heart trembles to tell it, lest the house should split open. If there had been wailing laments over Mustajab's death, it is a thing one could swear to that Azadi, young as she was, would not have survived, and it would have been no wonder if she had taken two or four others with her in dying. But it was very fortunate that at the right moment Hadi Begam foresaw this outcome and did not let such a situation arise. Still, the near relations could not be forbidden, and their grief was not pretence and show. Wailing laments are forbidden in the sacred law, and the kind of laments that are made certainly deserve to be forbidden. The Lawgiver's purpose is that if some calamity falls on us, it does not befit servanthood that we should be angry with God. The highest degree is that we should be grateful to Him even in calamity; but if a person cannot do so much, being impatient and weak in faith, then, far from bringing a word of complaint to his lips, even the scent of complaint coming into his heart is close to unbelief. And in truth, when, God forbid, the servant has thought God unjust, unfair and pitiless, what difference is left between him and an unbeliever? "Alas, I am robbed! O God, You had not even this much pity! What great luck had I found in Your Godhead that today this mountain has fallen on me? O people, even God does not hear me!" These and such laments make a person an apostate and an unbeliever. Weeping as such, and the bare expression of one's distress, are neither forbidden in the sacred law nor possible for a person to hold back. At Azadi's, how could we say that there were no laments? There were, but few, and not against the sacred law; and even so the *maulvis* heard the voices outside and at once came to the door to forbid them. The laments in the *maulvis*' house were these:

Azadi (softly): O God, what shall I do? As you were leaving for Bhopal you said, as you went, "I shall send for you"; why do you not send for me now? You never told a lie; you kept in mind to come before the night of the new moon. What fault did I commit that I may not even come to your grave? Alas, had I known this, I would not have fallen short in serving you.

Hadi Begam: Surely we belong to God, and to Him we return. Did you take her (Azadi's) hand for this day, to leave her in midstream and go? You could not bear to see even a speck of dust in her eye, and now she lies writhing like this and you do not care. Alas, with what ears shall I hear people call Azadi a widow? Alas, there was not even a child born, to comfort her heart.

Mustajab's mother: He went without even asking pardon for my milk. Such a passion for service took hold of him that the child lost his life. Once you asked my leave to go on the pilgrimage; why did you not ask my leave now? You did not leave any keepsake of yourself before my eyes either. While you were there I had my way with my daughter-in-law in everything; now on what strength shall I keep my daughter-in-law with me? For his father's obstinacy he gave up preaching, and then he gave up his country, and in the end he gave up the world.

In the same way each person lamented according to his connection. What were they weeping for Mustajab? They were wearing themselves out over their own interests. But alas, nothing in this wretched world has permanence, no state endures, no condition stays. And how could it? Grief and joy too are two states that come upon a person; when man himself is exposed to passing away, his states are so all the more. Grief or joy, it is a gust like the breeze of the rains: when it comes, a breath or two, and then the same sultriness again. Mustajab's death was something unexpected for him and his kin, and all deaths in their own way are more or less unexpected. Show me the trace of one human being on the face of the earth who ever died and whom people sent off from the world with a glad heart. This very weeping and beating of breasts is the proof that death is unexpected; but these are all our misconceptions. We do not like our own death or the death of anyone dear to us, and though we cannot put death off, we do not fall short on our part in putting it off. So death is not unexpected; rather we have forcibly made it unexpected in our own minds, and that is the cause of our grief, for grief is the very name of the coming about of some state contrary to expectation. But this grief, besides being out of place and fruitless, is also unreliable: however severe the grief may be, as the days pass its bitterness keeps lessening, until it would be no wonder if a day came when even the person who was perhaps ready to kill himself over that grief would no longer give it more weight than a tale; like a wound, where there is burning, pain, restlessness, agony; when it has healed, only a scar remains, and even that fades and grows fainter day by day. Mustajab's death was not different from this general rule nor an exception to this law.

The guests from far away took their leave after the third-day rites, and in the same way, slowly, by ones and twos, they kept slipping away; now there remained Azadi and her mother Hadi, who clung to her like her shadow and was with her like her twin spirit. What a bad thing grief is! In those few days Azadi had come to look like someone ill for years: not a trace of blood in her body, her colour yellow as turmeric when you looked, rings under her eyes, difficulty in sitting, trouble in rising, her face fallen, her look sad; all the time a sort of drowsiness keeps coming over her, but sleep has fled so far that the whole night passes sitting up and turning from side to side. At her mother's insistence she takes a morsel in her mouth and tries to swallow, and it comes back up her throat. Someone made up a betel roll and gave it her; she put it in her cheek, but does not feel like moving her mouth. God knows in what thought she is drowned, that people sitting by her are talking and she does not even know. Her mother lifts her up and sets her upright, and she keeps falling over. Whenever you look, silent; whenever you glance, quiet; every moment a cold sigh; again and again "Alas, alas." Hadi Begam kept growing more and more frightened looking at her daughter: "O God, what shall I do and what not do? If she were not in her *iddat* (the widow's waiting period), I would take her about in the gardens, show her the sights of Qutb Sahib, and perhaps her heart would be diverted and her spirits recover. And if she stays in this state for some time, she will waste away like this and be finished one day." At last it came into her mind: "I will have her listen to the *maulvis*' sermons. That is the only remedy. If it misses, it is only a stick; if it hits, it is an arrow." God be praised, there is no shortage of *maulvis* in the city of Delhi: be content to pay eight annas, or at most a rupee, and send for whatever *maulvi* you like and have whatever sermon you like preached. And Hadi Begam had plenty of *maulvis* in her own family. Their rule [TN: of taking a fee] was being broken, but out of regard for kinship, and besides that for the sake of their reputation, the *maulvis* rained down sermons. And the truth is that these sermons did Azadi a great deal of good, because they were given without return and without fee. The medicine must be right, whoever the physician may be and whatever he may be like. There were many sermons, but two or three of them were truly very moving, such as the first, by Azadi's grandfather, Maulvi Muqtada.
