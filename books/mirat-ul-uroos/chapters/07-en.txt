When Asghari received her father's letter, a surge of love stirred her heart strangely, and she longed to weep in spite of herself. But she was newly married, and weeping in her husband's home would be thought improper; so she controlled herself, pressed her father's letter to her eyes and put it away very carefully in her prayer book. Every day without fail she read the letter and pondered its meaning.

While Asghari was a new bride she felt very restless, because she had suddenly had to leave her mother's house and live in a new house among new people. She was used to work and management, and could not rest for a moment without something to do; now, for the first time, she had to sit silent for months in a closed room. The freedom she had had in her parents' house was gone. Here, in her husband's home, as soon as she arrived people began to watch and scrutinise everything about her. One looks at her face, one measures the length of her plait, one eyes her height and figure, one fingers her jewellery, one appraises her clothes. When she eats, they watch every mouthful: how big a morsel she took, how wide she opened her mouth, how she chewed and how she swallowed. When she gets up, they see how she put on her *dupatta* and how she held up the legs of her trousers. When she sleeps, they note the time: when did she go to sleep, when did she get up? In short, her every movement and every pause was watched.

In such a situation Asghari suffered a great deal, but since she was sensible and well brought up, she came through this hard test perfectly, and her husband's family liked all her ways. When she talked, it was neither so much that people would say, "What a girl! Married four days, and what a terrible chatter she keeps up!", nor so little that they would think her bad-tempered and sulky. When she ate, it was neither so much that the neighbourhood would talk of it, nor so little that her mother-in-law and sisters-in-law would sit wearying themselves pressing her while she paid no heed. When she slept, it was neither so early that people would say, "The wick is in the lamp, and my darling is off to bed", nor so late that it was as if she had made a wager with the dead to see who could sleep longer.

It is the custom for the girls of the neighbourhood to crowd round a new bride. With Asghari too, whenever you looked, there were five or ten of them; but Asghari became particularly friendly with none of them. If a girl sat with her all day, she did not say, "My dear, go home"; if one did not come, she did not ask her, "My dear, where were you? Why didn't you come?"

Because of this way Asghari had of meeting them and treating them, the crowd of girls gradually grew smaller. The girls of the low-born people of the neighbourhood especially are only friends for the sake of titbits; when they saw that there were neither endless *pans* to be had nor any talk of shopping errands, within six or seven days they dispersed and left her, like wind from the belly.

Asghari first made friends with Mahmuda, her husband's sister. Mahmuda was only a child, after all, and was won over by a little attention. All day she would be clinging to Asghari, so that her mother would sometimes even exclaim, "Why are you so fond of this sister-in-law? You used to run from your elder sister-in-law's very shadow." Mahmuda would answer, "She used to hit me; my younger sister-in-law loves me."

Through her friendship with Mahmuda, Asghari got a great deal done. First, by asking Mahmuda questions, she learned all about the household, and indeed about the whole family and neighbourhood; and whatever she could not say herself at first, out of shyness and modesty, she would say through Mahmuda. Little by little Asghari began to take a hand in the housework in this way: in the evening she would get cotton from Mahmuda and twist the wicks for the lamps; she would prepare the vegetables; she would mend Mahmuda's torn and unstitched clothes; she would make *pan* for her mother-in-law and her husband. By degrees she began to go as far as the kitchen and to advise Mama Azmat about frying and seasoning, until the food came to be cooked as Asghari advised. From the time Asghari began to take a hand in the cooking, the family discovered that food could be a wonderful blessing. In the end it came to this: on any day when for some reason Asghari did not advise Mama Azmat, the dishes went round untasted.

Quarrels between mother-in-law and daughter-in-law are a commonplace thing; Asghari was not one to quarrel in that way, so it was her skill that caused the trouble. Mama Azmat was so much in charge in this house that everything depended on her. Groceries, cloth, grain, whatever came from the bazaar, all came through Mama Azmat's hands. Mama Azmat even had the jewellery made and brought it home; when a loan was needed, that too was raised through Mama Azmat. In short, Mama Azmat managed the house like a man.

From the time Asghari began to take a hand in the cooking, Mama Azmat's embezzlement began to come to light. One day kebabs of choice cuts were being cooked, and Asghari was sitting in the kitchen giving Mama Azmat directions. When the meat had been pounded and was ready, and the time came to mix in the curd and spices, Asghari said to the maid, "Let me taste the curd; if it is sour and stale, the kebabs will be spoilt." The maid took out the leaf-cup of curd and put it in Asghari's hand. Asghari tasted it: it was sour as could be and several days old, the bluish whey in one place and the lumps of curd in another. Asghari said, "Oh dear! What nasty curd! It is not fit to put in kebabs at all. Mama, go quickly and pick out two pice worth of good, fresh, sweet curd."

The maid said, "Oh, madam, what would two pice worth of curd do in kebabs of a whole *ser* of meat? A caraway seed in a camel's mouth! This curd you don't like cost an anna."

Asghari was amazed to hear it and said, "At home we were always making kebabs, and we always put a pice and a half worth of curd to a *ser* of meat. By that reckoning, I thought I was ordering more than enough with two pice, so that the kebabs would be nice and soft and red."

The maid said, "Madam, never mind the accounts of your own neighbourhood. Where is Chandni Chowk and where is the Turkman Gate? What costs a pice in Chandni Chowk can't be had here for an anna. This wretched neighbourhood is 'a ruined town in a desolate land'; there is always a shortage of everything, a famine of everything."

Since the meal was getting late, Asghari said nothing to this and told the maid, "Well, get it quickly, whatever it costs." But Asghari was not so simple as to accept what the maid said. She said to herself, "There is certainly something black in the lentils. A difference of a farthing or two wouldn't matter; but it's outrageous to have a difference of double and four times over between two quarters of the same city!"

From then on Asghari was on the watch. The next day the maid brought *pan*. When Asghari saw it she said, "Mama, you bring nothing but green leaves; there is no flavour and no taste in them. Now that the cold weather is coming, look out crisp, well-matured leaves and bring those."

The maid said, "Matured leaves cost a pice for two, and here, God keep us, we use half a bundle a day; that's why I bring new leaves." Just then Asghari's own maid from her mother's house, Kifayat-un-Nisa, turned up to ask after her. Since *pan* was the subject of the moment, Asghari asked her own maid, "Tell me, Kifayat-un-Nisa, how much *pan* do you get for your money these days?"

Kifayat-un-Nisa said, "Madam, sixteen for a pice."

Asghari opened her little box, put two pice in Kifayat-un-Nisa's hand and said, "Get some *pan* from the *pan*-seller in this neighbourhood." Kifayat-un-Nisa brought back forty big, crisp, thick-veined leaves.

Asghari said, "That's four more leaves for a pice than in Chandni Chowk!" Kifayat-un-Nisa said, "Madam, this quarter is the gateway of the city; whatever comes into the city comes in through this gate. Meat, grain and *pan* are cheap in this quarter. Green vegetables, it's true, go straight from the vegetable market into the city through the Kabuli Gate, so they will be a little dearer. I got forty matured leaves; if I had bought new ones I would have got sixty."

Asghari said, "That wretched Mama Azmat sets fire to the price of everything like this. Kifayat-un-Nisa, stay here for a few days; I'll send word to Mother. Anyone can look after the work there for a few days."

Kifayat-un-Nisa said, "Madam, I am at your service. God forbid, are this house and that one two separate houses?"

So for four days all the shopping from the bazaar came through Kifayat-un-Nisa's hands, and in every single thing Mama Azmat's embezzlement was proved. But it was all done in such a way that Asghari's mother-in-law never even heard of it; only Asghari, Kifayat-un-Nisa and Mama Azmat knew. For Asghari was a woman of great kindness and consideration, and she thought, "What is the use of disgracing and shaming this old maidservant?"

At night, when supper was over, Asghari was eating *pan* on the roof, and Kifayat-un-Nisa was sitting beside her, when Mama Azmat came up. Kifayat-un-Nisa said, "Well, Bua Azmat, what is all this? What servant doesn't steal? Look, here is the lady of my own house; I served her for seven years without a break. She ran the whole business of the house; God keep it, a rich house and rich spending; thousands of rupees worth of goods came through these hands of mine. As for my customary commission, how can I say I didn't take it? That much is a servant's right and duty, whether God forgives it or punishes it; but more than that cannot be swallowed. Going further than that counts as betraying the salt you have eaten."

Azmat said, "My dear, who doesn't know my ways? I don't care who knows! Yes, I do steal and plunder; and not just from today, it's always been my way. But just look at my position too, at the terrible amount of work in this house. Inside and out I am the only servant; the work of four servants falls on my one pair of hands. And then, my dear, nobody grinds her bones like this for nothing. The mistress has dismissed me several times, but in the end it was me she sent for again. It's all a matter of how you look at it; one sees it this way, another that. I am one woman instead of four, so I ought to get four servants' wages." And the truth about this Mama Azmat was this: she had been in that house for twenty-five years and had always been out to plunder it. If it had happened only once it might have been hushed up, but her frauds came to light day after day. Several times she was turned out; but whenever she was dismissed, the grain-dealers, cloth-merchants, goldsmiths, butchers and greengrocers from whom loans had been raised through her came demanding payment, and for fear of this she was sent for again. So thieving and brazenness were written in Mama Azmat's fate. She took openly and stole openly; she showed what she was taking as she took it, and denied what she had had written down. The household income was small and its habits were spoilt: choice food, elegant clothes, the whole concern ran on credit, and the wheel of credit turned by Mama Azmat's grace. She used to say quite openly, "Getting rid of me is no easy matter; I'll go only when I've had this house sold up; I'll bring it down brick by brick before I leave." When Asghari began to check the accounts, Mama Azmat became her mortal enemy and set about making trouble for her with Muhammad Kamil and his mother. But Asghari knew nothing of this intention; indeed, when she saw that the maid had complete control of the house, and would neither mend her ways nor leave, she said to herself, "Then what is the use of useless squabbling? Why should I fall out with the maid for nothing?" She stopped going to the kitchen and taking a hand in the cooking altogether.

But the family had acquired a taste for Asghari's cooking, and from the very first meal they began to pull faces. One woman would say, "Oh dear! The meat is all gristle in one's mouth"; one man would say, "The salt in the lentils is poison; you can't put them on your tongue." But who could ask Asghari to do the cooking? They had no choice but to eat the meals Mama Azmat cooked and set before them, good or bad.
