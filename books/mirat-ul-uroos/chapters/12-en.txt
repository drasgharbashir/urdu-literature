When everything had found its routine and the household was in order, Asghari turned her attention to other matters. Muhammad Kamil did read and write, but in the careless, half-hearted way in which boys who are free and their own masters do. His father lived away from home; Muhammad Aqil, though the elder brother, was only two and a half years older, and had little authority over Muhammad Kamil. He read his lessons morning and evening, and also played *ganjifa* (a card game), chess and *chausar* (a dice board game) with boys of his own age. Sometimes, when he was absorbed in a game, he came home a whole watch or more into the night. Asghari knew all this, but she was looking for an opportunity to speak of it in a way that would not give offence.

One day Muhammad Kamil came home very late at night, and he was in good spirits (perhaps he had won his game). The moment he arrived he asked for his meal. Diyanat-un-Nisa ran to warm the curry. Muhammad Kamil thought it was still cooking, and asked, "Maid, hasn't your pot come off the fire yet?"

Asghari said, "It has come off and gone back on several times already. You eat at such odd hours that the food goes cold and turns to dust. Either arrange to eat early, or have your meal sent out to you. Meanwhile Mother is put to trouble every day waiting for you."

Muhammad Kamil said, "What! You all wait for me? I thought you must be eating."

Asghari said, "Why should the women sit down to eat while the men are still to come?"

Muhammad Kamil said, "If it were a matter of two or four days, it could pass; but what is the point of being stubborn about it? You all go ahead and eat."

At the time Asghari said nothing more. Up on the roof, Muhammad Kamil himself raised the subject again. Asghari said, "How strange! You cannot go against your own routine, yet you want us to go against ours. You come home early."

Muhammad Kamil said, "After a meal I don't feel like going out, and I am slow to fall asleep; lying about at home with nothing to do makes me restless. So I come late on purpose, so that I can go to sleep after eating."

Asghari said, "Occupation is in your own hands. If one orders one's time, there are a thousand things to do. Is reading so small an occupation? I used to see my elder brother poring over his books till midnight, and on the days he happened to fall asleep early he would be very sorry for it. You put too little effort into your studies; that is why idleness makes you restless."

Muhammad Kamil said, "What more effort should I make? I read my lessons morning and evening and learn them."

Asghari said, "I don't know what sort of reading you do. On the day Azmat's accounts were gone into, Father asked you to do sums and you could not; I felt ashamed."

Muhammad Kamil said, "Arithmetic is a different art; I study Arabic. What has that to do with arithmetic?"

Asghari said, "Reading and writing are learned so that no worldly business should be held up. My elder brother has read a great deal of Arabic and Persian, but he cannot find employment. Father always says, 'Until you learn accounts and the work of the courts, don't think of employment.' Now Mal Andesh studies at the school, and he is cleverer at accounts and writing than my elder brother; Father is very pleased with him and says, 'Study two more years at the school, and then I'll find you a post somewhere.'"

Muhammad Kamil said, "They only take young boys into the school; I'm too old."

Asghari said, "Why does it depend on going to the school? Aren't there teachers enough in the city? Spend on this the very time you waste on games."

Muhammad Kamil said, "Do I play games day and night? Now and then I sit down for an hour or so."

Asghari said, "Playing is a habit like opium: it starts small and keeps growing until it becomes an addiction, and then it is hard to give up. In the first place these games are sinful; in the second, they keep a man from acquiring other accomplishments. Men of business never play; it is the idle who pass their days like this. In these games, just as winning delights the heart, losing grieves it very much; and just as the delight has no substance, the grief too is for nothing; and very often, in the middle of a game, a pointless quarrel breaks out. If you take my advice, give these games up entirely. People say nothing to your face, but they laugh behind your back. Only the day before yesterday a man came to fetch you; the maid answered from inside that you had gone out; and that man said to his companion, 'Come along to Master Husaini's house; we'll find him there in the crowd at the chessboard.' Father has a great name in the city and people hold him in esteem; going to such places brings one a bad name. And I heard Father grieving: 'Alas for my fate! Of two sons, not one has turned out such that the sight of him gladdens my heart. Aqil was given some schooling, and now he is so taken up with chasing his post that he has forgotten what he learned; and the younger gentleman has no leisure from play and sport.' Indeed, someone even told my own father about it; he asked me, and I said, 'It's all lies; if there were anything in it, I should certainly have known.'"

Asghari's advice had an excellent effect on Muhammad Kamil. He gave up playing altogether, began to work harder at his Arabic than before, and also began learning accounts and the like from a teacher outside the school. God has put great blessing in time: by spending it in an orderly way, within a few days Muhammad Kamil's grounding in Arabic became sound, and he had worked through several books of arithmetic and mathematics as well.

While Muhammad Kamil was busy in this way, Asghari, during the same time, set up another enterprise. In that neighbourhood lived Hakim Ruhullah Khan, a man of great name and fame. The Hakim Sahib himself was *diwan* (chief minister) at the court of the Maharaja of Patiala, but his home and family, his sons and children, were all in this neighbourhood. House, apartments, servants and retainers: it was a great establishment, and the family was counted among the high houses of the city, with marriage ties in high places and dealings with high people. The Hakim Sahib's younger brother, Fathullah Khan, had for a long time been chief agent at the court of the ruler of Indore; and when Munshi Ami Jan gained great influence at that court, he judged it wise to withdraw. But there were lakhs of rupees in the house and he cared nothing for employment. He had bought property in the city worth many thousands of rupees, and hundreds of rupees came in every month in rent. He lived in great style: a guard of soldiers at the gate, thirty or forty servants inside and out, and a horse, an elephant, a palanquin and a carriage ready for riding.

Fathullah Khan had two daughters, Jamal-ara and Husn-ara. Jamal-ara had been married to the son of Nawab Isfandyar Khan, but there was such discord that in the end all ties were broken off. There had been, God forbid, no divorce, but no connection of any kind remained either; even the things of her dowry had come back. Husn-ara was betrothed into the family of the Nawab of Jhajjar. These girls' aunt, Shah Zamani Begum, lived in the neighbourhood where Asghari's parents lived. In that neighbourhood Asghari's abilities were the talk of everyone, and Shah Zamani Begum knew all about Asghari; she had seen her several times at weddings. Shah Zamani Begum came to visit her younger sister, Husn-ara's mother.

It is the way of the world that no human being is free of sorrow; and this is ordained by God, for if there were happiness on every side, man would forget God and never remember Him, nor count himself His servant. Shah Zamani's younger sister, Sultana Begum, had every pleasure of the world at her command, but she was always grieved on account of her daughters. On one side, Jamal-ara, after all the wedding and the procession, was sitting at home; on the other, Husn-ara's temper had turned out so badly that she was at odds with everyone in the house: no regard for her mother, no respect for her elder sister, no fear of her father. The servants complained on their own account; the maidservants begged for mercy on theirs. In short, Husn-ara kept the whole house in uproar. With Shah Zamani Begum's arrival one would have expected Husn-ara, out of regard for her elder aunt, to sit quiet for an hour or two; not a bit of it! Shah Zamani Begum had hardly stepped down from her palanquin when two or three complaints came in one after another. Nargis came crying, "Begum Sahib, look, the younger young lady has torn my new scarf to shreds." Sosan cried out, "Begum Sahib, the younger young lady pinched my cheek." Gulab wailed, "My ear is all bloody." The nurse shouted, "Look, she struck my wretched girl so hard with a stick that there's a weal on her arm." From the kitchen the cook cried out, "Look, she is throwing fistfuls of ashes into the curry pots." Shah Zamani Begum called, "Husna! Come here." Recognising her aunt's voice, Husn-ara did at last come: no greeting, no salaam, ashes on her hands, mud on her feet, and in that state she ran and flung her arms round her aunt.

Her aunt said, "Husna, you have become very naughty." Husn-ara said, "That witch Nargis must have complained." With that she slipped out of her aunt's lap, sprang at Nargis and tore at her hair. Her aunt kept crying, "What! What!", but she would not listen.

Shah Zamani Begum turned to her sister and said, "Sultana dear, for God's sake engage some *ustani* (woman teacher) for this girl."

Sultana Begum said, "Sister, what can I do? I've been looking for a teacher for months, and I can't find one anywhere."

Shah Zamani Begum said, "Oh my dear, you're like the proverb: 'Crying it through the city, with the boy on your hip.' In your very own neighbourhood, Maulvi Muhammad Fazil's younger daughter-in-law is a teacher worth a hundred thousand teachers."

Sultana said, "Till today I had no idea; look, I'll send someone at once." With that she called the housekeeper of her household: "Maniji, some Maulvi Sahib lives in this neighbourhood; Sister says his younger daughter-in-law is very well educated. See whether she will take service as a teacher, and if so, bring her here. Food, clothing and ten rupees a month, with money for *paan* and tobacco, we are ready to give; and when the girl finishes the first *sipara* (one of the thirty parts of the Qur'an) and learns manners and good behaviour, we will reward the teacher over and above her salary."

Maniji came to the Maulvi Sahib's house. She exchanged greetings with Muhammad Kamil's mother and asked, "Good lady, are you the Maulvi Sahib's wife?" Diyanat-un-Nisa said, "Yes, this is she; come, sit down. Where have you come from?"

Maniji said, "Where is your younger daughter-in-law?"

Muhammad Kamil's mother said, "She is up on the roof."

Maniji asked, "Shall I go up to her?"

Diyanat-un-Nisa said, "Tell us who you are and where you are from; the young mistress will come down here."

Maniji said, "I have come from the Hakim Sahib's house."

Muhammad Kamil's mother asked by name after the health and welfare of everyone, great and small, and said to Mani, "What business do you have with Tamizdar Bahu?"

Maniji said, "I'll tell her when she comes."

It was by now time for Tamizdar to come down in any case, for after saying the afternoon prayer Asghari would come down, and she said both the sunset and night prayers downstairs.

When Maniji saw Asghari, she hesitated to speak of employment as a teacher. In the course of conversation she said, "The Begum Sahib wishes to have her younger daughter educated; the elder Begum Sahib mentioned you, so the Begum Sahib sent me."

Asghari said, "Give both the Begum Sahibs my very warm greetings, and tell them that I have no objection to sharing whatever little I know with anyone; that is why a person learns to read and write, to be of use to others. And the elder Begum Sahib will know how many girls I used to teach at my parents' home. I should very much like to teach the Begum Sahib's daughter; but what can I do? The Begum Sahib will not send her daughter here, and I cannot go there."

Maniji did not openly mention a salary, but she said in a low voice, "The Begum Sahib is ready to take responsibility for every kind of expense as well."

Asghari said, "That is all her kindness, and it befits her rank; but even we poor folk living under her shadow are not left naked and hungry by God. I am ready to serve as a slave-girl without wages; but if a paid teacher is wanted, there are plenty to be found in the city."

After this Maniji asked about Asghari; and when she heard that she was a *tahsildar's* (revenue officer's) daughter, and that Maulvi Muhammad Fazil too held a post at fifty rupees a month, Mani was ashamed of having hinted at employment for nothing. But hearing Asghari talk, Mani was quite won over; though she had seen the establishments of nobles, Asghari's polished speech left her astonished, and she apologised: "Lady, please forgive me."

Asghari said, "Why do you drag me over thorns? In the first place, service is no disgrace and no sin; and then, if you asked out of not knowing, what harm is there in that?"

So Maniji took her leave, went back and said, "Begum Sahib, the teacher really is a teacher worth a hundred thousand teachers: just to look at her would make a person human; to sit by her one would gain humanity; if her shadow fell on someone, they would learn good sense; if her breath touched them, they would learn manners. But she is not one to take service. She is a *tahsildar's* daughter, the daughter-in-law of the agent of a Lahore nobleman; there is a maid in the house; a white floor-sheet is spread in the hall, with an embroidered cloth and a bolster; a comfortable, easy life. What need has she of employment?"

Shah Zamani said, "That's true. Sultana dear, you did send Maniji, but I never believed she would take service."

Maniji said, "But she is such a good person that she is gladly willing to teach for nothing."

Sultana asked, "By coming here?"

Maniji said, "Now, Begum Sahib, how would someone who doesn't care about employment come here?"

Sultana said, "What, then the girl is to go there!"

Shah Zamani said, "What harm is there in that? The house is only two steps away; and did you think the Maulvi Sahib a man of no standing? He is the son of the first cousin, on the father's side, of brother Ali Naqi Khan."

Sultana said, "Oh, then in a way they are of our own clan."

Shah Zamani said, "Well! God forbid they should be just anybody! Their affairs used to be very prosperous; since the nobleman's fortunes declined, the poor things have become poor. Even so, they have always kept a maid, and there are one or two men at the gate too."

Sultana said, "Very well, Husn-ara shall go there."
