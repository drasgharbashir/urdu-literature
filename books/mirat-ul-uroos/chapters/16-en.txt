Although Asghari had firmly resolved to go to Sialkot, she sent for Tamasha Khanam to ask her advice and told her the whole story. Tamasha Khanam said, "My dear, has someone gone mad? Leaving the city, where would you go trailing off to Sialkot now?"

Asghari said, "What do I care about the city? Wherever the one I am bound to is, that is my city."

Tamasha Khanam said, "Oh dear! What will the family say? No one from our family has ever gone away before."

Asghari said, "What is wrong with it? In the end they'll only say, 'She went to her husband; what harm did she do?' And as for the family custom, in the old days there was no mail coach, no railway, and the roads were not safe; it was very hard for women to travel, and so people did not go. What difficulty is there now? If I take the mail coach today, and God keeps all well, I shall be in Sialkot the day after tomorrow; it is as if I had gone to Meerut."

Tamasha Khanam said, "Has a letter come sending for you?"

Asghari said, "No, no letter has come."

Tamasha Khanam said, "Then it isn't proper to go without being called."

Asghari said, "You are thinking of what is proper and improper, and I tell you that if I don't go, the household will be ruined for life."

Tamasha Khanam said, "Oh, sister, why do you abase yourself like this? What do you need him for? God keep your school safe; you feed ten people from it."

Asghari said, "Really, what an idea! I set up this school only to occupy myself; I have no wish to earn a living from it. God knows whether you will believe it or not, but to this day I have not spent a single pice of the school's money on myself. Only, when your brother was going to Sialkot, I did give him fifty rupees in cash and twenty for clothes, and that too is entered as a loan; and the account of every last cowrie is written down and there to see. Is a woman's earning any earning at all? If households were kept going on women's earnings, what would men be for? So long as my own home stays sound, I wouldn't care if ten schools like this were ruined."

Tamasha Khanam said, "Where will you go in the middle of the rains like this? Let the cold weather come; then, when the weather is open, see about it."

Asghari said, "Oh, it would be disastrous to delay. What can be settled now by talking will not be settled later even by great quarrels."

Tamasha Khanam said, "Oh sister, doesn't your heart ache at leaving your home?"

Asghari said, "Why shouldn't it ache? Am I not human? But which is better, this short heartache, or burning for a lifetime?"

Tamasha Khanam said, "Have you asked your mother-in-law's leave too?"

Asghari said, "Would she give leave? But my mother-in-law is a simple soul, poor thing; if I explain it to her, I'm sure she won't stop me."

So one day Asghari explained her intention and her reasons to her mother-in-law. The matter was reasonable; who could argue against it? Asghari's going was settled. One day she went and told her mother the whole story too, and took leave of her. As for the school, she told the girls, "Mahmuda is quite enough to teach you all; I am going for only two months; all of you come as usual."

On the occasion of taking leave, she went to her elder sister. Muhammad Aqil asked, "Well, Tamizdar Bahu, you are going; what arrangements are you making for the school?"

Asghari said, "I am leaving the school and the house in your charge."

Muhammad Aqil said, "That's a good one! I have nothing to do with the house and no connection with the school; what can I do?"

Asghari said, "Whether you keep a connection or not is entirely up to you."

Muhammad Aqil said, "Tamizdar Bahu, that doesn't become you; what choice do I have? Your sister made me leave the house; and as for the school, it is for girls; if it were a boys' school, I'd gladly teach them all."

Asghari said, "Now you and my sister should both come and live in the house; Mother is alone."

Muhammad Aqil said, "Persuade your sister."

Asghari said, "What need is there to persuade her? Sister knows and understands for herself. Here, on your own, you are put to trouble; there is no one to look after the children and no one to see to the house. Sorrow and joy go along with people; it isn't right to live apart without need; and what is past is past. Why this disagreement among ourselves? And why this resentment between us?"

Akbari had had her fill of keeping a separate house, and was looking for an excuse for someone to ask her to live together again. She agreed at once, and Asghari brought them both back with her. Muhammad Kamil's mother had been deeply distressed at Asghari's going; now she too was comforted: "Well, if one daughter-in-law is going, the other is here." Mahmuda, it is true, was very anxious, wondering what would happen; but Asghari reassured Mahmuda, explaining that her sister was no longer what she had been; and on the other side she told her sister, "Mahmuda is grown up now; don't say anything harsh to her." As for the school, she told Muhammad Aqil only this: "Mahmuda will do all the teaching and so on; you just keep an eye on the general management, and have Mahmuda write up the accounts of the school's money."

So Asghari took her leave, boarded the mail coach and went straight to Sialkot. There Muhammad Kamil was greatly astonished at Asghari's sudden arrival, and asked, "Is everything all right? You haven't quarrelled with Mother and come away, have you?"

Asghari said, "Heaven forbid! Is Mother my equal, that I should go quarrelling with her? In these four years have you ever seen me quarrel with her or with anyone?"

Here Muhammad Kamil had thoroughly got out of hand and fallen into bad company. Flatterers had gathered round and were making a fool of him. Bribery was flourishing; he no longer kept even from dancing and music. He lived in grand style, his ordinary spending four times his salary. If things had gone on like this a little longer, James Sahib would surely have grown suspicious, and in the end he would have lost his post. Asghari arrived just in time. At once she stopped up the gaps on every side, and reasoned with him: "God has made you an officer on a hundred rupees; is this how you thank Him, by not being content with it?"

Muhammad Kamil said, "What harm is there in taking what is given willingly?"

Asghari said, "Glory be to God! Is money the sort of thing anyone gives willingly without a reason? These days people are so much in need of money that they don't care even for their honour, but they won't let go of money. Let a man judge by himself what he gives to anyone. There is the *zakat* (the obligatory alms), which still means something: on every hundred, once a year, a fortieth part, two and a half rupees; and giving even that takes one's breath away! Where have people got a treasure of Korah [TN: Qarun, proverbial for vast wealth] heaped up, that they give to you without a purpose? When they see that their business is going wrong, and that if they don't give, their case will be lost, then in desperation they borrow and pawn and sell the jewellery of the women of the house to pay the bribe."

Muhammad Kamil said, "I don't take it myself; then what is there to fear?"

Asghari said, "In the first place, bribery cannot be hidden; and besides, suppose it were not revealed to men, God, who sees behind every veil, knows it. Heaping up the sins of others and gathering up an account to answer for at the Last Day is great recklessness."

So, explaining everything for and against, Asghari made Muhammad Kamil repent. After staying a few days, Asghari asked, "These four men to whom food is sent outside, who are they?"

Muhammad Kamil said, "They are candidates for employment; poor men, far from home. I said to them, 'Very well, stay with me until you find a post.'"

Asghari asked, "And haven't they found posts yet?"

Muhammad Kamil said, "Posts are to be had, but they are beneath their station."

Asghari said, "When their condition has come to this, that they eat their bread lying on another man's back, what question of station is left? Let them take whatever they can get, little or much."

Muhammad Kamil said, "Heaven knows what you are saying! How can they take something beneath their dignity?"

Asghari said, "So there is disgrace in a lowly post, and no disgrace in sitting on another man's doorstep! When these people haven't even that much self-respect, their other habits are bound to be bad too; their company is not good. They are sure to be taking something in your name as well. Tell them either to take employment or to leave."

Muhammad Kamil said, "My good manners won't let me send them away."

Asghari said, "When they have no consideration, why must you show consideration? If anything is left over from us, there are many poor people in the family; they have the first claim. What is the use of giving to strangers, and among strangers, to people like these? And there is no need to send them away harshly; explain it to them somehow."

In short, these very men were Muhammad Kamil's evil spirits. Asghari tactfully had them turned out. Among the servants, those of bad character were picked out one by one and dismissed. And staying a year and a half, Asghari set everything in order, inside and out. Now Miyan Muslim's wedding was about to take place; a letter came asking Asghari to come, and Tamasha Khanam wrote very pressingly. As a long time had passed, Asghari decided to come to Delhi; but she thought to herself that it would not be wise to leave Muhammad Kamil alone. She said to Muhammad Kamil, "It is not right to live alone away from home; some relation of your own should be with you. So I think you should send for your cousin, Muhammad Salih, your maternal aunt's son; he will learn the work of the court here with you, and study too; and perhaps he will find a post somewhere as well."

A letter went to Amir Begum, and Muhammad Salih arrived while Asghari was still there. This boy was extremely well-behaved, and only two years younger than Muhammad Kamil. Now that Asghari was easy in her mind, she took leave of Sialkot and reached Lahore, where she stayed a week with Maulvi Muhammad Fazil Sahib. Maulvi Muhammad Fazil was nearly sixty years old, and the agent's work was very laborious: every day without fail going to all the officials' courts to see to the nobleman's cases, and morning and evening calling on the clerks. When the poor Maulvi Sahib came home at night, he was utterly tired out. Asghari said, "Father, your age is no longer fit for such toil; you should think of sitting at home. I have read in a book that a man should divide his life into three parts: the first part for childhood, the second for managing the affairs of the world, and the third for rest and the remembrance of God. So now come home and sit at your ease."

The Maulvi Sahib said, "In the first place, the nobleman won't let me go; in the second, there has to be someone to do the work in my place."

Asghari said, "When you plead your old age to the nobleman, he will most likely agree; and as for doing the work, what's wrong with my brother-in-law?"

The Maulvi Sahib said, "What does he know of the customs and rules of courts and durbars?"

Asghari said, "Send for him and keep him with you for a few days; by watching and looking he will learn everything. He is a learned man; Hindus take up court employment after reading two or four Persian books."

The Maulvi Sahib liked what Asghari said. Asghari went on to Delhi, and the Maulvi Sahib sent for Muhammad Aqil. Within a few days Muhammad Aqil had taken over all his father's work and greatly pleased the nobleman with his service. Then the Maulvi Sahib said to the nobleman, "Let this boy now serve Your Highness; grant me my freedom:

'It is the custom that the masters of the deed
Should set the aged slave free.' [TN: from Sa'di's *Gulistan*; Persian: rasm ast ke malikan-e tahrir / azad konand banda-e pir.]"

The nobleman was very generous at heart; he settled a pension of twenty rupees on the Maulvi Sahib for life, and took Muhammad Aqil on at full pay in the Maulvi Sahib's place.

When Asghari came to Delhi, she turned her thoughts to Mahmuda. Husn-ara had come home from Jhajjar, and in those same days Jamal-ara too had come from her in-laws' house to see her younger sister. The Hakim's whole household were Asghari's devoted followers. Hearing of Asghari's arrival, both sisters came running; they talked of all sorts of things. Jamal-ara said, "Teacher, I can't tell you how my heart longed for you. Husn-ara, of course, is your pupil; but I am more than a pupil; it was you who made my ruined home whole again."

Asghari said, "What am I worth?"

Jamal-ara said, "Really, teacher, as long as I live I shall never forget your kindness; but what can I do? You will never accept any service from us; otherwise I would have shoes made for you from my own skin, and even then perhaps I would not repay what I owe you."

Asghari said, "In the first place, I was able to do you no service; and if, in your graciousness, something I did pleased you, then, Begum Sahib, God has made you able to do everything; what is it for you to make poor folk like us happy?"

Husn-ara said, "Oh teacher, how can you say such a thing!"

Asghari said, "Listen, Husn-ara dear: teacher and pupil are things of the past; that was only for the school; now, God keep you, you are married. On one side you are the wealthiest among the grandchildren and the crown of the wealthy; and on the other, this lady is of noble birth, daughter and wife of nobles. There is no one wealthier than you in this city now; if anyone reaches you and still goes away disappointed, it is the fault of his own fate."

Husn-ara said, "Dear teacher, what is it?"

Asghari said, "My dear, it is a very difficult matter; if you promise not to disappoint me, I'll tell you."

Husn-ara and Jamal-ara thought, "She will ask for a post for someone." Both said, "Teacher, by God, we are at your service with heart and soul. Why, we long for you to ask something of us."

Asghari said, "In my eyes the matter is a great one; but if both you ladies are willing with all your hearts, it is nothing great."

The two sisters said, "Teacher, God knows, if it is something we can do, we shall never hold back."

When she had made them give a firm promise, Asghari said, "My petition is this: accept Mahmuda as a daughter of your house."

Hearing this, both sisters fell silent. Then they began to talk of this and that. When they were both about to get up, Asghari caught Husn-ara's scarf with one hand and Jamal-ara's with the other, and said, "Now I shall fight and quarrel for my due; and until my request is granted, by God, I shall not let you go."

Husn-ara said, "Teacher, what say do we have in this? Arjumand is still a boy; and besides, in such matters, while the parents are alive, what say do sisters have?"

Asghari said, "Elder sisters who are married are equal to a mother; and matches are not made without everyone's advice. It is impossible that you would not be consulted."

Husn-ara said, "But nothing has been mentioned anywhere in our house yet."

Asghari said, "You may not know it: a proposal was sent to Ulwi Khan's house and came back."

Jamal-ara said, "Teacher, if you've heard so, it must have been sent; but nobody has said anything to us about it. What was wrong with Ulwi Khan? Heaven knows why they took the proposal back." In this way one thing led to another. Asghari said, "Good ladies, my business is being left aside; give me an answer, 'yes' or 'no'."

Jamal-ara said, "Teacher, how can we possibly give our consent?"

Asghari said, "There are three things: wealth, looks and character. Wealth, we poor people have none of, not even in name. As for character, Husn-ara dear, you know Mahmuda well; you were together for two years; tell the truth: modesty, consideration, good manners, virtue, good sense in every task and every kind of skill, reading and writing, sewing, cooking: does Mahmuda have all these or not? It isn't because Mahmuda is my sister-in-law or my pupil; no, God has made that girl endowed with every quality. Well, Husn-ara dear, if I'm telling a lie, say so."

Husn-ara said, "Teacher, can anyone throw dust on the moon? Mahmuda Begum, God bless her, has no equal in the great houses. Why, let anyone come up to even a fraction of Mahmuda Begum!"

Asghari said, "And as for looks, she has a nose, ears and eyes like anyone else; she too is a human child; when she grows up, she will become somewhat better looking."

Jamal-ara said, "Oh teacher, you call Mahmuda Begum a human child! By God, she is a child of the *houris* (maidens of Paradise). In the great houses, high shop and tasteless sweets: we have never seen a good-looking face. Here we two sisters are; by God, some of our maidservants are better looking than we are; and Mahmuda is as lovely as the sun and the moon; where does one see people with a face like hers?"

Asghari said, "Then, my dear, apart from our poverty, what is wrong with us? Though it is a big thing for a small mouth to say, not many generations have passed since the late Ali Naqi Khan, and after all we too are among those who bear his name."

The two sisters said, "Teacher, you are our crown; and are you and we two different families? One clan, one blood."

Asghari said, "Then why hesitate? Grant my request."

Husn-ara said, "Very well, teacher, today we will mention it to Mother."

Asghari said, "Not mention it; I could mention it myself; rather, help in this with all your hearts; and now that the matter has been raised, let it be carried through to the end."

The two sisters promised: "Teacher, as you intend, so, God willing, it shall be."

So for the time the two sisters took their leave. The next day Asghari herself went to call on Sultana Begum, and presented her with a very fine shawl kerchief worth two hundred rupees which she had brought from Sialkot. Sultana Begum said, "Teacher, you put me to great shame; it is we who ought to serve you, not take from you instead."

Asghari said, "I had this kerchief made specially for you to order; please accept it. For a year and a half it has lain tied up in my bundle in the hope that I would come to Delhi and present it myself."

Sultana Begum said, "I accept it as a blessing; but by God, I am ashamed. If only you had ever asked me for something, so that my heart might have been glad."

Given this much opening, Asghari stood up with her hands folded and stated her request.

Sultana Begum said, "Very well, teacher; but do sit down."

Asghari said, "Now I shall sit down only when I have my wish."

Sultana Begum took her by the hand and made her sit, and said, "The affairs of sons and daughters are difficult affairs. When we buy a farthing cup from the potter, we tap it and test its ring; and this is a bargain for the earnings of a whole life; it should be done after thought and consideration and taking advice. You have raised it; now let me consult their father, my elder sister, and two or four others in the family; then we shall see how it goes. And Arjumand is still a boy; what hurry is there for his marriage?"

Asghari said, "I have asked beyond my reach. Just as in Egypt an old woman came with a skein of yarn to buy the Prophet Joseph [TN: in the popular story, an old woman came to the slave market with a skein of yarn as her bid for Joseph], so I have nothing to give or take except poverty and humility; now only your kindness is needed."

Although Sultana Begum said nothing in words, it was clear from her manner that the request had not displeased her. As she left, Asghari said to Jamal-ara and Husn-ara, "Now it is in your hands to see it through."
