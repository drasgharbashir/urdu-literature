When Muhammad Aqil had finished his prayers and come back, his mother said, "Now go to your in-laws' house, and I make you swear on my life not to quarrel or say a word there."

Muhammad Aqil said, "Then don't send me."

His mother said, "Boy! Heaven forbid, heaven forbid! O God, what an evil tongue! It is your own wife's home; whom else should I send? Here, give this rupee into the hand of your sister-in-law Asghari as her *eidi*, and this half-rupee to your aunt-in-law's son, Miyan Muslim; and take half the toys with you too. Have Mama Azmat [TN: *mama*, a maidservant] go with you carrying a tray of vermicelli and milk and a basket of sweets. Now mind, don't you say a word."

Muhammad Aqil said, "And, Mother, shall I take the hand-drum and the tambourine as well?"

His mother said, "There now, that's enough! Don't you go blurting out anything like that over there."

So Muhammad Aqil reached his mother-in-law's house. Inside, Akbari Khanam was making a racket with her friends, and the noise of it all could be heard out in the lane. Mama Azmat went in. Asghari saw the maid from a distance and said in a low voice, "Oh, sister, oh, sister, be quiet! The maid has come from your husband's house."

Once inside, Azmat called Muhammad Aqil: "Young master, please come in."

So Muhammad Aqil went in and greeted his mother-in-law. She said, "Long life to you! May you live long!" Meanwhile Asghari too came out of the inner room, carefully arranging her veil, and greeted her brother-in-law with a deep, respectful bow. Her brother-in-law sat Asghari on his lap and gave her the rupee. Asghari looked towards her mother; her mother said, "Take it; your brother-in-law is giving you your *eidi*." Asghari took the rupee, greeted him again, got down from his lap and respectfully sat apart. Then she got up and, very neatly, spread a clean cloth before her brother-in-law, and brought vermicelli in a plate, milk in a bowl, sugar in a saucer and a spoon, and set them before him. Her mother said, "Eat, my son."

Muhammad Aqil excused himself: "I was kept a long time at the Eidgah; I have only just eaten."

His mother-in-law said, "What does it matter? Vermicelli is nothing but water; do eat."

While Muhammad Aqil ate the vermicelli, Asghari put in cardamom and made him a delicious *pan*. After eating they talked about this and that. A little later Muhammad Aqil said, "Madam, I'll take my leave."

His mother-in-law said, "Where will you go now? Sleep here."

Muhammad Aqil said, "Today is Eid; there are visitors to meet. Then there are things to send round to people; and I didn't tell my mother I would be away for the night."

His mother-in-law said, "It is too late now for visiting, evening is coming on; and your mother is quite able to see to the sending of things." And she laughed and added, "You are not a baby at your mother's breast any more! In any case Azmat is going back; she will tell them."

Muhammad Aqil made a great many excuses, but his mother-in-law would not listen to one of them, and he had to stay against his will.

When they had finished eating and drinking, an hour or two into the night, Asghari put away the dishes and pots and everything lying about in its proper place; bolted the chain on the outer door; locked the storerooms and handed the keys to her mother; put out the lamps in the outer verandah and the kitchen; made *pan* for her mother, her sister and her brother-in-law; and, her work done, went off to sleep.

Now the mother-in-law said to Muhammad Aqil, "Tell me, son, what is this constant fighting between you, husband and wife? Akbari has such a bad habit that she never, even by mistake, tells me anything about her husband's home. It is the way of daughters the world over to tell their mothers every little thing about their husband's home; I don't know what has got into this one, God help her. You may wear your mouth out asking her, but heaven forbid she should tell you anything. But news of the neighbourhood reaches one from ear to ear; sitting at home, I too hear things from outsiders."

Hearing this from his mother-in-law, Muhammad Aqil hesitated for a while, and out of respect the answer would not come out of his mouth. But he thought, "This chance has come after a long time, and she herself has raised the subject and asked. To keep silent at such a moment would be quite unwise. Better to spit out the poison of a lifetime; perhaps something for the future will come out of today's talk."

So Muhammad Aqil said, with much embarrassment, "Your daughter is here; ask her what trouble she has had at our house. Has she been lacking in any kindness or attention? Has anyone quarrelled with her, or said anything bad to her? You know that there are only a handful of us in the house. As for my mother, the whole neighbourhood knows her; she is such a peacemaker that she has never once in all her life quarrelled with anyone. If anyone says ten harsh words to her, she keeps quiet. Muhammad Kamil is busy all day with his reading and writing; he goes out in the morning and comes home at night, eats his dinner and goes to sleep. I have never even seen him speak to her. Mahmuda is afraid of the very sight of her. That leaves me, and here I sit; whatever complaint she has of me, let her speak freely."

Muhammad Aqil's mother-in-law now turned to her daughter and said, "Yes, my dear, whatever is in your heart, you too say it out plainly. It is not good to keep things in your heart; keeping them there makes the grievance grow and the trouble worse."

Akbari, though she was very bold at telling lies, could not bring herself to say anything at that moment in front of Muhammad Aqil, and she was afraid inside that "I have come and told my mother a great many lies; I hope the tinning doesn't wear off and show me up now." [TN: an idiom: the deception might be exposed.] Thinking this over, she put the question aside, and all she said was, "We are going to set up a separate house."

Akbari's mother said to her son-in-law, "Tell me, my boy, what objection have you to living separately? By the grace of God you are in service yourself and earn your own living; you depend on your parents for nothing. Your own food, your own clothes: why then live dependent on someone else? My son, however dear a bride may be, where is the comfort of living separately to be found in a parents' house? To eat what you like and cook what you like. And this is a thing worth thinking about: live with your parents and earn a hundred thousand, and still you get no credit. How are people to know whether you eat your own bread or are a burden on your parents?"

Muhammad Aqil said, "If you are asking about comfort, we shall only know the value of what we have now after we separate. Twice a day we eat what is ready cooked and sit at ease without a care. Once we are separate, we shall have to think about flour, lentils, meat, vegetables, dung-cakes and firewood, all of it; and judge for yourself how many worries there are in keeping house. To take all these troubles on one's head for no reason does not seem to me sensible at all. As for eating what we like and cooking what we like, we have that now; ask her yourself whether she has ever asked for anything that was not done. In big families, it is true, there is this kind of difficulty: one has a fancy for sweet rice, another wants fried *khichri*, a third must have *pulao*, a fourth would like *qorma* (a rich meat curry), and the physician has put a fifth on a special diet; where are ten cooking pots for ten people to come from every day? Our family is not very big, after all. Whether she asks for something or not, it's only us. Let even that go: if she feels so strongly, let her see to the cooking herself. My mother herself has said so several times; ask her whether she has said it or not. And as for credit, which you mentioned, that too does not seem sensible to me. What matters is one's own comfort; let people think what they like in their hearts. And suppose people did think that we were a burden on our parents, what dishonour would that be to us? They are our parents, not strangers. Our parents brought us up, nurtured us, fed us, clothed us, taught us to read and write, and arranged our marriages; there was no dishonour in any of that. So what feather of the ruddy goose has grown on us now, that being dependent on them should be thought a cause of dishonour?" [TN: "a feather of the *surkhab* (ruddy shelduck)" is an idiom for some special distinction.]

His mother-in-law answered, "If everyone thought as you do, why would anyone separate? It is the way of the world, and it has always been so and always will be, that sons part from their parents. And I know that there can be no bride in the world whose husband earns money who would choose to live among her mother-in-law and sisters-in-law."

Muhammad Aqil said, "What you say is right; if sons did not part from their parents, where would so many houses in the city have come from? But everyone's circumstances are different. Living separately does not seem at all suitable for my circumstances. I am in service on ten rupees; on that income it looks very hard to keep up a separate house. And then there is no relying on this job either. God forbid, if after we separated I lost my job, it would be very hard for me to go back to my father's house. That would indeed be a disgrace: 'The gentleman went off on his own, and now he has had to come crawling back to live on his father's crumbs!' Copying other people is no good in this matter; one must think about one's own situation. You have heard the story of the man who bought salt and cotton in the bazaar. He loaded the salt on a mule and the cotton on a donkey. As they went along, they came to a river on the road. The river could be forded, so the man drove the mule and the donkey into the water just as they were, loaded. In the middle of the river the mule ducked under. After a moment it raised its head, and the donkey asked, 'Why, friend mule, what did you do that for?' The mule answered, 'Brother, you are very lucky; you are loaded with cotton, and its weight is very light. Wretched me, I have salt on me; the weight has cut my back till it bleeds. This master of ours is so cruel that he never gives a thought to our suffering; he loads us with as much as he likes, any old how. I thought, by the time I reach the stage my back will be gone; take a dip, and the salt will soak in the water and some of it at least will melt. The lighter I am, the better. The most the master will do is give me six or seven more blows with his stick, and I get beaten all along the road anyway. Look, now my load is only half.' The stupid donkey copied the mule and ducked under too. The cotton soaked up water and became even heavier. When it raised its head, it could not move. The mule laughed and said, 'Well, brother donkey, how are you?' The donkey said, 'Friend, I'm dying.' The mule said, 'You fool! You copied me, but you might at least have understood that it is cotton on your back, not salt.' Mother dear, I am afraid that if I copy other people, I shall end up like that donkey."

His mother-in-law said, "My boy, you are not one to be convinced by anyone, and I have not studied logic as you have. I see the simple fact: you earn ten rupees a month, by the grace of God, and times are cheap; no little ones, no children, God keep you. Two of you, husband and wife, can eat meat and bread comfortably and dress in fine muslin. If everyone worried about the future the way you do, the world's business would come to a stop. Never mind a job, there is no relying on life itself. As long as there is life, it should be spent in laughter and happiness."

Muhammad Aqil said, "That is just what I am thinking about: whether the happiness lies in living separately or together."

His mother-in-law said, "What is the point of arguments and proofs? Why don't you say plainly that you don't want to separate from your mother? Your wife asked one thing of you, and you are terribly reluctant to agree to it; and then you say you are not lacking in kindness to her. What is comfort and happiness? Whatever makes your wife happy, and whatever she thinks is comfort." After this ill-feeling began to seep into the talk, and Muhammad Aqil fell silent. It was late at night, too. Muhammad Aqil said to his mother-in-law, "Now please go and rest; I will think the matter over again."
