No sooner was the betrothal made than the younger Hakim Sahib began to press for the wedding, and sent word to the Maulvi Sahib: "For a long time I have intended to go on the *hajj*, and I am waiting only for this; there is no relying on life; I wish the marriage to take place in the month of Rajab." The Maulvi Sahib asked Asghari. Asghari said, "For the present we should send word: 'We are seeing to it and doing what we can; if the modest provision we mean to give can be got together in that time, then we too have this last duty to perform, and the sooner the better.'"

The Hakim Sahib sent word again: "I did not make this match with your family in hope of dowry and provision. I want the girl; don't trouble yourselves about provision." The answer went back: "Very well; we too are willing to hold the marriage in Rajab." The twenty-seventh of Rajab was fixed, and preparations began on both sides. As soon as the preparations began, the Maulvi Sahib grew anxious. Sometimes he said, "I'll borrow from Hazari Mal"; sometimes he thought, "Shall I sell the ghee market, or mortgage it?" Seeing the Maulvi Sahib troubled, Asghari asked, "What plan have you made?"

The Maulvi Sahib said, "What can I tell you? The wedding date is bearing down on us, and there is no way of finding money from anywhere. I asked Hazari Mal for money, and he put me off too; I thought of selling the ghee market, but no buyer comes forward."

Asghari said, "On no account borrow, and don't sell the property either. There is nothing worse than debt; and what is easier than parting with property? But getting it together is very hard."

The Maulvi Sahib said, "If I'm not to borrow and not to part with the property, am I an alchemist? Or do I know how to make money appear by magic? Where is the money to come from?"

Asghari said, "First look over the household accounts. Some clothes are ready already; only a little trimming will be needed, and some of my suits are very heavy; by reducing them, enough trimming will come out to do for Mahmuda's suits. The vessels are there; nothing needs to be bought. The odds and ends and the other furnishings I'll give from my own; they are only lying about uselessly and spoiling, and I have no use for them. And surely you have some money in hand too."

The Maulvi Sahib said, "Only five hundred rupees."

Asghari said, "That's plenty. When I went to Sialkot there were four hundred rupees of the school's money, which are kept in trust; while I was away, another two hundred came in; half of that is my sister's due and a hundred is Mahmuda's; put together, that makes five hundred from the school's money. I have written to Mahmuda's younger brother and asked for three hundred rupees; my brother-in-law has written that he is sending two hundred; so in this way there are fifteen hundred rupees in cash at present. And what use to me are the bracelets worth a thousand that I was given at Husn-ara's wedding? I meant to give them to Mahmuda; but on second thoughts it does not seem right for that house's bracelets to go back into the same house; I'll sell them. I sent them to the bazaar through Tamasha Khanam; Panna Lal offered thirteen hundred rupees; if by Mahmuda's luck some buyer who needs them turns up, God willing, fifteen hundred will be got. And one more plan comes to my mind: go to Lahore to fetch my brother-in-law, and when asking the nobleman for his leave, mention the matter to him. The nobleman is very open-handed; I hope he will certainly help somewhat. It has always been the custom of Indian princely households to help their trusted servants on such occasions."

So Asghari sent her father-in-law to Lahore. When the Maulvi Sahib went to pay his respects to the nobleman, the nobleman asked, "Maulvi Sahib, what brings you here?"

The Maulvi Sahib submitted, "It is the marriage of your servant's daughter; I have come to ask that Muhammad Aqil be granted a month's leave. I cannot presume to ask that anyone from Your Highness's family should attend; but if the Diwan Sahib, who is in Delhi, would grace the gathering on behalf of the court, it would raise my standing among my peers."

The nobleman granted Muhammad Aqil's leave, gave the Maulvi Sahib his travelling expenses, and sent orders to the Diwan Sahib: "Attend the Maulvi Sahib's gathering on our behalf, and give five hundred rupees as the wedding gift." Thanks to Asghari's advice, these five hundred rupees came for nothing, without anyone stirring. Meanwhile the jewelled bracelets reached Nawab Hatim Zamani Begum through Tamasha Khanam; she was enchanted at the sight of them and, without a second thought, handed over two bags of money. Now money came pouring in from every side. Under Asghari's management, the very finest suits of clothes were made and a set of jewellery for the four limbs. The wedding was such as had not been held in the Maulvi Sahib's family for several generations, and even the bridegroom's family were astonished at the provision. Whatever was given was plentiful and costly, and every article was of a new fashion. Two suits came from the bridegroom's side: one of gold-spangled tissue for the night, and one of gold embroidery for the fourth day. And the ornaments, from dowry and from the bridegroom's gifts together, were countless: for the nose, a nose-ring and stud; for the forehead, the *tika*, the *jhumar* and the *bena*; for the ears, rings and leaf-pendants, jewelled and plain, spangled hoops, ear-chains, *magar*, *murkiyan*, *bijliyan*, *karanphul* and *jhumke*; for the neck, *gulband*, collar, *champakali*, *kanthi*, *tora*, *dhukdhuki*, *chandanhar*, chain and necklace; for the upper arm, *joshan*, *nauratan*, *bhojband* and *naunage*; for the hands, bracelets, *chuhe-dantiyan*, *lachchhe* and wristlets; for the fingers, rings, plain rings and *jor*; for the feet, anklets, *tore*, bangles, *lachchhe*, toe-rings and plain rings [TN: names of traditional ornaments]. Gold-embroidered, net-worked and braided, twenty-five suits in all; two hundred vessels, and other furnishings on the same scale. In short, the marriage was solemnised with great pomp. Mahmuda went to her husband's house, and there received the title of Qamar-Astani Begum.

Hakim Fathullah Khan was a very pious, God-fearing and devout man; he had long intended to make the *hajj*, and was only waiting for Arjumand Khan's marriage. Now, after the wedding, he watched his daughter-in-law's ways for a few days. What need was there to watch? Mahmuda had been turned on Bi Asghari Sahiba's lathe; no flaw or shortcoming of any kind remained in her. However much the Hakim Sahib tested her, he found his daughter-in-law skilful, intelligent and full of good sense. It was a sweet melon to begin with, and sugar added on top: in the first place Mahmuda was good by nature, and on top of that came Asghari's teaching and Asghari's advice; what more could one ask?

In short, the Hakim Sahib was fully convinced that Qamar-Astani would manage the house well. Now, all of a sudden, with great energy, the Hakim Sahib began preparing for Arabia: he had meant to make the pilgrimage, and now he resolved to emigrate there for good. Whatever he had in cash he took with him; the houses, shops, markets, granaries, villages and inns, everything he made over in his son's name. His relations, as is the custom, did try to dissuade him; but the Hakim Sahib was absorbed in God and would not listen to a word. Calling on the name of God he set off, and left a whole world of property to his son and daughter-in-law.

Although Mahmuda was married, she showed Asghari even more respect and regard than before, and asked her advice on every little matter. Now indeed Asghari had the chance to try her own intelligence: a great establishment, great affairs. She made such arrangements that, may God not make me tell a lie, she made Arjumand Khan the king and minister of his day. No household for far and wide, let alone in Delhi, could compare with his.

How far can this story be written? This much has been written, but to tell the truth, not an ounce of what is in my mind has yet been told. Until now Asghari had been poor; what can a naked woman bathe, and what can she wring out? [TN: a proverb for an obvious impossibility.] But now, God keep her, wealth and plenty were her lot; she had all the control of management and all the scope for arrangement her heart could wish. The things this woman did in this condition will certainly be remembered in the world till the Day of Judgement; but I am sorry to say there is no time to write them. Still, for anyone who will take advice and listen to and understand what is said, what has been written is not little. Every kind of lesson and every sort of teaching is in it; in name it is a tale and a story, but in truth it is counsel and guidance. Now, before ending this book, one more thing must be written. It is this: Asghari became a mother at a very young age, and nothing has yet been said of her children. Asghari had many children, but by the will of God few of them lived. Only one son, Muhammad Akmal, who was married at last to Mahmuda's only daughter, Mas'uda, survived. This boy was born after several other children; before him a son, Muhammad Adil, and a daughter, Batul, had died.

Great care was taken in bringing up the children: protection from cold and heat; fixed times and measured amounts even for their food; and watchfulness that they never put anything heavy or unwholesome in their mouths. When their teeth began to come, the gums were lanced, so that the child should not suffer too much from teething; when they were four years old, they were vaccinated as a protection against smallpox. In short, every arrangement was made as far as human reason reaches; but no one's skill prevails against fate. Muhammad Adil died at the age of four. He had dysentery; he was given medicine to stop the flux; fever set in; delirium followed; the boy, so carefully reared, slipped out of her hands. His loss was still fresh when Batul fell ill at the age of seven. Such a terrible flux set in that it stopped only when it had taken her life. Every medicine in the world was tried; but when does death heed medicine? Within a single week the girl wasted away and was gone. Batul's death was a very great blow to Asghari. In the first place she was a girl; and in the second, whether because she was fated to die or for some other reason, she was so devoted to her mother that she would not leave her for a moment. When her mother said her prayers, she sat on the prayer mat; she slept with her and got up with her; even if it was her mother's medicine, she must taste it; and at that tender age she was so devoted to study that she had begun the translation of the tenth *sipara*.

When Muhammad Adil died, the women had begun trying to shake Asghari's faith. One would say, "There is something wrong with your womb; get Mihr Ali Shah to treat you." Another would say, "There is an evil eye on your milk; have an offering placed at the crossroads." Another would say, "It is the wasting sickness of the spirits; have Ramazan Shah tie a charm." Another would say, "The house is unlucky; have Mir Alim nail a spell into it." Another would say, "You came and went on a journey, and some witch has fastened on you; go to Kichhauchha." [TN: the shrine of Kichhauchha, famous for exorcisms.] People suggested amulets and charms, spells, sorcery and magic from all over the world; but bravo, Asghari! Though two children died one after the other, she remained ever content with God's will. If anyone said anything, this was her only answer: "When God wills, He can show His grace without all that."
