The others went to sleep, but Muhammad Aqil spent the whole night turning this over in his mind, arguing with himself. When he got up in the morning he saw Asghari sweeping. Seeing him, Asghari greeted him and said, "Brother, there is hot water ready for your ablutions."

Muhammad Aqil said, "No, my dear, I shall say my prayers with the congregation in the mosque."

Asghari said, "Brother, please don't go away afterwards; I have made tea for you. But will you take it plain or with milk?"

Muhammad Aqil said, "Whichever there is." Asghari said, "Your voice sounds rather thick; perhaps a cold is coming on, and then milk will do you harm."

Muhammad Aqil said, "No, it isn't a cold coming on. I sat up talking a long time with Mother last night; I have slept badly, that's all."

When Muhammad Aqil came back from his prayers, he found his mother-in-law had finished her own prayers and was eating *pan*. He greeted her and sat down. Asghari brought a tray and set it before him: piping hot tea in a teapot, two cups, two spoons and sugar in a saucer. Muhammad Aqil drank the tea: it tasted good, had a good colour and the right fragrance, and drinking it did his heart good. Akbari lay asleep as usual. Muhammad Aqil said, "Mother dear, do urge her to say her prayers too." His mother-in-law said, "My son, she was her grandmother's great favourite, and the old lady's love has spoilt her temper and her habits completely. When she was little, if I scolded her about anything, her grandmother would stop speaking to me for days on end; and as for anyone daring to lay a finger on Akbari, there was no question of it! Akbari would make a fuss about every little thing and break and smash things, and for fear of her grandmother nobody could say a word. Her father and I used to fall out over this every day."

Now Muhammad Aqil began to take his leave. As he was going, his mother-in-law said, "Son, remember what we said last night, and be sure to make some arrangement about it."

Muhammad Aqil thought about it all the way home. When he reached the house, his mother saw that his face looked worried. She thought, "He must have quarrelled at his in-laws' today." She asked, "Muhammad Aqil, didn't you do as I told you?"

Muhammad Aqil said, "Mother, I tell you truly, there was no quarrelling or fighting at all."

His mother said, "Then why are you so downcast?"

Muhammad Aqil said, "It's nothing. I have come straight from my bed without washing; perhaps that is why my face looks sad to you." His mother said, "Boy, come to your senses! As if I had never seen you just out of bed! Tell me the truth, what is it?"

Muhammad Aqil was obliged to tell his mother the whole story of the night before. As soon as she heard it, she turned so pale you would not have found a drop of blood in her body if you had cut her. But she was a very wise woman, and she said, "My one wish was that as long as there was breath in my body I should keep you all close to my heart, and that you two brothers should live in harmony; but when I look, I see everything going the opposite way. Well, today I will tell you: since the second month of her marriage Mizajdar Bahu has meant to set up a separate house. The ten rupees you bring and give me every month are very galling to her. Day after day I hear from your wife's friends, 'The bride is going to take a house in the Ballimaran quarter, and she will take Zulfan with her.' As long as those girls sit together, that is all they talk about among themselves. I once even said straight out to your aunt-in-law, 'If Mizajdar Bahu dislikes living with us, let her keep her own food and clothing separately and live in this same house.' Then I learned from your aunt-in-law that Mizajdar Bahu would not agree even to that. A man marries for happiness and comfort; a fight every day and a quarrel every other day is a very bad thing. If this is what your wife wants, and living separately makes her happy, then in God's name, we have no objection. Wherever you live, be happy and prosper. God has made us love our children; so whenever you come this way I shall see you for a moment and be content, and whenever I get free from the work of the house I shall go round myself and see you."

No sooner had she said this than Muhammad Aqil's heart was full, and he began to weep in spite of himself, feeling that today he was being parted from his mother. His mother wept too. After a while Muhammad Aqil said, "I will not live separately, whether my wife stays or goes."

His mother said, "Oh, my son, how can that be? Do respectable people ever give up their wives? You have to spend your life with her. What about us? We are sitting with our feet dangling in the grave; 'if I die today, tomorrow is another day'. Take my advice and do what she says. The day we married you, from that day we counted you as separate. You are not the only son, nor am I the only mother; what son has ever stayed with his mother?"

Muhammad Aqil asked his friends' advice as well. They all said, "It is best to put an end to the trouble. And why should everything depend on living together? Live apart from your mother, and serve and obey her."

When everyone gave him the same advice, Muhammad Aqil too said, "Well, let us try living separately. If this woman mends her ways, and treats the house as a home, and gives up her bad temper, disobedience and sharp tongue, then living apart is no shame and no sin. It only means I shall have to worry about housekeeping and live on less; and while one lives in this world there is no escape from worry in any case. If there is no worry now, this daily quarrelling is a torment in itself. And fear of being short of means is pointless too: whatever livelihood is written in one's fate will reach one in any case. What part do a man's efforts and plans have in that?" With these thoughts Muhammad Aqil made up his mind to separate.

As it happened, a house next door to that very one was empty. He took it at a rent of one rupee a month, and even paid the key money and signed the agreement. He took the key too, and sent word to his in-laws: "The house is settled; when you come, we will move into the new house." And he told his mother as well, "I have taken the Tarkash house." [TN: apparently a house named after its owner or former occupant, a *tarkash* (quiver-maker).]

His mother had everything that belonged to Mizajdar Bahu, the chests of clothes, the dishes, the floor-spreads, the curtained bed and the bedstead, put into a separate room. In the evening Mizajdar Bahu arrived too. In the morning the mother got up, opened the room and said to Muhammad Aqil, "Now, my boy, look your things over carefully, both of you, husband and wife."

Muhammad Aqil said, "Mother, what are you saying? Was this some stranger's house?"

His mother said, "It isn't that, my son; but I don't want anything to go astray while it is being moved about." And she said to the maid, "Azmat, you and the neighbour woman carry all these things over to the Tarkash house."

Akbari's friends, Chuniya, Rahmat, Zulfan and Sulmati, turned up, and in no time they had picked up all the things and carried them from one house to the other. Mizajdar Bahu came and settled happily in her new house. For three days Muhammad Aqil's mother sent food over twice a day. On the fourth day Muhammad Aqil said to his wife, "Well, madam, now let us start making some arrangement for meals."

Mizajdar said, "All the things are still lying about anyhow. When they are put away I'll see to the pots and the stove at my leisure; I have no time just now."

So for seven days the bread was baked at the public oven; at night they sent to the bazaar for kebabs, and in the day sometimes for *rabri* (thickened sweet milk), sometimes for curd, and husband and wife ate their bread with that. At last, by asking her every day, Muhammad Aqil got Mizajdar to cook. Mizajdar had never cooked a meal in her life. The bread she made was of an extraordinary shape: neither round nor square, with one corner sticking out this way and four corners that way; thick at the edges, a lump in the middle; burnt in one place, raw in another, and black with smoke. And when she cooked lentils, the water was in one place and the lentils in another. In short, Mizajdar cooked such delicious and delicate food that the sight of it would drive away your appetite. The curry she made had a bad colour and a bad taste; if she put in salt it was poison, and sometimes it was tasteless water. For a day or two Muhammad Aqil put up with it; in the end he began to take his meals at his mother's house. Mizajdar too made arrangements for her own comfort. Twice a day she sent to the bazaar for *kachauris* (fried stuffed breads) and cream, *kunda khoya* (thickened milk), *rabri* and kebabs and ate them. Whatever food was cooked, Zulfan and the others ate it and grew fat. For these cats the hanging larder had broken open, as the saying goes. But how could such dainties be had on ten rupees a month? Quietly the household things began to be sold, and Muhammad Aqil knew nothing about it at all.

One day, when Muhammad Aqil had gone to his work, Mizajdar fell asleep at midday. When Chuniya came and saw the bride sleeping soundly, she told her brother Miran. He was a cunning scoundrel. Mizajdar went on sleeping, and Miran came in broad daylight and stole all the dishes and pots. When Mizajdar got up and looked, the house had been swept clean. The storeroom was locked, so the things in it were saved; but everything lying about outside he had carried off, one by one, until there was not even a bowl left to drink water from.

When Muhammad Aqil came home from work and heard of it he was very distressed, but what use was regret once "the birds had eaten up the field"? He quarrelled fiercely with his wife and beat his head in despair; in the end, after weeping and wailing, he sat down resigned. Borrowing money here and there, he bought two light cooking pots; he asked his mother for some small dishes, and his mother-in-law sent a basin, a griddle and a plate. So somehow things got going again.

As it happened, in those days a procuress had come to the city, and the whole city was talking about her. Muhammad Aqil too had told his wife, "Don't let any strange woman into the house. There is a procuress about these days who has already robbed several houses." But Mizajdar was foolish to the last degree, and it was her habit to make friends quickly with anyone. One day that very procuress came into her lane disguised as a *hajjan* (a woman who has made the pilgrimage to Mecca). To beguile foolish women, this cunning *hajjan* used to carry all sorts of holy relics and hundreds of other things: rosaries of healing clay from Karbala, little flasks of Zamzam water, dates from holy Medina, collyrium from Mount Sinai, a scrap of the covering of the Kaaba, beads of sea-agate and coral, and *Nad-e-Ali* [TN: a prayer invoking Ali, worn as an amulet], the five Suras [TN: a small book of five chapters of the Quran], and many medicines. When she came into the lane and set out her wares, a crowd of girls gathered. Mizajdar heard of it too and said to Zulfan, "When she gets up to leave the lane, bring the *hajjan* here; I want to see the holy relics too." Zulfan went and stood there and brought the *hajjan* back with her. Mizajdar made a great fuss of the *hajjan*, sat her down beside her and looked at all her things. Mizajdar liked two of them, the collyrium and the *Nad-e-Ali*. In the course of their talk the *hajjan* saw that this woman would soon fall into her trap. She weighed out a great deal of collyrium for one pice, handed over the *Nad-e-Ali* for two annas, and made her a present of a turquoise ring for nothing. Mizajdar was delighted.

After that the *hajjan* told her about the sea and about Arabia, and made up two or three stories out of her own head, which Mizajdar listened to with the greatest eagerness, and she felt drawn to her. The *hajjan* asked, "Tell me, Bi, have you no children?"

Mizajdar said, "Where was I to get such luck?"

The *hajjan* asked, "How long have you been married?" Mizajdar said, "Not yet a year." Now the *hajjan* was sure of Mizajdar's stupidity, and she said to herself, "At the mere mention of children she has heaved a sigh as if she had been hoping for years." The *hajjan* said, "There's no cause for despair. You will have so many children you won't be able to manage them. But for now, of course, you must feel lonely in an empty house. How is your husband?"

Mizajdar said, "He is always displeased with me."

In short, at their very first meeting Mizajdar became so free with the *hajjan* that she told her all about herself, everything, and in the course of talk the *hajjan* learned all her secrets.

The *hajjan* sat there a full watch [TN: about three hours]. When she began to leave, Mizajdar begged her, "Dear Bi Hajjan, when will you come again?"

The *hajjan* said, "My niece lives in the Momgaron ka Chhatta [TN: a quarter of Delhi, "the arcade of the wax-makers"], and she is very ill. I have come from Agra to look after her. Her medicines and treatment leave me little time, but, God willing, I'll come and see you every two or three days." The next day the *hajjan* turned up again, bringing a silk trouser-cord. Seeing the *hajjan* coming from a distance, Mizajdar was delighted and asked, "What's that trouser-cord?"

The *hajjan* said, "It's for sale."

Mizajdar asked, "How much is it?"

The *hajjan* said, "Four annas. There's a begum living in my neighbourhood who has fallen on hard times; she lives by selling off her things one by one. I often sell things for her."

Mizajdar was enchanted to see so cheap a trouser-cord. At once she took out the money and put it in the *hajjan*'s hand, and pleaded with her earnestly, "Dear Bi, whenever there is anything for sale, show it to me first."

The *hajjan* said, "Very well: you first, and the others afterwards."

After that they talked about this and that. As she was leaving, the *hajjan* took out a little purse; in it, wrapped in several layers of cloth and paper, were a few cloves. She gave two of these cloves to Mizajdar and said, "The reason for friendship in this world is that friends may do each other good. I am giving you these two cloves. Tie one in your plait. The other ought really to be kept in your husband's turban, but perhaps your husband would suspect something; never mind, sew it into a pillow, and see their effect from this very day. But take care to keep them in a clean, pure place. And measure out a piece of red thread the length of your height and give it to me; I'll have a charm made for you and bring it. When I went on the pilgrimage, a begum of Bhopal was travelling on the same ship; perhaps you have heard her name, Bilqis Jahani Begum. God had given her everything; there was no end to her wealth; servants and attendants, slave-girls and slaves, palanquins and litters, she had them all. But for one thing, she was always sad about children; she had no child. And for another, the Nawab paid her no attention at all; perhaps he did not love her because she had no children, for otherwise the Begum was lovely as the sun and the moon, and with all that beauty and wealth her manner was so simple that she would seat nobodies like us beside her and talk to us! The Begum had the very highest faith in holy men. Once she heard that a perfect saint had come to a place three *kos* away; on a dark night she went to him from her house on foot and stood before him for a whole watch with her hands folded. My life for the fame of holy men! When at last the Shah Sahib raised his eyes and looked at her, he said, 'Go, mother; this very night you will receive a command.' In a dream the Begum received the good tidings: 'Go on the pilgrimage, and bring up from the sea the pearl of your desire.' Next morning preparations for the pilgrimage began. The Begum paid the fares of five hundred poor people herself and put them on board the ship; I was one of them. Being near her all the time, the Begum Sahib (O God, may she be honoured in both worlds!) began to show me great kindness and used to call me her friend. For ten days the ship sailed on and on; on the eleventh day a mountain appeared in the middle of the sea. The captain said, 'This is the Mountain of Abyssinia; a great and perfect holy man lives on it. Whoever goes to him comes back with his wish granted.' The Begum Sahib said to the captain, 'Get me to that mountain somehow.' The captain said, 'Your Highness, the ship cannot get to the mountain; but if you command, we will anchor the ship and take you in a boat.' The Begum said, 'Very well, so be it.' Five women went with the Begum to the Mountain of Abyssinia, myself and four others. When we reached the mountain, a wonderful fragrance filled the air. We walked on until we came to the Shah Sahib. It was a desolate place, with not a soul about; the Shah Sahib lived all alone in a hut. What a radiant face! Like an angel! When he saw us all he blessed us; he gave the Begum twelve cloves, recited something and breathed on her. To me he said, 'Go to Agra and Delhi and help people in their affairs.' My daughter, these two cloves are two of those twelve. When we came back from the pilgrimage, the Nawab, who before would not so much as ask after the Begum, had now come to Bombay a month ahead and was waiting there to meet her. The moment the Begum set foot off the ship, the Nawab laid his head at her feet and wept and begged her pardon. For six years after the pilgrimage I stayed in Bhopal. Through the blessing of the holy man's prayer, God keep them, the Begum had four sons one after another while I was there. Then I longed for my own country and asked the Begum's leave. She tried hard to keep me, but I said, 'The Shah Sahib has entrusted me with the service of Delhi and Agra; I must go there.' Hearing this, the Begum let me go, though unwillingly."

Two cloves, and with them a charming romance two leaves long! Mizajdar became a believer heart and soul. The *hajjan* gave her the cloves and went. Mizajdar Bahu bathed, changed her clothes and put on scent; then, saying "In the name of God", she tied one clove in her plait, changed the sheet on her husband's bed and the covers of the pillows, and put the other clove in one of the pillows.

When Muhammad Aqil came home, he found his wife neat and clean and the sheet on the bed changed without his asking. He was pleased and talked to her kindly.

Mizajdar said, "Look, I bought something today." With that she showed him the trouser-cord. Muhammad Aqil said, "How much did you pay for it?" Mizajdar said, "You guess what it's worth."

The cord was genuine Lahore work and very fine, broad and wide, with clustered tassels of gold thread. Muhammad Aqil said, "It can't be worth a pice less than two rupees."

Mizajdar said, "I got it for four annas."

Muhammad Aqil said, "Tell the truth."

Mizajdar said, "I swear by your head, I got it for just four annas."

Muhammad Aqil said, "That's very cheap. Where did you find it?"

Mizajdar said, "There's a *hajjan*, a very good woman, who has been coming to the lane for a long time. It belongs to some begum; she brought it to sell." With that Mizajdar showed him the collyrium, the *Nad-e-Ali* and the turquoise ring.

Greed is such an evil thing that even a very shrewd man is deceived by it. Wild creatures, the myna, the parrot, the *lal* [TN: the red avadavat, a small finch] and the bulbul, flee at the sight of a man, but greed for grain traps them in the net, and they stay prisoners in a cage all their lives. In the same way Muhammad Aqil was pleased to see his own advantage; and when Mizajdar said, "The *hajjan* has promised to bring me all the begum's things that are put up for sale," Muhammad Aqil said, "Certainly, we ought to look at them; but make sure they are not stolen goods, or there will be trouble afterwards. And yes, make sure the *hajjan* is not a swindler."

Mizajdar said, "Heavens, don't say such a thing! That *hajjan* is not like that." So the matter passed.

After the talk she had had with Muhammad Aqil that day, Mizajdar's faith in the cloves was firmly fixed. The next day she sent Zulfan to call the *hajjan*, and that day Mizajdar made herself the *hajjan*'s daughter and made the *hajjan* her mother. At night the *hajjan* came up again in talk with Muhammad Aqil, and again Muhammad Aqil said, "Mind you keep your wits about you; there are many procuresses and swindlers in that disguise." But greed had drawn such a veil over Muhammad Aqil's own understanding that he did not grasp the plain fact: does anyone give away goods worth two rupees for four annas without a reason?

Muhammad Aqil ought to have forbidden that *hajjan* to come at all and had all her things sent back; and as for Mizajdar, where had she the sense to see what lay beneath?
