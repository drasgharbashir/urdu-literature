When the news of Batul's death reached Durandesh Khan Sahib, he was much distressed, and in his distress he wrote this letter to his daughter.

"To my dear Asghari Khanam, after blessings, be it known: I have just learned from the Delhi letter of Batul's death. I cannot deny that it has grieved me; but my reason has not become so unbalanced that I should lose patience like foolish people. My great anxiety is for you; it would be no wonder if this blow has been very hard for you to bear. But in every situation a person should take counsel with reason. Reason has been given to us for this very purpose: that in grief or in joy we may take its help.

"It is very necessary to reflect on the state of the world, and such reflection is not without profit. Earth and sky, mountains, forests, rivers, men, animals, trees: there are hundreds of thousands of kinds of things in the world, and the world is a very great workshop. The rising of the sun by a regular rule in the day, then the coming of night, and the shining of the moon and the stars; now heat, now cold, now the rains; and from the effect of water, the springing up of fruits and flowers of every variety and colour: each one of these is enough to keep a thoughtful man thinking for years. And is a man's own condition not enough to reflect on? How a man is born, how he is nurtured and grows, how the states of boyhood, youth and old age pass over him, and how in the end he departs from the world: this is a very fine and difficult subject. God has set this whole workshop going for some purpose, and as long as He wills it, it will go on in the same way. This world has existed for only seven or eight thousand years, and its life is very short; that is, the Day of Judgement is now very near, and the world must soon come to an end. It has been established by census of the world that close on three and a half thousand people die in the world every hour, that is, one person every moment; and about as many must be born. Now reckon how many hundreds of thousands of people die and are born in the world in a single month; and then consider that this has been going on for seven thousand years; that is, countless people have died in the world up to now. So death is a necessary and ordinary thing. Great and mighty kings, great scholars, great physicians, even great prophets who could raise the dead, could not themselves escape death. It is God's binding decree that whatever is born into the world must one day die; so if one day this decree is carried out on us or on someone near and dear to us, we have no cause for complaint or lament. This is not a subject to be passed over lightly; reflect on it well; and when you come to understand the reality of death, I am sure you will understand, as I do, that to grieve at anyone's death is fruitless and useless.

"Grief at someone's death depends on our attachment. If we hear that the emperor of China has died, the news has no effect on us at all, because we had no attachment to him; indeed, if some stranger in our own neighbourhood dies, with whom we have no connection of any kind, we shall grieve very little. So we grieve at the death of the person to whom we are attached; and the stronger the attachment, the greater the grief. If a grandmother's, a niece's, an aunt's daughter-in-law's, or a father's sister's niece dies, what of it? A distant connection, a distant relationship. And indeed, why should it depend on relationship? There is grief in love and friendship too. Now we must consider to whom in the world we are most attached; there is no fixed rule for this. If someone is a near relation, but there are constant quarrels and constant ill-feeling, such relations count as strangers; but if someone is a stranger, with no relationship and no kinship, but much love and friendship, he is somehow more than a relation. So each person has particular attachments according to his own circumstances. All these worldly attachments arise from advantage and interest; if our own blood relation stands in the way of our advantage, he is bound to drift away from us; if a stranger is of use to us, he is bound to become as dear to us as our own. But the advantage from which attachment springs need not be in rupees and pice, though most often it is of that kind; sometimes attachment springs from hope and expectation as well. Many people are our friends who give us nothing, but the expectation that 'if ever we are in need of any kind, they will be of use' is the cause of the attachment. I could draw out this discussion at great length, and it would be proper to draw it out as long as possible; but my real purpose in this letter is to discuss only the attachment to children; and if I find the leisure, God willing, I shall write a book on this attachment and send it to you.

"This attachment to children is universal; no parent, indeed not even an animal, is without it. From this it is clear that it is not founded only on advantage and interest; rather, the Lord of the worlds, who is supremely wise, has ordained that parents must love their children. Children need to be reared for some years; so that they may be well reared, He gave parents a love for their children, so that by force of this love they may nurture them and bring them up until, grown up, they can make their own way in the world. So parents are their children's servants for the purpose of rearing them; and so to rear one's children, only this much attachment was given to parents by God. All the rest of the fuss, that now one longs for children, and if there are none, then there are medicines and treatments, amulets and charms, spells and prayers; or if there are children, then the worry that there should be sons and not daughters, or that whatever there are should live: these are the pompous show of man's own desires.

"There remains the question why man has created in his heart this longing for children, beyond God's will. No doubt it is for advantage and interest; but there are several kinds of advantage. Some think that children carry on one's name; some imagine that they will help them in their old age; some fancy that they will inherit their property after them. Now reflect how absurd and mistaken these ideas are. What does carrying on one's name mean? That people should know that these are So-and-so's sons and So-and-so's grandsons? In the first place, when we ourselves are no longer in the world, what does it matter whether anyone knows of us or not? Besides, consider how far a name is carried on; ask any man the names of his forefathers; perhaps everyone can tell you as far as his grandfather, but beyond that the descendants themselves do not know who their great-grandfather and great-great-grandfather were. Secondly, what need have people to dig up the bones of other people's dead? So even supposing the name is carried on, it is for one or two generations; after that, all is over! And even the carrying on of a name for one or two generations is only a fancy. For ten years I have been in the hills; thousands of people know me and I know thousands; but they do not know my father, nor am I acquainted with their fathers; nor does the need ever arise to mention or ask a father's name.

"The second reason for longing for children is the advantage that they will help us in old age; but this idea too is sheer nonsense. How can it be certain that we shall live until they grow up, or that they will live until we are old? And even supposing that life is granted, children's helping us is a mere fancy. In these times we find very few children who pay regard to their parents' dignity, or who think of serving their parents; respect and service apart, nowadays parents mostly suffer pain and trouble from their children. People receive grief at the hands of the very children they longed for, from beginning to end. While they are small, rearing them is a misery: today they have sore eyes, now pains in the ribs, now they are teething, now they have smallpox. When at long last they are grown up, there is the worry of their food and clothes. Who knows what state a man is in, whether he is employed or not, whether he has money or not? They must be given, from wherever it can be found; let the parents starve if they must; even if there is nothing in the house to buy provisions with, these must have their farthing's worth of roasted gram every day. Whether it is Eid or Baqr-Eid, a fair or a festival, 'Come on, give me a new suit', 'Four pice to buy sweets': even this much is a mercy. Now the parents want the boy to learn a trade and to study; and the boy is a scamp who runs miles at the very name of study; he swears never to go to school until four of the schoolboys carry him off by the legs; and when he gets there, the moment the master's eye is turned, he slips off to the crossroads, or stands by the canal playing tipcat, or wanders the bazaars sifting the dust. And when they are a little bigger, they begin to answer back to their parents; the company of louts, the society of scoundrels; no shunning of what is low, no avoiding bad company; they go about bringing disgrace on their fathers and forefathers. In this way some become accomplished scoundrels, thieves, gamblers and drunkards.

"Now the child is of an age to be married; the whole city is searched, and nowhere is a suitable match to be found. The go-between has walked her feet off; friends and acquaintances have given up; the relations have asked everyone, one after another, and no one will consent. Life is one long misery: the poor mother goes about making vows here, standing to catch omens from overheard words there, holding a doll's wedding somewhere else; five times a day the prayer is, 'O God, send someone from the unseen!' When at long last a match is settled, it is in such a place that, while the poor mother hasn't so much as a silver thread, the bridegroom's family demand spangled hoops. Somehow, selling herself, she holds the wedding: the sparrow lost its life, and the one who ate it got no taste of it. The dowry is tossed contemptuously about; the bridegroom's mother says, 'Ugh, what have they given? Why did she have to bear a daughter when she was so poor?' Nothing is good enough for her; there is a taunt in every word. When his lordship the son-in-law comes, there is no end to his conceit; he won't so much as wash his hands until his father-in-law has set his shoes straight for him, let alone eat. The fourth-day ceremony is hardly over when husband and wife come to blows; the parents gave away their daughter, and bought themselves a quarrel into the bargain. And then it is not just for a day or so; no, the wheel of misery turns for a lifetime. The daughter begins to have children; her mother becomes an unpaid slave, a nurse without wages; she has endured the misery of rearing her own children all her life; and now, when at last she had had two years of rest, she has her daughter's whining brats to look after. And if a daughter-in-law comes in, she is a knot of mischief, a bundle of quarrels: she does not think her mother-in-law worth a shoe; she makes her sisters-in-law's lives a misery; she has no modesty before her husband's elder brother and no respect for her father-in-law; she is a woman who would snatch the turbans off men's heads; God preserve us! And look at the worthless son: while his wife stirs up all this trouble, the wretch takes his wife's side and actually quarrels with his parents, until the poor parents leave the house and go to live apart in a rented house. This is the reward parents get from children nowadays; there are very few who find comfort in their children. So in our folly, how we long for children, as if we were petitioning for calamity and misery to be sent to us!

"There remains the idea that we should long for children so that our property may have an heir. How empty, hollow, flimsy and foolish this idea is, is plain. When a man has himself left the world, what does it matter whether his sons take his wealth, or whether it is declared heirless and goes to the government? This wealth is of no use in the hereafter, except as much of it as we ourselves spend in the way of God before we go, or as is spent in our name in the way of God after us. When we have not spent our wealth ourselves, and have left so necessary a task to our children, there is no greater fool than we. Children who come by their parents' savings for nothing never hesitate to spend them. A man values only the money he has earned by the strength of his own arm and the sweat of his brow; and money that comes without labour fares as the saying goes: 'Goods for nothing, a heart without pity.' The children, no doubt, will squander the wealth freely on dancing, music, outings and shows; but if they are asked to have the *fatiha* said over a dish of millet porridge in their father's name, they will not hear of it. Are there not hundreds and thousands of examples in the world of people who went on hoarding all their lives in meanness and miserliness, and whose children, as soon as they came into the wealth, made such merry that in a few days they squandered the savings of their father's lifetime?

'Good God! Who squandered it? And who had saved it?' [TN: a Persian line, allah allah ke talaf kard, ke andokhta bud.]

"From this account it will be clear that the attachment we have allowed to grow in our hearts for our children does us very great harm. We are commanded to keep only so much attachment to our children as to rear them while they need our help; and even in rearing them we should not let the hope enter our hearts that the children, when grown up, will ever serve us in return for this rearing. To cherish such a hope is the height of folly; rather, we should understand that God, who is our Master, has entrusted to us the service of rearing them; in rearing our children we are carrying out His command. This garden is God's, and we are the gardeners of this garden on His behalf. If the owner of the garden orders some tree to be pruned or cut down, what right has the gardener to say, 'I have reared this tree with great labour; why is it being cut down and pruned?' All the attachments of the world exist only so that people may be of use to one another. We have been sent into this world for a few days for some purpose; and here we have been made someone's father, someone's son, someone's brother, so that people may help us and we may help them, and so that we may live out in harmony together the life that has been allotted to us. The world is not our home; we must go and live elsewhere; no one is ours and we are no one's; if we are someone's father, it is only for a few days, and if we are someone's son, that too is only for a few days. If we see someone die, what is there to grieve over? We might grieve if we were to stay sitting here; but the same journey lies before us ourselves; who knows at what hour the summons will come and we shall have to set out?

"And the hardest thing of all is that dying is not only that the life leaves the body, as if the soul had gone from one house to another; no, there we shall have to give an account of every single thing. The tongue will have to answer for lies, backbiting, oaths, obscenity and idle chatter; the eye will be punished for its evil looks; the ear will have its ear pulled in return for listening to slander and to music; the hand that has wronged anyone or stolen another's goods will be cut off; the foot, if it has walked in wrong ways, will be clamped in the rack. It will be a very hard time; only if God of His grace carries us across can it be done. Whoever is free of these concerns may grieve at someone's death or rejoice at someone's birth, and rightly; but is there anyone in the world who has been freed from care about his own end? Asghari, look to yourself, and make provision for that day when nothing but your deeds will be of any use. And pray that the Lord of the worlds, for the sake of His friend Muhammad (God's blessing and peace be upon him), may give us all a good end. With prayers,"

The sinner, Durandesh Khan
