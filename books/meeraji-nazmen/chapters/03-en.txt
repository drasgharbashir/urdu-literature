# 11. The Call of the Sea

These whispers are saying, come now, for after calling and calling you for years a deep
weariness is settling over my heart.
Sometimes for a moment, sometimes for a long while, I have heard voices, but this strange call is coming:
no one has ever yet grown tired of calling and calling, nor perhaps will anyone tire in future.
My dear child, see how much I love you; if
you do this,
no one will be worse than me; O God, O God.
Sometimes a sob, sometimes a smile, sometimes only a frown,
but these voices have kept on coming;
through them life of two days has met eternity.
But this strange call, over which deep weariness is settling,
keeps threatening to wipe out every voice.

Now there is no movement in the eyes, no smile on the face, no frown;
only the ears go on listening.
This is a garden; the wind blows, the buds burst,
the blossoms give scent and the flowers open, and having opened, wither,
fall, and make a velvet carpet on which
the fairies of my longings move with such strange grace
as if the garden itself were a mirror.
From this mirror every form came out bright, adorned itself, faded, and once faded never rose again.
This is a mountain, silent and still;
sometimes a spring, bubbling up, asks what lies beyond its rocks;
but the skirt of the mountain is enough for me: in the skirt is a valley, in the valley a river,
in the river the drifting boat itself is a mirror.
In this mirror every form came out bright, but once it began to fade in a moment,
it never rose again.
This is a desert, a spreading, dry and leafless desert;
here the whirlwinds have become the embodied image of fierce ghosts.

But I keep my eyes fixed on a clump of trees far off;
now there is no desert, no mountain, no garden;
now there is no movement in the eyes, no smile on the face, no frown;
only one strange voice is saying that after calling and calling you a deep
weariness is settling over my heart.
No one has ever yet grown tired of calling and calling, nor perhaps will anyone tire;
then this call is a mirror: it is only I who am tired.

No desert, no mountain, no garden: only the sea calls me now,
for everything came from the sea and will go and join the sea.

# 12. The Clerk's Song of Love

All my night passes in dreams, and I sleep;
then the goddess of morning comes.
I get up from my bed, I wash my face;
the loaf of bread I brought yesterday,
I ate half of it;
what is left is my breakfast today.

The colours of the world are strange:
the man who lives opposite me has a wife in his house,
and on the right is a one-storey house; it is empty;
and on the left is a rake who keeps a mistress;
and among all these there is I too, only you are not.
I have every other comfort; only the scent of tresses is not.
I finish my breakfast and come out of my house,
I walk the road to the office.
On the way there is the bustle of the city: a tonga, two cars;
children go to school; and what shall I say of the tongas!
The cars are skimming lightning; how shall I bear the arrows of the tongas?
Granted, in them is the wealth, the riches, the treasure of good families;
some are lively, some innocent too;
but on the road, on foot, there are unlucky, sorrowful ones like me too.
On the tongas is the lightning of smiles,
the sweet music of talk.
Again and again the thought urges me: there is pity in the heart of nature.
Everything is here; only you are not, only you are not;
and in my eyes there is no courage to weep, there are no tears.

Somehow the road comes to an end, and the prison comes.
Come, set your heart on your work: so someone advises me.
Slowly, slowly I take my heart into the office.
The heart is foolish, a silly child; I put it off another way.
Then the river of work flows, and when do I keep my senses?

When half the day has gone, the officer comes from home
and has the peon call me to his room.
He says this and he says that, but it all stays useless.
I grow tired of such talk of his, I grow tired, I grow tired.
For a moment I come to my own room to fetch a file,
and a fire smoulders in my heart: if only I too were some officer,
then my house would be some way off from the dust and lanes of this city,
and you would be there.
But I am only a clerk, and you are the queen of a high house;
this is my love story, and it is older than the earth.

# 13. That Is

I think I will write a poem,
but what shall I say in it?
In one thing there are a hundred things;
here are victories, there defeats.
The heart speaks, I listen,
and so I pick the flowers I please.
When I am defeated, let me not keep silent,
and when I win, let me speak out boldly [TN: reading uncertain];
in a moment let me write a poem;
but what shall I say in it?
When the tangle grows like this,
then the goddess of contemplation comes.
Mostly she stays silent;
if she speaks, she says only this:
"Why do you think of writing a poem?
Why speak what is in your heart?
It is better just to keep silent."

But then this thought comes:
when the river flows on
in its endless story,
heedless and running so,
granted, it turns at every bend,
yet it passes on saying what is in its heart;
what falls on its head it bears,
and what comes to its mouth it says;
it climbs over the breast of the earth
and goes on and on ahead.
So let me too speak my heart;
if I feel like it, let me write a poem,
whether in one thing there are a hundred things,
whether they bring victories or defeats,
whether anything comes of it or not,
whether my joys or my sorrows,
whether someone comes and says to me,
"Why did you speak out, why did you not keep silent?"

Whether, having spoken, I keep silent:
I think I will write a poem,
but what shall I say in it?

# 14. Wine

It is pointless,
this talk;
the eye sees a few bottles on the shelf.
Come, let us go,
come, let us go where the thought never comes to us that life is an error of sight;
come, let us go where this door, these knocks upon knocks, cannot be heard at all;
where this window cannot quarrel with the wretched eye;
where the open air, the open air, as if someone were saying, "Come,"
as if it were saying, "Come, this is open air; here, do come."

But even in the open air, now pits, now standing trees, are saying, "Look,
this talk is pointless,
pointless."
The eye sees a few bottles on the shelf.
Come, let us go.
The lap that was the mother's was the mother's lap;
there everything that was pointless was a mistake.
The eye sees a few bottles on the shelf.
Come, let us go.
Sister was saying, "Now you really should settle down and make a home."
I was thinking, whose home? Our home, your home?
And at that brother spoke up: "It is pointless, this talk is pointless."
The eye sees a few bottles on the shelf.

Come, let us go where there is no shelf, no few bottles, where they cannot say, "Come, let us go,
this talk is pointless";
but let there be no pit there, nor any tree;
there let us go and meet the last peace.

But here too there are a few bottles on the shelf.

# 15. I Remember Home

Tell me, why does the earth not shrink and become a point?
Why did this spreading sky charm the heart at that time?
Now on every side there are strange people and their talk;
some slips off the heart, some pricks the breast.
On the waves of this very talk this barge drifts on,
which finds no shore.
Whoever I come before, I must have these lips say, in a light smile, "I
know you," while the heart says, "When did I want to?"
On these same waves I drift; I find no shore.

Tell me, why does the earth not shrink and become a point?
What smile was that? It was my sister's smile; my brother too used to laugh;
he laughed, my sister laughs and says in her heart,
"What a thing brother said! Look, Mother and Father laughed."
But so time flows on; the shore has become a spectacle;
I find no shore.

Tell me, why does the earth not shrink and become a point?
What turn of fate is this? Perhaps it is no turn; but
why does this spreading sky charm the heart at this time?

Everyone's short life flows on, and mine too;
I look at everyone: one smiles, one laughs;
one seems to laugh, one seems to weep;
I look at them all, and having looked, I stay silent;
I find no shore.
