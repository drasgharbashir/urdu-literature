# The Reform of Poetry

When the art of poetry has degenerated, its reform becomes almost impossible. In the first place, because of long familiarity and habit, poets are not even aware that there is any road other than the one they are travelling. And if someone does leave the nation's highway and take another road, he meets two very hard difficulties. First, to set foot on an untrodden path, cross all its stages and reach the goal is itself a very hard and arduous task. The second difficulty, harder still, is that since the taste of present society is wholly strange to this new way, no one can measure its difficulties and nowhere can its labour win recognition. So no one can ever set foot in this lane unless he gives up all hope of appreciation from his age and, like the farmer who plants a *khirni* sapling in his land at the end of his life, plans a feast for the taste of future generations on a mere faint hope. [TN: the *khirni* is a slow-growing fruit tree, proverbially planted for one's grandchildren.]

It is possible that a poet who walks the new way may, by setting some subject in the dress of verse to suit the needs of the age and the demands of the time, win some fame or acceptance among the novelty-loving people of the country, and that from one particular point of view he may receive more praise for his verse than he expected. But as poetry this is not really praise of his verse, nor does he take it as praise; rather, hearing such praise, he quietly recites this verse to himself:

The warrior's blood-stained hand and sword are left unpraised; you look first at the beauty of his horse and the finery of its trappings.

His contemporaries among the poets, partly from prejudice in favour of the old poetry and mostly because his taste is strange and foreign to them, do not accept his way, because it is apart from the highway. And some, in their own view, satirise him gracefully by saying that so-and-so has not written poetry, but has stored up provision for the next world by writing useful and moral subjects. But if he has really given up hope of appreciation from the present generation, he should not care about such things; he should hope rather that if there is any moisture left in the soil of the nation, the seed will not go to waste.

# Goldsmith's Poetry

When Goldsmith first left the way of the old poets of his country, which was founded on lies, exaggeration and themes of lust and greed, and took up true natural poetry, he met these same difficulties, and he has described this state in a poem. In it he addresses his poetry of the new kind and says: "O my dear poetry, you are the first to flee from places where the passions run riot. In this age that does not value you, instead of winning hearts and gaining a pure fame, you are reproached everywhere. Because of you I have to feel ashamed in public gatherings, but when I am alone I am proud of you. You are the guide of those who seek excellence and the nurse of virtue. So God himself will be your guardian. In any part of the world, whether it be the peaks of Torno [1] or the hill of Pambamarca [2], whether the hottest zone of the equator or the freezing cold of the pole, wherever you are criticised, face the times and overcome the gusts of the adverse wind, and with your moving laments help the truth, which people despise. Teach the misguided to scorn wealth, and convince them that those who rely on their natural resources may be poor yet can be happy. The prosperity that trade brings to a country makes a show for a time, but very soon it sinks like a potter's kiln, as the waves of the sea at last destroy the dam that was built with the utmost toil and labour. Countries that rely on their natural resources face the hardships and ravages of time as rocks face the waves and floods of the sea, and stay firm where they stood." [TN: Hali paraphrases the closing lines of Oliver Goldsmith's *The Deserted Village* (1770).]

# How Poetry Can Be Reformed

To lay the foundation of a new poetry, it is necessary to publish as many fine examples of it as possible; and it is equally necessary to set out in some detail the true nature of poetry and the conditions required for becoming a poet.

# What Condition Is Thought Necessary Today for Becoming a Poet in Urdu

In our country today only one condition is required for poetry: a natural sense of metre. For a man who can put words into metre in a few simple, familiar metres, nothing more remains to be waited for before he becomes a poet. A store of ordinary subjects and ordinary comparisons and metaphors, which people have been repeating for several centuries, is ready to hand, and by chance he also has a sense of metre; what more does he need? But in truth the rank of poetry is far higher than this.

# Is Metre Necessary for Poetry or Not?

Metre is to poetry what words are to a melody. Just as a melody in itself does not need words, so poetry in itself does not need metre. Here, just as two words are used in English, *poetry* and *verse*, so with us too two words are in use, *shi'r* and *nazm*. And just as with them the condition of metre applies not to poetry but to verse, so with us too this condition should be held to apply not to *shi'r* but to *nazm*.

# What the Arabs Understood by Poetry

The ancient Arabs certainly understood poetry in this sense. Anyone who spoke in a more moving and charming way than ordinary people they took to be a poet. The old poetry of the Age of Ignorance mostly contains ready, captivating phrases and sayings of this kind, which stood above and apart from the common speech of Arabia. That is why, when the Quraish heard the unique and wonderful language of the Holy Quran, those who did not accept it as the word of God began to call the Messenger of God (God bless him and his family and give them peace) a poet, although the Quran kept no metre at all. The scholar al-Tusi writes in the *Asas al-iqtibas* that in Hebrew, Syriac and ancient Persian real metre was not required for poetry; the Arabs were the first to make metre obligatory.

# How Far Poetry Needs Metre

There is no doubt, however, that metre doubles the beauty and power of poetry. A European scholar writes that although poetry does not depend on metre, and for long ages at first it lacked this ornament, metre undoubtedly makes its effect sharper and its lancet more effective.

# Is Rhyme Necessary for Poetry or Not?

With us rhyme too has been thought as necessary for poetry as metre; but in truth it too is necessary for verse, not for poetry. The *Asas* says that among the Greeks rhyme, like metre, was not required, and mentions a poet writing in Persian named Jashu'i, who collected unrhymed verses in a book. In Europe today blank verse, that is, unrhymed verse, is more in fashion than rhymed. Rhyme, like metre, increases the beauty of poetry, making it very pleasant to the ear and giving the tongue more delight in reciting it. But rhyme, and especially rhyme as the poets of Persia have bound it with very strict rules and then added the *radif* to it, undoubtedly keeps the poet from fulfilling his duties. Just as being bound to verbal ornaments kills the meaning, so, and far more, the fetter of rhyme gets in the way of expressing the sense. Instead of first arranging a thought in his mind and then finding words for it, the poet must first of all choose the rhyme, and then arrange some thought to suit it and find words to express it whose very last part can be the chosen rhyme. For if he does not, it may be that after arranging the thought no suitable rhyme can be found, and he must give the thought up. So in truth the poet does not set down a thought of his own; he sets down whatever thought the rhyme permits. In *ghazals* and *qasidas* the last hemistich, which contains the rhyme, is often made up first, blindly, on some subject or other, and then a first hemistich to suit it is fitted on. The truth is that to put on poetry, for the sake of making it more beautiful, a fetter that leaves nothing of its real nature is exactly like giving a garment, to make it more beautiful, a cut that defeats both its final purposes, comfort and covering. In short, metre and rhyme, on which our present poetry wholly depends, and apart from which it has no quality that would justify calling it poetry, both lie outside the essence of poetry. That is why scholars of our time do not set prose against poetry, as is commonly thought, but knowledge and philosophy. They say that just as the direct task of philosophy is to instruct, to help in inquiry and to make truths clear, whether or not anyone is delighted, astonished or moved by it, so the direct task of poetry is to produce delight, wonder or emotion at once, whether or not any purpose of philosophy is served by it, and whether it is in verse or in prose.

# The Essence of Poetry

Many definitions of poetry have been given, but none that includes all its kinds and excludes everything else. What Lord Macaulay wrote about poetry, though it cannot be called a definition, does bring the mind close to what is meant by poetry today. He says: "Poetry, as was said two thousand years ago [3], is a kind of imitation, resembling painting, sculpture and acting in most respects. But how much more perfect is the imitation of the painter, the sculptor and the actor than that of the poet! What is the poet's machine made of? Of the parts of words. And words are such that even when artists like Homer and Dante use them, they cannot put into the hearer's imagination as exact and true a picture of outward things as the work of the brush and the chisel puts into our minds. But the field of poetry is so wide that sculpture, painting and acting, all three arts together, cannot reach its breadth. The sculptor can copy only form. The painter shows colour as well as form. And the actor, provided the poet has supplied him with words, adds motion to form and colour. But poetry, though it can do the work of all three arts in imitating outward things, excels all three in this: the inner self of man is the realm of poetry alone; neither painting, nor sculpture, nor acting can reach it. Painting, acting and the rest can show man's character or feelings only so far as they can be shown by the face, by colour and by motion, and even these are always incomplete and deceptive likenesses of the states that really exist within man. But the fine, deep and many-coloured states of the human soul can be shown only through words. Poetry can draw a picture of all the outward and mental things of creation. The world of the senses, the revolutions of states, the character of man, the society of mankind, all the things that really exist, and all the things that can be imagined by joining the parts of different things together, are all enclosed in the kingdom of poetry. Poetry is a kingdom whose realm is as wide as the realm of thought." [TN: Hali paraphrases Macaulay's essay on Milton (1825).]

Another scholar has defined poetry thus: a thought expressed in words in an unusual and novel way, so that the hearer's heart is delighted or moved on hearing it, is poetry, whether it is in verse or in prose. To make the meaning of the above statements plainer, we think it fitting to give a few examples here.

(1) Firdausi says:

He rubbed the bow of Chach in his hand, and fitted the arrow's notch to the string of elk-hide;
he made his left arm a pillar and bent his right, and a cry rose from the curve of the bow of Chach.

In these two verses he shows Rustam as he went on foot onto the field of battle to fight Ashkabus of Kashan and fitted an arrow to his bow to strike him. Clearly, if someone who was not a poet had told the subject of these verses in an ordinary way, it would have been enough to say that Rustam fitted an arrow to the string of his bow. But that statement does not copy at all the state in which he stood, bow drawn, ready to shoot. The manner Firdausi has chosen, however, copies that state sufficiently, so far as words could help. Yet since this is a state that the eye can perceive, a sculptor or a painter could show it in a clearer and more visible form than Firdausi.

(2) Sa'di of Shiraz:

Such a year of famine came upon Damascus that lovers forgot love.

In this verse he describes the state that some famine in Damascus brought upon its people. A man who is not a poet could say no more about this than that people were dying of hunger and thirst, or that grain and water could not be had, or other such ordinary things that usually happen in a famine. But the picture of the utmost severity of famine that Sa'di has drawn in these words could never be drawn by such ordinary statements. And since this is a state that the senses cannot perceive, painter and sculptor alike are powerless to copy it; only the poet can. An actor might manage such a show to some extent, provided the poet had supplied him with enough words.

(3) Ibn Darraj of Andalusia, in an ode, describes his infant child as he looks up into his face while the poet takes leave of his family to go somewhere far away:

Unable to give an answer in words, yet his glance knows well where the longings of hearts are found.

That is, he cannot answer what is said to him, yet his eye knows the charms that draw hearts to it. In this verse the master has drawn a picture of a purely inward state, which the painter, sculptor and actor of today could no doubt imitate to some extent, but not as the poet has. Moreover, this way of putting it could never occur to anyone but a poet. For all that he means in this form is that the way the child looked at him as he was leaving made him love it beyond control. This ordinary thing he expresses by saying that the infant, who had not even speech in his mouth, had an eye that knew a secret which many great wise and learned men do not know: how to draw other people's hearts to oneself.

(4) Naziri of Nishapur:

Under the rose-branch a viper has bitten the nightingale; what do singers who were never bitten know of the wound?

The joy and longing that rise in the nightingale's heart in spring, from the opening of the flowers, or the mildness of the air, or the quickening of the blood in its body, which poets call its love for the rose and the garden, and in whose ardour and passion it sings all day long, this state and feeling the poet has likened to the surge of a viper's bite. Even if this image too falls short of showing the true reality of that state, the idea of it that these words create could not be created even to that degree by a picture or a play. So the expression of this feeling lies beyond the reach of painting, sculpture and acting.
