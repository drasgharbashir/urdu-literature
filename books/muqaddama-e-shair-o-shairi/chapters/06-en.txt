# What Qualities Poetry Should Have

So far we have described the qualities without which a poet does not reach perfection. Now we must describe the features generally found in the verse of all the accepted poets of the world. Milton has stated them in a few brief words. He says that "the excellence of poetry is that it should be simple, sensuous and passionate." [TN: Milton's phrase, from *Of Education* (1644), is "simple, sensuous and passionate"; Hali renders "sensuous" as "founded on reality", and discusses it as such below.] A European scholar explains these words thus: "By simplicity is meant not only simplicity of words; the thoughts too should not be so fine and subtle that ordinary minds have no room to understand them. To walk the highway of the senses, not to stray from the straight road of naturalness, and to hold thought back from its caperings: this is what is called simplicity. The road of learning cannot be as clear for those who seek it as the road of poetry should be for those who hear it. The student must cross heights and depths, caves and mounds, gravel and stones, waves and whirlpools before he reaches his goal; but the reader or hearer of poetry should find a road so level and clear that he can walk it at ease, with streams and brooks running on either side, and fruits and flowers, trees and houses everywhere to lighten his journey. The verse of the poets who have been accepted in the world has always been seen to be like this, and has always been heard to have this quality: it makes peace with every mind and finds room in every heart. Homer everywhere in his verse has drawn such a picture of nature that the young, the old, and nations that live as far apart as the poles can understand it equally and enjoy it alike. On every inch of the world of the senses that his verse has reached, its light is spread like the sun's; it lights up the inhabited and the desolate alike, and affects the learned and the ignorant alike. Shakespeare's case is the same as Homer's. Unlike ordinary poets, these two do not take the exceptions [1]; they always choose the general case. They do not wish to make people enamoured of their special talent by showing particular forms and rare chances."

"The second thing Milton said is that poetry should be founded on reality. By this is meant that the thought should be founded on something that actually has some existence; not that the whole subject should be like the spectacle of a dream, where a moment ago everything was there and when the eye opened there was nothing. This is as necessary in the words as in the subject; for instance, one should not use comparisons whose existence lies in the world above."

"The third thing was that poetry should be full of passion. This does not mean only that the poet wrote the verse in a state of passion, or that his passion shows in what the verse says; along with this it is necessary that it should stir passion in the hearts of those addressed, and for this purpose their hearts must be searched, and a magnetic attraction must be placed in the expression to draw their hearts."

The magnetic attraction that this scholar mentions in explaining Milton's words, Lord Macaulay says, is found in Milton's own expression. He writes: "The common saying that poetry has the effect of magic is, in general, a phrase with little meaning; but when it is applied to Milton's verse it fits very well. His poetry acts like a spell, although at first sight there seems nothing more in his words than in other men's. But they are the words of a charm: no sooner are they pronounced than the past becomes present and the distant near, new forms of beauty appear, and at once the graveyard of memory raises all its dead. But change the structure of a phrase, or put a synonym in place of a word, and at once the whole effect vanishes like camphor. Whoever tries to raise the same enchantment after such a change in his verse will find himself in the same error as Qasim [2] found himself in, in the *Thousand and One Nights*, when he stood at a door crying again and again 'Open, Wheat!' and 'Open, Barley!', but the door would never open until 'Open, Sesame!' was said." [TN: Hali paraphrases Macaulay's essay on Milton (1825).]

Although Milton's three conditions have been explained to some extent above, in our view they still need some further explanation.

# What Is Meant by Simplicity

Simplicity is a relative matter. The very verse that seems merely simple and easy to a philosopher, whose meaning comes to his mind the moment he hears it, and whose excellence, placed there by the poet, he grasps at once, an ordinary man is unable to understand or to discover its excellence. In the same way, a vulgar verse at which a low-minded, ignorant man jumps up and falls into ecstasy, a lofty-minded philosopher hears with his nose turned up and thinks it nothing but feeble, base and frivolous doggerel.

In our view, to apply the word simplicity to a simplicity that reaches the level of feebleness and baseness is to give simplicity a bad name. Such verse should be called not simple but vulgar. But verse that is simple and easy in the eyes of people of the higher and middle ranks, while people of the lowest rank cannot understand its real excellence, should be kept within the bounds of simplicity. It is true that fine verse so clear and easy to understand that people of every class and rank, from the highest to the lowest, can understand it equally and take equal delight and pleasure in it, more deserves to be called simple and easy; but no poem in which every verse is understood by the common people and admired by the select, whether its writer be Homer or Shakespeare, has ever been accomplished to this day or ever can be. If it were so, why would there be any need to write commentaries on Shakespeare's works?

In our view the standard of simplicity in verse should be this: however lofty and subtle the thought, it should not be tangled and uneven, and the words should, as far as possible, be close to idiom and everyday speech. The further the construction of a verse is from ordinary speech, the more it will be held to be bare of the ornament of simplicity. By idiom and everyday speech is meant neither the speech of the common crowd and the market nor that of the learned and the scholars, but the words and idioms generally current in the speech of select and common alike. But in the Urdu language such strict simplicity cannot be kept up in every kind of verse; if it can be kept up anywhere, it is only in the love *ghazal* or the love *masnavi* (narrative poem in couplets), as Mir and Sauda, most of their contemporaries and some later poets did in these two forms in particular. In the *qasida* (ode, usually of praise) even practised poets like Sauda and Zauq could not keep up such simplicity. Mir Anis, although he is deeply devoted to the refinement and purity of the language, has had in the new style of *marsiya* (elegy, especially on the martyrs of Karbala) to use Arabic and Persian words in abundance and to bring them for ever into his everyday speech. Especially in this age, when people's knowledge and information grow from day to day, and new thoughts keep being added to poetry for which words are not to be had in refined Urdu, it is not possible to express every kind of thought within the limited everyday speech of Urdu.

# What Is Meant by Reality

Being founded on reality does not mean that the subject of every verse must be founded on actual fact; it means that the thing on which the verse is founded should really exist, whether in fact, or in the belief of people, or merely in the poet's own view, or should seem really to exist in his view. Again, being founded on reality does not mean that the statement must not go a hair's breadth beyond reality; it means that most of it must be real, and if the poet has added or taken away a little on his own part, there is no harm in that.

An example of the first case, in which the verse is founded purely on actual facts, is what the Shaikh of Shiraz says in praise of spring:

What wonder if a son of man is moved to joy, when in the garden the cypress has begun to dance, and the willow and the plane?
Wait until the dewy bud opens its mouth at dawn, like the mouth of the musk-pod of the deer of Tartary.
Hail has fallen on the tulip at the hour of dawn, just like the rose-scented cheek of the beloved bathed in sweat.

The wind has brought the scent of jasmine, and of the rose, the hyacinth and the willow; as though the perfumer's boy were opening the splendour of the shop.
Wallflower and marshmallow and water-lily and amaranth: patterns at which the eyes are left dazzled.
The Judas-tree, scattered over the green threshold of the meadow, is just like gold coins on a board of brocade.
And this is only the beginning of world-illuminating March; wait until the fortune of April and May pitches its tents.
The branches are still the virgin daughters of the garden; wait until they become pregnant with fruits of every colour.

An example of the second case, in which the verse is founded on the belief of the hearers, is what Mir Anis writes, for instance, in mourning for the Prince of Martyrs:

The Tablet and the Pen and the exalted Throne are trembling; the Seat is so shaken by this blow that it quakes every moment;
the angels have formed their ranks in a circle of mourning; it is feared the register of the world may be overturned.
The pen has fallen from the hands of Mercury;
on every single being a heaven of grief has crashed down.

The moon in the sky has covered its face to weep; the sun of heaven has bared its head, its eye full of tears;
grief so lies upon the stars too that they have no strength left; it is plain to the planets that rest is nowhere to be had.
It is the day of the killing of the son of the Lord of *Lawlak*;
it is the day of the end of the Five Pure Ones. [TN: *Lawlak*, "were it not for you", alludes to the tradition in which God tells the Prophet that but for him the heavens would not have been made. The Five Pure Ones (*Panjtan Pak*) are the Prophet, Ali, Fatima, Hasan and Husain.]

An example of the third case, in which the poet founds the verse merely on his own view, is what the Shaikh of Shiraz says, addressing the beloved:

My reason became a moth and still did not see a candle like you in a thousand gatherings.

A second example of the same case, describing the spring season of Shiraz:

Is it the breath of sweet basil or the scent of Paradise? Is it the dust of Shiraz or the musk of Khotan?

An example of the fourth case, in which it appears to the hearers that things are in the poet's view as he describes them, is what Naziri says, describing his own greatness and the neglect of the age:

You, Naziri, had come down from heaven like the Messiah; you went back again and no one knew your worth, alas.

Urfi praises himself thus:

I raised my head out of one collar with the moon of Canaan; I am the beloved who seeks to be gazed at, and I hold the mirror. [TN: the moon of Canaan is Joseph, the type of beauty.]

Readers may at first sight be surprised that such self-praise and boasting is held to be founded on reality; but on reflection it will appear that though such subjects are not free of exaggeration, there is certainly some glimpse of truth in them, more or less. And even supposing there were no truth at all in such subjects, there is no doubt that in the boasting and pride of some poets there is such passion that it is plain they really thought of themselves so when they wrote the verse; and merely their thinking so is enough for their verses of pride to be held founded on reality. For what we understand by reality is that there should be some intent or some thing reported behind the poet's statement, existing either in fact or merely in the poet's mind.

An example of the fifth case, in which the poet has added something to reality, is what the Shaikh of Shiraz says in praise of Turkan Khatun of Kirman:

Proclaimed in every region and famed in the world is the report of your devotion, your fear and your hope;
the thanks of travellers, which they carry over the horizons, may reach the sky, but do not reach your bounty;
the sword of champions does not have so much effect in the enemy's land as your country-conquering resolve.

And again, in praise of Shaikh Abu Bakr Sa'd, he says:

Warriors have taken kingdoms with sword and spear; you have taken land and sea with justice, resolve and judgement.
Two qualities guard the kingdom and help the faith; I think God has spoken these two into the ear of your soul:
one, strike with force the necks of the mighty; two, come with kindness to the door of the helpless.
In the eye of reason these people of mine are kings, for you have cast your shade over their heads like the *huma*. [TN: the *huma* is a fabulous bird whose shadow was believed to confer kingship.]

Since it is known of both of the Shaikh's patrons that they possessed the qualities mentioned to some degree or other, these verses of praise by the Shaikh will be held to be founded on reality. But if these qualities were ascribed to a patron wholly devoid of them, as is commonly seen in the *qasidas* of our poets, it would be said that the verse is not founded on reality.

Apart from these five cases, no other case can be found in which verse can, by pulling and stretching, be held in any way to be founded on reality; and of such verse there is no lack in our poetry. Not only in the verse of the later poets but in that of the earlier ones too there are volumes upon volumes of such examples. Here only one or two are written down as a sample.

(1) Naziri of Nishapur, although a most reasonable and sober poet, says in praise of Prince Murad:

You are the one from whom comes the being and not-being of the world; we have spoken the truth, come what may.

(2) Urfi says in praise of the horse of Hakim Abul Fath:

That swift-goer which, when you give it rein, goes from eternity past to eternity to come, and comes from eternity to come back to eternity past;
the drop that drips from its forehead at the moment of setting out settles like dew on its crupper at the time of return.

# What Is Meant by Passion

By passion is meant that the subject should be expressed in such spontaneous words and so moving a manner that it appears that the poet did not compose the subject of his own will, but that the subject itself compelled the poet and made him compose it.

Such passion can be found in every kind of statement the poet makes, whether he describes his own state or another's, speaks of joy or of grief, praises or blames, or neither praises nor blames; in short, in all the kinds of subjects that can be stated in the form of verse. The poet has in his nature a God-given gift of being affected by everything, of sharing in everyone's joy or grief, and of taking on the feelings of everyone. He can describe the state of dumb and even lifeless things in the language of their condition so well that, if they had speech, they could not describe their state better than he does. Khaqani describes the ruin and destruction of the ruins of Nushirwan's palace, which he saw with his own eyes at Mada'in, in the language of their condition thus:

We were the hall of justice, and this tyranny came upon us; what disgrace will fall on the palaces of tyrants?

That is: we who were once the hall of Nushirwan's justice and equity, since the turning of fortune has brought us to this state, what must befall the palaces of tyrants?

Firdausi describes the conversation that Yazdagird held with the envoy of Sa'd Waqqas thus:

From drinking camel's milk and eating lizards the Arabs' affairs have come to such a pass
that they covet the kingdom of Persia; shame on you, O turning wheel, shame!

On this occasion Firdausi, as his statement shows, has put on Yazdagird's very garment, and has made the copy of his anger and passion into the original itself.

Passion does not mean that the subject must at all costs be expressed in very forceful and fiery words. The words may be soft, gentle and quiet, and yet the utmost passion may lie hidden in them. Khwaja Hafiz says:

I have heard a sweet saying that the Old Man of Canaan said: separation from the friend does what cannot be told. [TN: the Old Man of Canaan is Jacob, mourning for Joseph.]

Mir Taqi says:

Whenever anyone spoke your name before me, I clutched and clutched at my wounded heart.

But only those can keep passion alive in such quiet words who know how to do the work of a sharp dagger with a sweet knife; and to measure that passion fully is the task of people of taste, on whom a thousand ill-timed sighs and laments have less effect than one well-timed cold sigh.

# Notes

[1] Hali's note: An example of founding verse on exceptional cases is this verse of Momin:
Select and common gather in the beloved's lane; there is one house that is inhabited in a ruined world.
That is, seeing a few of the beloved's admirers, who must be counted as exceptions compared with all mankind, gathered in her lane, the poet has decreed that the whole world gathers in her lane. Although his manner of expression certainly shows the poet's grace of nature, it has no effect. In contrast, the same poet says elsewhere:
On one side are we, who have come to regret it so; on the other is she, who must still be longing to be loved.
Here he has chosen a general case in which exceptions have very little part, for the end of desire and passion is always regret, and its beginning is full of longing and hope. So every heart accepts this at once, and is therefore more affected by it.

[2] Hali's note: In the *Thousand and One Nights*, in the tale of the two brothers Qasim and Ali Baba, it is told that there was a cave in a certain mountain. Whatever robbers looted from here and there they used to store in it. The door of the cave always opened on saying "Open, Sesame!" and closed on "Shut, Sesame!". Once Ali Baba, hidden, saw the robbers opening and closing the door. When they had gone he opened the door in the same way and brought away a great deal of goods loaded on donkeys. When Qasim heard of it, he too learned from him the charm for opening the door and went there. When anyone opened the door and went in, the doors closed of themselves and opened again only with the same charm. When Qasim went in he remembered the charm, but when he wanted to come out with the goods he forgot "sesame", and in its place began to say "Open, Barley!" or "Open, Wheat!". The door did not open, until the robbers arrived and killed Qasim.
