# How Urdu Poetry Can Progress in Step with the Times

So far we have set out in some detail the true nature of poetry and the conditions on which the excellence of verse and the mastery of the poet depend. Now, according to our understanding and opinion, we offer a few pieces of advice to those of our countrymen who wish to see poetry progress in step with the times.

It is plain that the means by which the poetry of Asia always went on progressing are lacking for Urdu poetry today, and there is no hope at all that such means can ever be provided in time to come; as someone has said, "The very trellis is gone on which the creepers used to grow." It is also plain that the natural spring that has always been the source of every nation's progress, namely self-help and reliance on oneself, has had its channels stopped in our nation for a long time. So in such a state, to nurse the idea of the progress of Urdu poetry is as good as to fight against an unfavourable age; especially in an age when even the poetry of languages far higher and nobler than Urdu is in decline, when science is cutting at its root, and civilisation is breaking its spell and wiping out its magic like a word written in error. But since it is the natural demand of every living creature to struggle hand and foot until the last moment, in despair as in hope, and the slaughtered beast's struggle and the consumptive's hope last until the final breath, what we wish to write is not meant to imply that anything will come of it, but to show that we wish it might.

# Natural Aptitude Is Necessary for Poetry

First of all we advise that only that man should set foot in the lane of poetry in whose nature this gift has been placed; otherwise all his labour and all his effort will go to waste. In every art and every profession, it is true, natural fitness is needed for attaining perfection; but in poetry, as has been said above, it is needed most of all. Unless a poet's thought has at least as much inventiveness as a weaver-bird has in building its nest and a spider in spinning its web, it is not at all fitting for him to waste his time on this idle fancy; he should rather thank God that his brain is free of this disorder.

The beginning of poetry is exactly like the beginning of chess. The man whose nature is drawn to chess begins in two or four days to see fine and deep moves, and takes such delight in chess that he forgets eating, drinking and sleep, and his play improves from day to day; but those whose nature is not drawn to it are in the opposite case. Though they play chess all their lives, their play never rises beyond the level they reached in their first few days of practice. So it is with poetry. Those in whose nature the gift lies find their nature opening the way from the very start. If for some reason they do not turn to it, the demand of their nature draws them to it by force; when they do turn to it, they certainly have some success or other, and so their heart grows bolder day by day. They have full confidence in their own faculty of discernment. They can judge the good and bad of their own verse without taking anyone's advice or correction. Their nature has the capacity to be affected by every state and every event, whether that state or event befalls themselves, or Zaid and Amr, or an ant, and if they wish they can profit greatly from this capacity. They need to gather the material of their poetry from outside only as much as the weaver-bird needs to bring straw and twigs from outside for its nest; the skill needed to arrange and choose words and thoughts they find within themselves, just as the weaver-bird finds within itself the art and skill of building its nest. From the verse of the masters they do not merely gain knowledge of what the masters wrote or composed; sometimes from a single hemistich or a single word of theirs they learn a lesson that a non-poet cannot learn from a master in months.

So the custom that has come down among us from of old, of taking a master for poetry and always showing him one's verse for correction, offers no hope of any appreciable benefit to the pupils. What more can a master do with a pupil's verse than mend some grammatical mistake, or correct some error of prosody or of vocabulary? But this brings no progress in the poetry itself. As for the master raising the pupil's low verse to a height, or making the pupil his equal, that is beyond the master's own power and control. If masters had the power to make their pupils their equals, Maulana Nizami would not have given his son this advice: "Do not entangle yourself in verse for the sake of a high name, for that has been sealed with Nizami." And if becoming someone's pupil were necessary for perfection in poetry, then Sana'i, Nizami, Sa'di, Khusrau and Hafiz would certainly have had such masters, whose fame, if not greater than their pupils', would at least have been equal to it or a little less.

To become a poet, first natural aptitude, then the study of nature, then reading the verse of the masters extensively and following their choicest verse, and, if it can be had, profiting from the company of those who have a true taste for verse (whether or not they are poets): this much is enough, and no more. It is true that those who lack sufficient command of the standard language may have doubts in the use of idioms; but the removal of these doubts does not depend on some practised and expert master; they can be removed by any native speaker of the language, even a midwife, a maidservant, a greengrocer's wife, or indeed a sweeper-woman.

# One Should Avoid Falsehood and Exaggeration

The second most necessary thing is that in verse one should not, as far as possible, let go of the thread of truth and reality. In our explanation of reality above, it is true, we widened the circle of expression a great deal and found many aspects of reality; but the demand of the age is that falsehood, exaggeration, slander, calumny, open flattery, empty claims, unjustified boasting, pointless accusation, ill-placed complaint, and other such things that are contrary to truth and honesty and have entered into the very substance of our poetry, should, as far as possible, be avoided altogether. It is true that in our poetry, from the time of the Abbasid caliphs to the present day, falsehood and exaggeration have gone on increasing, and lying has been held not merely permissible for the poet but the very ornament of his poetry; but there is also no doubt that from the time falsehood and exaggeration entered our poetry, its decline began. The pure Arabs and the poets of the earliest period hated falsehood and counted it among the faults of poetry. Zuhair ibn Abi Sulma, a poet of the earliest period, said, "The best speech is that which deeds bear out," that is, the best speech is that to which deeds bear witness; and this is a famous verse of the same poet:

"And the most poetic verse that you can say is a verse of which, when you recite it, it is said: 'He spoke the truth.'"

Of this same Zuhair, Hazrat Umar Faruq (God be pleased with him) used to say: "He is the most poetic of poets, for he praises only one who deserves it." Once the Banu Tamim asked Salama ibn Jandal, a poet of the Age of Ignorance, "Glorify us with your verse." He said, "Do something, so that I may speak of it."

The author of the *Iqd al-Farid* writes that "the poets of Arabia raised the honour of those they praised by their praise and made people base and disgraced by their satire." The reason for this was none other than that they described real virtues or real vices; otherwise no one can be honoured or disgraced by false praise or false satire.

Mu'awiya ibn Abi Sufyan said that "poetry is a thing by reading which the miser becomes generous, the coward brave, and the unworthy son worthy and obedient." It is plain that if any verse can answer to this description, it can only be verse that is free of falsehood and exaggeration. Abu Nuwas once composed this verse in praise of the Caliph: "And you have so terrified the people of idolatry that even the seeds not yet created fear you," (that is, you have so terrified the idolaters that the seeds that have not yet been conceived fear you while still in their fathers' loins). People objected to this: how can seeds not yet conceived feel fear? And no one could give any answer on behalf of Abu Nuwas, except that some made it out to be correct by forced interpretation.

The advice to write truthful verse is not given because lying is a sin; no, it is given because effect, which is the final purpose of poetry, does not survive at all in falsehood. Besides, the progress of the sciences and of knowledge now taking place in the world is the ruin of false poetry. The humbug over which people of the old taste still nod their heads will before long be thought the raving of madmen.

# Natural Poetry

At this point it seems fitting to explain somewhat the term "natural poetry", which is now so often on people's lips. Some gentlemen think natural poetry means poetry associated with the "Naturis", or poetry in which the religious ideas of the "Naturis" are stated. [TN: *nechari*, "naturist", was the hostile nickname given to Sir Syed Ahmad Khan and his rationalist followers.] Some think natural poetry is that in which the progress or decline of the Muslims in particular, or of any nation at all, is described. But neither of these meanings has anything to do with natural poetry. By natural poetry is meant poetry that accords with nature, that is, with the natural order or with habit, in both words and meaning.

By according with nature in words is meant that the words of the verse and their construction and arrangement should, as far as possible, accord with the ordinary speech of the language in which the verse is written; for the ordinary speech and everyday idiom of every language has the force of nature, or second nature, for the people of the country where that language is spoken. So the further the expression of a verse is, without necessity, from ordinary speech and everyday idiom, the more unnatural it will be held to be. By according with nature in meaning is meant that the verse should state such things as always happen in the world, or ought to happen. So a verse whose subject is contrary to this will be held unnatural. For instance:

"One, with her stick placed under her chin, stood stock-still like the narcissus;"

"one stayed with a finger pressed between her teeth; another said, 'This house is ruined.'"

These two verses will be called natural, for the expression accords with ordinary speech, and the subject too is such that on the occasion where it is brought in, things always happen just so. Or, for instance:

"In love my heart and I take counsel thus, as one friend gives counsel to another."

This verse too will be called natural, for in love, and in every difficulty, a man takes counsel with his heart in just this way. Or, for instance:

Tell me, how shall I compare your cheek and tresses? The tulip has no such colour, the hyacinth no such scent.

This verse too will be called natural, for to the lover no colour or scent really seems better than, or equal to, the colour and scent of the beloved. Or, for instance:

"You are, as it were, with me when no one else is there."

This too will be held a natural verse, for the image of one to whom the heart has become attached is always before the eyes in solitude. Or, for instance:

"In a few days the heart will have had its fill; this storm has risen and it will subside."

"Desires will remain until the moment of death; this longing will not be filled in a day."

Though the subjects of these two verses seem opposed to each other, each accords with nature in its own place. In truth the ghost of lust and desire mounts to the head with great force and clamour, but very soon comes down; and in truth the appetite is never sated with the desires of the world. Or, for instance:

"When man grows used to grief, grief disappears; so many hardships fell on me that they became easy."

This verse too is natural, and points to a somewhat deep and hidden quality of human nature which, once stated, no one can deny.

All the verses above, as is plain, are such as should be called natural in both words and meaning. Now we give a few examples that cannot be called natural in words, or in meaning, or in both. For instance:

"Now the heart thinks of her cheek, now remembers her eyelashes; now there are thorns at my side, now a rose-garden at my side."

This verse can be called natural only in words, not in meaning. The image of the beloved may no doubt bring the lover joy as well as pain; but when there is joy, it should come from the image of both cheek and eyelashes, and when there is pain, from the image of both. It cannot be that the image of the eyelashes, which resemble thorns, puts thorns at one's side, and the image of the cheek, which resembles the rose, puts a rose-garden at one's side. Or, for instance:

Tell me, where is such heat in the substance of thought? A thought of wildness crossed my mind, and the desert burned.

However much heat there may be in the substance of thought, it is in no way possible that when the thought of roaming the desert comes into it, the desert itself should burst into flame. Or, for instance:

What delicacy! When you broke a flower from the rose-branch, the fire of the rose raised blisters on your hand.

However delicate one may be, it is not possible that the fire of the rose, that is, the touch of the rose itself, should raise blisters on the hand. Or, for instance:

Where one slain by your coldness lies buried, there the camphor tree mostly grows.

There can be only as much coolness in coldness of heart as there is in the word "cold"; and then for the dust of one slain by it to have so much power that a camphor tree grows from it is mere words upon words, with no trace or sign of meaning at all.

In every language natural poetry has always been the portion of the ancients. But in the first class of the ancients poetry does not reach the rank of acceptance. Their second class makes it shapely, casts it in a mould and shows it in an attractive and charming form, yet keeps its natural state intact in that attractiveness and charm. After them the round of the later poets begins. If these do not step outside imitation of the ancients, and stay confined within the same circle of thoughts the ancients set forth, and never raise their eyes from the scene of nature that was before the ancients to look in another direction, their poetry gradually declines from its natural state, until they fall very far from the straight road of nature. The comparison should be understood thus. In a place where people used to eat whole, raw, unsalted black gram or green gram soaked in water, one cook boiled it in water, added salt and fed it to them. They thought it a great boon compared with their ordinary food. A second cook had the gram split, washed the lentils, added suitable spices and ghee and prepared a dish. Now for a third cook, if he wishes to show his mastery in cooking lentils alone, no other chance of variety is left than to put in more chillies, sourness and ghee than is proper, and so make people fall for his spicy pot.

We try to make the same point clear in another way. Suppose that in the Persian language, on which Urdu poetry has been founded, those who first wrote the *ghazal* must surely have made the causes and motives of love and affection, in a purely natural and plain way, the beloved's face, beauty and loveliness, glance, coquetry and manner and so on. After them people stated these same things in the dress of figure and metaphor; for instance, they expressed the glance and eyebrow, or the wink, coquetry and grace, figuratively as the blade and the sword; and through this novelty and freshness the subject became more subtle and pleasing.

When the later poets fell upon this same subject and could lay hands on no metaphor better than that of the ancients, and the thought of producing novelty seized them, they set aside the figurative sense of blade and sword and began to mean by it a real Sirohi or pure-bred sword, which has hilt, edge, point, temper, lustre and scabbard; which stays in its sheath; which is hung from the neck in a baldric; which wounds; which hacks off pieces; which takes off heads; which sheds blood; which cuts through a target; whose edge may be sharp or blunt; with which the killer's hand may tire of striking; which may slip from the killer's hand and fall; for whose victim a case may be brought in court; for which retaliation may be taken; for which blood-money may be paid to the heirs. In short, every property a real iron sword can have, they began to prove of it.

Or, for instance, the earlier poets had expressed falling in love with someone figuratively as "giving the heart", "losing the heart" or "selling the heart". Little by little the later poets made the heart into a thing that, like a jewel or a fruit, can be snatched from the hand; can be taken back; can be lost and found; whose price is sometimes haggled over; which is given if the bargain is struck and otherwise not; which the beloved sometimes takes from the lover, puts in some niche and forgets; which by chance falls into the lover's hands, and he, unseen, carries it off; then a search is made for it at the beloved's house and the lover gives no receipt for it; sometimes it vanishes in a gathering of friends in the twinkling of an eye, the whole house is searched and no trace of it is found; and by chance, when the beloved combs her hair, it drops out like a louse; sometimes it is so lost that every curl and every lock of the beloved's tresses is searched for it, but no clue is found anywhere; sometimes it is sold to the beloved on the terms of a sale with option, on condition that she keep it if she likes it and otherwise return it; and sometimes it is put up for auction, to go to whoever bids highest.

Or, for instance, the earlier poets had figuratively called the beloved a hunter because she, as it were, hunts people's hearts. Little by little the later poets applied to her all the rules of a real hunter. Now she here sets a net and catches birds; there shoots them down with arrows; here shuts them alive in a cage; there plucks their feathers; here slaughters them and leaves them writhing on the ground. Whenever she sets out for the forest with bow and arrow, all the birds and fowl of the forest beg her for mercy; she has roasted and eaten hundreds of birds on skewers; scores of cages of ringdoves, pigeons, larks and quails always hang at her door; all the bird-catchers hold their ears before her. [TN: holding one's ears is a gesture of admitting defeat or apologising.]

Or, for instance, the earlier poets had figuratively expressed the love of God, or the spiritual love that one human being can have for another, as the intoxication of wine, and in keeping with this had used the words cup and flask, jar and measure, cupbearer and wine-seller and so on as metaphors; or some Sufi poets had, by way of good omen, made wine a means of reaching the Goal, because for a little while it frees the mind from the ties of this abode of illusion. Little by little wine and all its accompaniments came to be used in their literal senses, until the *mushaira* (poetry gathering) became, without exaggeration, a distiller's shop. One says, "Bring it!", another, "Bring more!", a third, "If there's no cup, pour it into my cupped hands!" Some are babbling and some are bawling; one jeers at the preacher, one makes a grab at the ascetic's beard, one knocks off the Shaikh's turban. Young and old, ignorant and learned, libertine and pious, are all dyed in one colour. Everyone is stretching in the languor of the hangover; wherever you look there is the cry, "Thirst! Thirst!"

Or, for instance, the ancients had taken thinness of body to be a necessary result of the sorrow of love or the blow of separation, and had described it in some moving way. Little by little the later poets brought it to such a pass that when the sweeper sweeps, he gathers up the wretched lover along with the straw and rubbish; when the beloved rises in the morning, she cannot find the lover in the bed because of his thinness, and is forced to shake out the bedding and look, in case something falls to the floor; death goes about searching for the lover but cannot see him anywhere because of his thinness; on the plain of the Resurrection the angels go searching on every side and the Judge of the Day of Reckoning sits waiting, but because of his thinness no trace of the lover is found anywhere.

In the same way the later poets carried every subject that the ancients had composed in a natural way beyond the frontier of nature into another world. Making the beloved's mouth narrower and narrower, they wiped it clean off the page of the world. Making the waist thinner and thinner, they made it wholly non-existent. Making the tresses longer and longer, they made them longer even than the life of Khizr. Increasing jealousy more and more, they became suspicious even of God. Stretching the night of separation further and further, they joined it to eternity. In short, when the later poets make the very subjects the earlier ones composed their cover and their bedding, they are forced to give up natural poetry and make a bull out of a speck of dirt. [TN: *mail ka bail banana*, "to make an ox of a speck of dirt", the Urdu equivalent of "to make a mountain out of a molehill".]

To impress this more firmly on the mind (in what state poetry begins, how the second class of the ancients then refines it in that same natural state, and what the later poets make of it after them), it seems fitting to quote a few examples from the verse of each class of Urdu poets.

First example: Shah Abru, who is counted among the very first class of Urdu poets, describes the feeling that arises in the lover's heart on seeing the beloved thus:

When he met my eyes with his eyes, he settled into my heart;
with his warm glance, the one with lovely eyes set a kind of fire in my heart.

Mirza Rafi Sauda, who should be counted in the second class, describes the same feeling thus:

Sauda, the one for whom you are in this state is not so much as that; who knows in what moment you saw her?

Mir Taqi, the contemporary of Mirza Rafi, expresses the same feeling thus:

Even this much desire is not good, Mir; pray that now, when I see her, I may not feel too much love.

Khwaja Haidar Ali Atish, who has been thought to belong to the fourth or fifth class, states the same feeling thus:

When my heart played the backgammon of love with the beloved's beauty, my sixes were so loosed that I was left checkmated. [TN: *shashdar*, the blocked position in backgammon, also means "dumbfounded".]

Second example: Shah Abru describes the length of time the lover feels in the days of separation thus:

What can I say, beloved, of the excess of the time of separation? Every hour of that tyrant that passed over us was an age.

Mir has expressed the same subject thus:

Without you every moment has become a year for us; what an age has come upon us, my friend, little by little!

Nasikh, who is of the fifth class, composes this subject thus:

Instead of the camphor of dawn we need the camphor of embalming; friends, this is the night of separation, not just a dark night. [TN: the "camphor of dawn" is the white first light of morning; camphor was also used in washing the dead.]

That is: the night of separation will not pass until it has taken our life; so it is useless to expect the camphor of dawn; in its place camphor is needed for washing and embalming the corpse. Although in subject all three verses can be called natural, since in the state of longing and waiting it is possible for every hour to seem an age to the lover and every moment a year, and possible for the lover, wearied by the length of the night of separation, to despair of living, yet Nasikh's manner of expression is so far from the ordinary speech of Urdu that it can in no way be called natural expression.

Third example: Shah Hatim, who has been counted in the first class, describes the longing to meet the friend and the eagerness to see him thus:

Life has become a headache, Hatim; when will my beloved meet me?

Mir has composed the same subject thus:

May God grant union with her! Mir, what all the heart desires!

Sauda says:

The heart longs, O breeze, to reach the beloved's lane with you, mingled in the dust.

Munshi Amir Ahmad Amir, a famous poet of the present class, expresses the same subject thus:

With the eye of my heart wide open, like a footprint, I watch for you on every road.

In this example too, although all three verses can be called natural in thought, the last verse, compared with Hatim, Mir and the Mirza, shows plain artifice and contrivance in its expression, and the expression has ceased to be natural. If one searched further, far more open and plain examples than this could be found in plenty.

From what has been said above it should by no means be supposed that the poetry of the later poets is always unnatural. No; it is possible that among the later poets there may be some who try their nature in a field other than the ancients' arena, or widen that arena somewhat, or bring more smoothness, suppleness, breadth and clarity into the language than the earlier poets. Thus we see that in Lucknow Mir Anis has carried the *marsiya* to boundless heights, and Nawab Mirza Shauq has made the *masnavi* much purer in language and expression; in the same way in Delhi Zauq, Zafar and above all Dagh have brought great breadth, clarity and dash into the language of the *ghazal*, as we shall describe in some detail further on.

# Using the Language Correctly

The third thing is to use the Urdu language correctly and cleanly. Although Urdu is current, more or less, in every part of India, it may be that the people of some regions can compose verse more easily in their own particular language than in Urdu.

So if any of our countrymen wishes to write verse in his own particular language, nothing could be better, for there can be no better or easier instrument for expressing thoughts than the mother tongue. Lord Macaulay says that "no man has ever produced a fine work that is a body of thought except in a language of which he does not remember at all when he learned it or how, and in which he talked for a long time before he knew its grammar." He writes that "great and able men of Rome wrote verses in French, but not one of those verses has remained as a memorial on the page of time. Many gifted and talented men of England compiled collections of verse in Latin, but not one of those collections, not even Milton's, can be counted of the first rank as poetry; indeed, it has no distinction even in the second rank." [TN: Hali paraphrases Macaulay; "Rome" here is presumably Italy, whose writers produced verse in French.] So since the poetic gift is a natural and innate thing, it will be more fitting, in putting it to use, to employ an instrument that is itself as good as natural and innate, and that can be no other language than the mother tongue.

But the Urdu language is, by general agreement, wider than all the other living languages of India and fitter for expressing thoughts; it can be generally spoken and understood in every part of India; it has the better claim to be made the national language of India and to be developed as far as possible; and to acquire it and gain sufficient skill in it is not as hard for the people of India as it is in other languages that are not one's mother tongue.

Besides, of all the living languages of India there seems to be none at present in which a store of verse equal to Urdu's exists; so it seems more fitting that whoever among our countrymen takes up writing verse should make Urdu the instrument for expressing his thoughts.

In India, as is generally accepted, there are only two cities whose Urdu is held authoritative: Delhi and Lucknow. The language of Delhi is held to be the standard language because Urdu arose and grew in that region. The language of Lucknow is held to be authoritative because, from the beginning of the decline of the Mughal empire, countless families of the gentry of Delhi went over a long period to settle in Lucknow and stayed there for good. So no city of India has had so much chance of intercourse with the people of Delhi as Lucknow has, so that a particular likeness has arisen between the language of the two cities, and apart from certain particular words and idioms no appreciable difference is to be seen in the speech, tone and accent of the two places.

No language can spread evenly through a whole country until the following means are provided in the country: (1) the preparation of a reliable and comprehensive dictionary of that language; (2) the compiling of a comprehensive grammar of it; (3) the writing, compiling and publishing of books in verse and prose in it in abundance; (4) the circulation of newspapers and journals in that language in every part and corner of the country. It is plain that to this day no comprehensive and authoritative dictionary of Urdu has been prepared, nor has any grammar of it been written from which enough help in learning the language can be hoped for. The practice of writing and compiling in Urdu, and the publication of newspapers and the like, has mostly come about in the last twenty or twenty-five years, and so short a time cannot be enough for the spread of a language.

It is, indeed, a matter of great joy that the more Urdu literature spreads in the country, the more the skill of writing the Urdu language, in verse and prose, generally grows in the various parts of India; but for expressing poetic thoughts, and especially for fulfilling the duties of natural poetry, in the standard language, such limited means may perhaps not be enough. Although a comprehensive and authoritative dictionary too, if there were one, could help much in fulfilling this aim, the most useful thing in this matter is the company of native speakers, and living in their society long enough for their words and idioms to come, unawares, to the tongue in appreciable measure. But since such a chance is hard for everyone to get, it is necessary to study the verse of native-speaking poets as much as possible, with care and attention, again and again; not with the intention of imitating them in thoughts and subjects, but with an eye to how they use words and idioms, and in what styles and forms they express their thoughts.

Ibn Khaldun says that "a non-Arab can, by constant practice in the speech of the eloquent Arabs, become worthy of being counted a native speaker." So the people of India have all the better claim to be regarded as native speakers through constant practice in the speech of native speakers.

Although the verse of many fine poets of Delhi has not yet been published, such as Khwaja Mir Asar, Shah Nasir, Mir Mamnun, Ma'ruf, Arif and others, though the full and bulky *diwans* of these worthies exist; and in Lucknow too it is no wonder if the verse of some of its authorities has not been published; yet the number of those whose *diwans* and *kulliyat* (collected works) have been published is not small either. Of these one should read especially all kinds of verse, *ghazal* or *masnavi*, *qasida* or *qit'a* and *ruba'i*, or *wasokht*, of Mir, Sauda, Dard, Jur'at, Insha, Mushafi, Mir Hasan, Nasikh, Atish, Wazir, Ghalib, Zauq, Zafar, Shefta, Dagh, Salik, Shauq [1], Rind, Asir, Barq, Amir and others; and most important and necessary of all is the study of the *marsiyas* of Khaliq, Zamir, Anis, Dabir, Munis and others. Although some of the *diwans* and *masnavis* mentioned above are full from end to end of idle thoughts and absurd subjects, those whose concern is purely with the language should shut their eyes to and overlook the idleness of the thoughts and the absurdity of the subjects, and with great patience and forbearance confine their effort to words and idioms, manner of expression and style of statement, and act on the maxim "Take what is clear and leave what is muddy." Apart from verse, one should also profit from the books that authoritative native speakers have written in Urdu literature on learned, historical, religious and moral subjects.

Those who think themselves the owners of the Urdu language, that is, the people of Delhi or the people of Lucknow, should not take pride in the fact that people follow their language and imitate their idiom. They should remember that if they do not look after their language, do not provide the means to preserve it, do not gather and arrange its words and idioms with great care, and do not develop its verse and prose in keeping with the taste of the age, then the part of their language of which they are proud, and which distinguishes their Urdu from that of the rest of India, will be wiped off the page of time like a word written in error; and this good-and-bad Urdu, which is spreading through the country by way of the common newspapers and new writings and which they have so far looked on with contempt, will within half a century at most become the standard and eloquent language of the country. Do they not know that in Arabia, from the time verse and prose fell into neglect and the masters of Arabic verse and prose became the people of foreign lands, the classical Arabic of which the Arabs were proud gradually took leave of the literary world; and the very corrupt language that the pure Arabs had looked on with contempt spread over the whole of Arabic literature and through Syria, Anatolia, Egypt, Barbary, the Sudan and elsewhere, until today that very language is held to be the standard and eloquent Arabic? Such an end seems in store for the language of Delhi and Lucknow, if it is not soon looked after. In Delhi, which should be called the birthplace and native soil of refined Urdu, authors, poets and prose-writers have ceased to be born. A few souls of the old people are left, who should be thought of as lamps at dawn; after them there seems to be utter silence. The state of Lucknow does not seem outwardly so; there poetry is heard to flourish far more than in Delhi; novels and dramas are constantly published from there through the country; but alas, its step does not keep pace with the march of the age; the further it goes forward, the further it moves from the road of progress.

To gain command of Urdu it is not enough merely to follow the language of Delhi or Lucknow; it is also necessary to acquire at least a middling competence in Arabic and Persian, and some mastery of Hindi *bhasha* as well. The foundation of the Urdu language, as is known, has been laid on Hindi *bhasha*; all its verbs, all its particles and the greater part of its nouns are taken from Hindi; and the foundation of Urdu poetry rests on Persian poetry, which is itself drawn from Arabic poetry; and besides, a very large part of the nouns in the Urdu language are taken from Arabic and Persian. So the Urdu poet who knows no Hindi *bhasha* at all and drives his cart by Arabic and Persian alone is as if he wished to bring his cart to its destination without wheels; and the one who is a stranger to Arabic and Persian and takes up this burden relying only on Hindi *bhasha* or merely his mother tongue is pushing a cart to which no oxen have been yoked.

One more point concerning the language is worth attention. For natural poetry, as is plain, our present language is not sufficient, so it is necessary that it be widened. So the way the people of Lucknow keep narrowing the circle of the language day by day is wholly against the demand of the time. In Lucknow a gentleman wrote a treatise on verse and composition in the year 1869, in which he has listed a little over fifty words that he himself, or other people of Lucknow, think ought to be given up. Some of them are peculiar to Lucknow alone, and the people of Delhi never speak so, such as *andhiyara* in place of *andhere*, *ujiyala* in place of *ujale*, *kyunkar se* in place of *kyunkar*. We too think it very fitting to give up such words, for this brings the language of Lucknow and Delhi into agreement. If the people of Lucknow are ready to give up such words, we can offer many more. Giving up such words makes no great difference to the breadth of the language either.

In the same treatise some words are declared fit to be given up that are used and spoken against the grammar of the original language or the analogy of its lexicon, such as *mausam* with *a* after the *s*, or *minnat* with *a* after the *n*, or *insha* in the measure of *zina* and *fakih*, whereas by Arabic grammar or lexicon *mausim* is in the measure of *masjid*, *minnat* with *i*, and *nash'a* in the measure of *wahdat*. [TN: Hali is quoting the treatise's examples of words whose Urdu pronunciation differs from the Arabic; the printed vowel-marks and measures are given as printed and are not all clear.] But in truth this is a mistake that our Arabic scholars often fall into from ignorance of the science of language. They do not know that the words of one language, when they pass into another, can never keep their original form, save as God wills. Why go far? In our own Urdu thousands of words of Sanskrit, Prakrit and *bhasha* have entered; yet rarely indeed will one find any that keep their original form. For instance *ghar*, *ghara*, *ujla*, *adha*, *andhera*, *asra*, *ankh*, *age*, *ungli*: all these words are corrupted from the following Sanskrit words, namely *grih*, *ghat*, *ujjval*, *ardh*, *andhkar*, *ashray*, *akshi*, *agre*, *anguli*. In the same way hundreds of words of Prakrit and *bhasha* are used in our language contrary to their origin, but since people do not know their origin, they think them correct and speak and use them without hesitation; but in Arabic or Persian, with which they have some acquaintance, wherever they see any word of it used in someone's Urdu verse or prose contrary to the original language, at once they turn up their noses. Yet they themselves use many Arabic words contrary to their original form, for instance *ghash* in place of *ghashy*, *musalman* in place of *muslim*, *mahafa* in place of *mihaffa*, *ghalati* in place of *ghalat*, *ziyadati* in place of *ziyadat*, *salamati* in place of *salamat*, *hadya* in place of *hadiyya*, *mughilan* in place of *umm ghailan*, *muhaba* and *mudara* and the like in place of *muhabat* and *mudarat* and the like. On the same analogy Persian words too are often spoken wrongly in Urdu. The people of Iran use hundreds of Arabic words with wrong pronunciation or in wrong senses, for instance *summ* and *bukm* in place of *asamm* and *abkam*, *hur* in place of *haura*, *abdal* in place of *badil*, *fuzuli* in place of *fuzul*, *huzuri* in place of *huzur*, *qiran* in place of *qur'an*, *mashshata* in place of *mashita*, *muwasa* and *mufaja* and the like in place of *muwasat* and *mufajat* and the like.

In English words have been taken from all the languages of the world, but no word has been kept in its original form; for instance, in place of *khalifa*, *tarjuman*, *makhzan*, *nawab*, *ta'rif*, *qutn*, *amir al-bahr*, *Usman*, *firdaus*, *manara*, *sipahi*, *shaghal*, *karwan*, *shakar*, *qirmizi*, which are words of the Arabic and Persian languages, they speak and use caliph, dragoman, magazine, nabob, tariff, cotton, admiral, Ottoman, paradise, minaret, sepoy, jackal, caravan, sugar, crimson.

In the same way, so far as one can survey, the words of one language do not keep their original form when they pass into another. So since words like *mausam*, *minnat* or *insha* are current on the tongues of all of us, select and common, why should they not be used in Urdu verse and prose? The point is that it is a mistake even to think that such words, taken into Urdu from Arabic, Persian or English and generally used contrary to their original form, are in their present form words of Arabic, Persian or English; no, they should be thought of as words of Urdu that are in origin taken from Arabic, Persian or English. To give up such words as wrong and to force people to use them according to their origin is exactly like forbidding people to say *lalten* and forcing them to say "lantern"; or stopping them from saying *ghara* and insisting that they say *ghat*.

There is a very great difference between *ghalat al-amm* (an error in general use) and *ghalat al-awamm* (an error of the common people). Wrong words that become current on the tongues of both select and common belong to *ghalat al-amm*. To speak such words is not merely permissible but better than speaking correctly. But wrong words that are current only on the tongues of the common and ignorant, and not on those of the select and the educated, ought certainly to be given up, such as saying *majaz* for *mizaj*, *munkar* for *munkir*, *nakhalis* for *khalis*, *be-nahaq* for *nahaq*, *darwza* for *darwaza*, *nakhsa* for *nuskha* and so on. [TN: several of these pairs differ only in vowels not shown in the script; they are given as printed.]

Besides these, many words have been declared fit to be given up that the earlier poets commonly used, that some poets of Delhi still use, and that, if one looks at everyday speech, select and common in Delhi go on speaking to this day, such as *ta'in*, *kabhu*, *kisu*, *ankhen*, *akhirash*, *pahnana* (in place of *pinhana*), *batlana*, *dikhlana* and the like; *sada* (meaning "always"); *talak*; *seti*; *mat* in place of the negative particle; *bin* (meaning "without"); *pa* (in place of *par*); *kije*, *dije*, *lije* in place of *kijiye*, *dijiye*, *lijiye*; *mira*, *tira* in place of *mera* and *tera*; *par* meaning "but"; *ik* in place of *ek*; *zor* meaning "strange" or "extremely".

These words may have been given up in Lucknow, or may yet be, but in Delhi and its neighbourhood they are spoken more or less constantly; and the demand of the age is that they will always be spoken, and if they are not spoken they will certainly remain in use in writing. In prose perhaps some of these words may not be needed, but in verse there will always be need of them (though there is some question whether there will still be need of verse).

Those gentlemen who give general instructions to give up such words are like people who, living themselves in Multan, will not allow those going to Kashmir to carry a bundle of warm clothing with them.

To argue further on this subject seems useless; for if the need with an eye to which we think it unwise to narrow the circle of the language is really coming, these restrictions will lift of themselves, and people, instead of narrowing and limiting their own language, will be forced to go begging from other languages; and if the idea of the progress of Urdu literature is as far-fetched an idea as that of the learned, civil and moral progress of the Muslims, then this discussion will be not premature but out of season.

# In What State One Should Turn to Composing Verse

The fourth thing is in what state one should turn to composing verse. Some are of the opinion that at night before sleep, and by day before the mid-morning meal, the nature opens more readily to verse. A philosopher has said that "nothing so tames wild thoughts as running water, solitude and a high seat." But in our view there is no occasion or place better for composing verse than when the passion of some subject arises of itself in the poet's heart; then garden and forest, town and wilderness, meadow and bare plain, running water and barren ground are all alike to him. Abu Nuwas would not set about composing verse until bouquets of flowers were placed before him. One day Abu al-Atahiya asked him, "Can you not think of a subject without them? I compose my verse in the privy." Abu Nuwas said, "That is why it stinks." But in our view composing verse needs neither bouquets nor sitting in the privy; it needs only passion and ardour, which depend on no restriction or condition. People asked Kuthayyir [2], "Why have you given up writing verse?" He said, "Youth, which used to raise high spirits in my heart, has passed; Azza, who used to warm my heart, has died; and Abd al-Aziz, from whom I hoped for reward, is no more; what is left now to make me write verse?" As it were, he hinted that until there is some kind of passion and ardour in the heart, verse cannot be produced. Farazdaq used to say, "In a state of hopelessness and despair I am the most poetic of men; but at times my state is such that pulling out a tooth with tongs seems easier to me than composing a verse," that is, verse cannot be produced without the demand of nature and the passion of the heart. The poet al-Khuraimi was asked, "Why is it that the odes of praise you wrote for Muhammad ibn Mansur in his lifetime are finer than the elegies you now write on him?" He admitted it and answered most honestly: "Our hopes and desires are stronger and more forceful than our loyalty and our sense of duty; hope made us write the odes, and loyalty makes us write the elegies, so a clear difference appears between the two." In short, until something stings the heart, the imaginative faculty is not generous in supplying subjects. But passion can survive in a poet's verse only so long as nothing hinders his freedom, or his free nature cares nothing for any fear or restraint; otherwise it may be that he cannot express well and finely a subject whose passion is really present in his nature.

Freedom is hindered in several ways. Sometimes fear of someone keeps the poet from expressing his thoughts freely; thus of Kuthayyir of Azza and al-Kumait ibn Zaid, who were most staunch Shi'is, it has been said that what they composed in praise of the Banu Hashim is not, as poetry, of the same rank as their odes in praise of the Banu Umayya. But such hindrance sometimes stirs the passion of a free-natured poet all the more. People were put to death even for writing elegies on Ja'far the Barmakid; yet some wrote his elegies with such passion and fervour that they are remembered to this day.

Sometimes the pressure of society, or greed and covetousness, or some other inducement turns the current of his nature from the straight road in another direction. This calamity has befallen most of our poets, and has turned many promising and bright-natured poets into jesters, obscene versifiers and buffoons.

Sometimes a fixed obligation is laid on the poet, so that he is forced to write something or other: for instance, writing an ode of congratulation on every occasion or festival, or producing a *ghazal* in the *tarah* (set metre and rhyme) of the *mushaira* every week or ten days. Although outwardly this seems no hindrance to freedom, reflection on human nature shows that such obligations put a stone in the way of a moving cart. Just as man is by nature greedy for what is forbidden, so he is by nature averse to what is imposed [3]. So long as Insha'allah Khan was unbridled, he would let off new jests and quips every day at the court of Sa'adat Ali Khan and invent witticisms at every word; but when Sa'adat Ali Khan laid on him the obligation to tell two new things every day that had never been heard before, this same Insha'allah Khan went about the streets and lanes like a madman asking boys, "Brother, tell me something new!"; and in the end, in this very search, he went completely mad. It is told of a powerful poet of Europe that when he had sold the copyright of his future works to some publisher, he used to say that this contract had stopped up his nature; whenever he sat down to write anything, at once the thought would come that now whatever he wrote he wrote not from the invention of his heart but to fulfil his contract; and at this thought his nature sank of itself.

In any case, as far as possible one should not take up the pen to write on any subject until its sting has touched the heart. Verse composed, or a poem produced, out of rivalry with someone, at someone's request, under someone's pressure, or for some other compulsion, without the demand of nature and inner ardour, is very hard to fill with effect and force.
