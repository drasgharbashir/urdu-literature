# Poetry Flourishes in Ages That Are Not Refined

In any case, if poetry does not go wholly beyond reality and rest on mere baseless fancies, power and the gift of sinking into the heart are part of its nature. But the opinions that most scholars of our time have formed about poetry lean towards the view that civilisation has a bad effect on poetry. The more exact knowledge becomes, the more imagination, on which poetry is founded, declines; and the habit of probing, which goes hand in hand with the advance of knowledge, is deadly poison to poetry. They say that so long as society is half-civilised, its knowledge and information limited and its grasp of causes slight, life itself seems a story. The account of a life, which is simply a chain of events, if told even in a plain and simple way in a half-civilised society, of itself arouses fear here, wonder there and excitement elsewhere; and on these things poetry is founded. But when refinement spreads, these springs dry up, and where they do not dry up, they are held back with great care, so that they are not laughed at.

A great supporter of this view says: "Poetry casts the same veil over the heart that the magic lantern casts over the eye. Just as the show of that lantern reaches its full perfection only in a completely dark room, so poetry shows its full wonder only in a dark age. And just as all the shows of the magic lantern vanish the moment light comes in, so, as the bounds of reality grow clearer and brighter and the veils of conjecture are lifted, the conjured splendours of poetry melt away; for two contradictory things, truth and illusion, cannot exist together."

# The Example of Firdausi

To make this point plainer, consider the following example. There was a time when what Firdausi wrote in the *Shahnama* about the strength and valour of his hero Rustam made people believe in Rustam's extraordinary greatness. Hearing of his strength and courage, they marvelled, and in the hearts of the hearers sympathy for him and hostility to his rivals arose of themselves. But now, as knowledge grows, that spell breaks a little more each day, and the time is coming when Rustam will be thought no more than an ordinary man.

# Poetry Can Survive in a Refined Age

Although the view of poetry given above is true to some extent, it should not be accepted without thought either. Those who oppose it say: "Although with the advance of knowledge the meanings of words have become narrower, and the belief in the reality of many things has faded, languages are becoming more flexible than before and fitter to express most purposes. Many comparisons have no doubt become useless in this age, but the mind has not lost the power to invent new ones. It is true that science and mechanics deaden passionate thoughts; yet through them a lasting store of new comparisons and images, which did not exist before, has been provided for the poet and is still being provided." They do not accept that the power of imagination grows weaker as society advances. They hold that so long as man believes that our bond with eternity is firm; so long as countless causes and hindrances that cannot be denied surround us on every side; so long as love rules the human heart and can make the life story of every human being an interesting tale; so long as patriotic feeling lives in nations; so long as mankind stands ready to join together in sympathy; and so long as the events and accidents that happen in life from time to time set joy or grief in motion, there is no fear that the power of imagination will diminish. "And there is still less fear, so long as the mine of nature lies open, that the poet's store will run out. Yet there is no doubt that the striking things of nature were picked out by the workmen who came first; and since to them these things were new, and so wonderful, no one now can outdo their wondering descriptions of them."

# The Connection of Poetry with Morals

Just as poetry inflames the passions of the self, it also brings spiritual joys to life; and the connection between man's pure spiritual joys and his morals is so plain that it hardly needs stating. Although poetry does not teach and train directly, as ethics does, it may fairly be called the deputy and representative of ethics. That is why one great order of the revered Sufis holds *sama'* (listening to devotional music), whose greatest part and main pillar is poetry, to be a means of drawing near to God and of purifying the self and cleansing the inner being.

# The Greatness of Poetry

A European scholar says: "Poetry awakens the powers that fall asleep through absorption in worldly occupations, and it freshens again those pure and innocent feelings of our childhood that were free of the stain of self-interest. Practice and long habit in worldly affairs no doubt sharpen the mind, but they kill the heart altogether. When, in poverty, men struggle for bare subsistence, or, in wealth, for rank and office, and selfishness is seen on every side in the world, then, when man met with hard trials, how would it be if he had no remedy that could quietly, yet with great strength, comfort and refresh the heart, acting as a balm in poverty and an antidote in wealth? This property God has placed in poetry. It takes us out of the circle of the senses and makes past and future states prevail over our present one. Poetry acts on morals not through reason alone but mostly through the mind and the perceptions. So every nation can gain fine moral qualities from poetry according to the quickness of its mind and the height of its perception. National pride, national honour, keeping one's word and covenant, carrying out all one's resolves without fear, bearing hardship with steadfastness, refusing to look at gains that cannot be won by honest means, and all such qualities, by whose presence a whole nation shines in the eyes of the world and by whose absence the greatest national state is despised in the world's eyes: if they are not created in a nation by poetry alone, their foundation is certainly laid in it by poetry. If Plato had succeeded in banishing the poets from his imaginary constitution, he would have done morals no favour at all. The result would have been a cold, selfish and ungenerous society, in which no work and no effort was ever made from the mere stirring and ardour of the heart, without hope of gain or calculation. That is why the whole world honours and reveres the poets, who, by the seal of Solomon that the imaginative faculty has put into their hands, have stirred and roused in man an impulse that is itself goodness, or leads to goodness."

# Poetry Follows Society

Yet, for all that has been said in support of poetry, it is possible that under the pressure of society or the demands of the age poetry may fall into such a state that, instead of reforming the morals of the nation, it becomes a powerful instrument for spoiling and ruining them. It is a rule that as the thoughts, opinions, habits, desires, inclinations and taste of society change, the state of poetry changes with them. And this change is quite unnoticed, for the poet does not change his colour on purpose after looking at the state of society; he changes of himself, along with society. What has been said of Shifa'i of Isfahan, that poetry ruined his learning and satire ruined his poetry, came from this same pressure of society; and that Ubaid Zakani [1] gave up learning and scholarship and took to ribald verse was the same demand of the age. Just as the relish of flattery and offerings and presents gradually corrupts the intentions of a pious and upright judge, so the applause of the court and the taste for rewards quietly bring a free-minded and passionate poet down to the bard's begging praise, to lies and flattery, or to ribaldry and mockery, until he comes to think this the perfection of poetry.

The boundless bounty of absolute kings, whose hand no one can stay and for whom the whole treasury is pocket money, is deadly poison to the freedom of poets. The poet, who should have been the crown of the nation and its pride, arrives at the door of a slave of lust and greed like a beggar, crying out and calling "Something, for God's sake!" At first, in praise and eulogy, truth is not wholly set aside, because at the start of a nation's rise those who are praised usually deserve praise, and the essence of freedom does not vanish from the poet's nature all at once. But when the real events run out, and the task of singing praise is laid on the poet for good, his poetry comes to rest only on fastening false attributions. Then, when the cycle of the sun of fortune, whose natural life in personal rule rarely exceeds a hundred years, draws to its close, and the kings and nobles no longer have the virtues that would earn them the thanks and praise of the people and free them from need of the poets' eulogies, they can think of nothing but the poets' fulsome praise to puff up their vanity. So they have to value the poets all the more. This makes false poetry flourish still further. Then many who are not poets, seeing poets constantly receiving rich rewards, robes of honour and gifts, have to force themselves to become poets. But since their natures have no stuff of poetic originality and invention, they imitate the real poets in a very clumsy way, until, just as a portrait of old age bears no likeness to a portrait of childhood, the face of poetry is gradually disfigured, and nothing is left of poetry's worth except that it gains the favour of kings.

# What Was Thought of Poetry in the Fourth Century of the Hijra

Mirza Muhammad Tahir Nasrabadi writes in his *tazkira* (biographical anthology of poets) that one day, in the time of his vizierate, scholars and poets had gathered as usual in the assembly of the Sahib Ibn Abbad Taliqani. In the course of talk the subject of poetry came up. Some praised poetry and some condemned it. Those who condemned it said that poetry mostly consists of praise or blame, and both are founded on lies. Then Abu Muhammad Khazin, a man of great learning and scholarship, said in defence of poetry: "The greatest merit of poetry is that, though we are skilled in every science and art, none of them can bring us success; poetry alone is the thing through which we gain a place of favour with kings and viziers. As for poetry mostly containing a great deal of lying and exaggeration, yes, of course it does; but when this copper (that is, the lie) is gilded with the gold of poetry, it takes on the colour of pure gold, and the beauty of the poetry outweighs the badness of the lie." Everyone liked this, and the debate ended.

Besides showing that in the time of the Sahib Ibn Abbad, that is, in the fourth century of the Hijra, our poetry was thought merely a means of gaining the favour of kings and nobles, this story also shows that lying and exaggeration had become part of the very essence of poetry.

# The Great Number of Muslim Poets

A European historian, speaking of Arabic literature, writes that the Arab nation alone has produced so many poets that the poets of all the nations of the world together cannot equal them in number. By the poets of the Arab nation he evidently means only the poets of the Arabic language. From this one can judge what the number of Muslim poets would come to if Persian, Turkish, Pashto and Urdu, which are languages of the Muslims in particular, were added to Arabic. And even if by the Arab nation he meant Muslim poets in general, it is no less astonishing that their number should exceed that of the poets of all the nations of the world.

# The Cause of This Great Number

There seem to be two causes of this great number. One is the custom of the person praised giving rewards and gifts for praise and eulogy, because of which everyone with a natural sense of metre thought of taking up poetry, whether he was fit to be a poet or not. The other is the custom of hearers applauding verse of every grade, fittingly or not. This second cause did even more than the first to encourage the writing of verse, for only those who needed rewards and gifts were greedy for them, but in the wish to hear applause kings, nobles and the poor were all alike. These two causes harmed the poetry of the Muslims from two sides. When rewards and gifts went equally to the deserving and the undeserving, and showers of applause fell on verse of every grade, in season and out of season, those who truly deserved reward and praise lost heart, and the high poetic gifts placed in their natures could not show themselves as they should because the buyers had no discernment; while the undeserving took heart and got the chance to spread their stench through the nation and to do violence to poetry.

# The Esteem of Poets in Arabia

Poets have always been esteemed throughout the world. States have always valued them and nations have encouraged them. In Arabia the poet was held to be the honour of his people. When a man of some tribe became distinguished in poetry, people of other tribes would come to congratulate that tribe, and all would rejoice together. The women of the tribe would come wearing their wedding jewels and sing boastful verses, that a man had been born among them who would uphold the honour of the whole tribe, guard their lineage and their speech, and carry their famous deeds down to their descendants. Poets were indulged so far that even if one made an impossible request, it was not refused outright. Once A'sha was passing through the lands of the Banu Amir with a great deal of wealth and goods, and for fear of robbers he stopped on the way with Alqama ibn Ulatha and asked for protection. Alqama gladly agreed. A'sha said, "Have you given me protection from jinn and men?" Alqama said, "Yes." A'sha said, "And from death?" He replied, "That is beyond possibility." A'sha left him in displeasure and went to Amir ibn al-Tufail, who promised both. A'sha said, "How have you protected me from death?" He said, "If you die under my protection, I will send your blood-money to your heirs." A'sha was delighted, wrote an ode in his praise and composed a satire on Alqama.

# The Esteem of Poets Is Useful in National States but Harmful under Personal Rule

Poets have been esteemed in the same way in other countries besides Arabia. In national states, where the king is not an absolute ruler, such patronage makes poetry flourish beyond measure. Until a poet is accepted by the whole nation he receives no support or help from the state; and only that poet can be accepted by the nation who fulfils the duties of poetry with complete freedom, without hope or fear. He neither cares for the patronage of the state nor fears being called to account by the king. But in absolute states the poet has at all costs to keep the court pleased and give up his freedom, until his true ardour and passion, without which poetry is a lifeless body, gradually turn to dust. He can neither praise anyone from the impulse of his heart nor satirise anyone with true feeling. Marwan ibn Abi Hafsa, a famous poet in the time of the Caliph al-Mahdi, wrote this verse in his elegy on Ma'n ibn Za'ida, whose courage and generosity were proverbial:

We said: Where shall we go after Ma'n? Bounty is gone, and there is no more bounty.

Al-Mahdi summoned him to court, made him recite this verse, and had him thrown out of the court with great indignity. It is written that after that no noble or caliph except Ja'far the Barmakid ever rewarded him. Wherever he took an ode, the answer came back: "Generosity went with Ma'n." For writing elegies on Ja'far the Barmakid, to whom a whole age, and especially the poets, owed gratitude, many poets were put to death by order of Harun. After most of the poets had been killed, al-Raqashi secretly wrote an elegy, at the end of which he says:

By God, were it not for fear of the informer, and of the Caliph's eye that never sleeps,
we would circle round your tomb and kiss it, as people kiss the Stone.

Translation: By God, were it not for fear of the tale-bearer and of the Caliph's wakeful eye, we would walk round your tomb and kiss it, as people kiss the Black Stone.

# Under Personal Rule a Poet's Freedom Brings Him Harm

In such an age, if a poet of independent temper and free nature does not care to please the court, he has to suffer the same consequences that Firdausi suffered. Firdausi was a free-spirited and contented man. Although Hasan Maimandi, the vizier of Sultan Mahmud, had great power to help or harm him, he paid no regard to him, nor even to the Sultan himself. When he learned of Hasan Maimandi's hostility, he wrote these two verses:

I, your servant, who from the first beginnings of my nature have never been drawn to wealth, nor greedy for rank either,
why should I turn towards the vizier's door, when I have no need of the king's court either?

The result of his freedom and plain speaking was that the Sultan was turned against him. Now his verse was used to argue that he was an atheist, now that he was a Mu'tazilite and a Shi'a; and for a *masnavi* of sixty thousand verses, for which the reward had been fixed at one *misqal* of gold a verse, he got nothing in return but deprivation and failure. But in truth hardly any poet has won such recognition for his verse as he did. His *Shahnama* conquered the hearts of the whole world, and the greatest acknowledged masters bowed before his eloquence. And the reason for this was nothing but that the pressure of society or the court did not overcome his free nature.

# What the Poetry of Early Islam Was Like

In the poetry of early Islam, until slavish flattery and sycophancy found their way into it, every true ardour and passion was present. Those who deserved praise were praised, and those who deserved blame were blamed. When a just and good caliph or vizier died, moving elegies were written on him, and tyrants were condemned in their lifetimes. The great events that occurred in the campaigns and conquests of caliphs and kings were told in odes. Moving verses were written on gatherings of friends broken up by the turns of fortune. Virtuous wives composed touching verses on separation from their husbands, and husbands on separation from their wives. Poets drew faithful pictures of the past gatherings and throngs at pastures, springs and valleys. They described the endurance and speed of their she-camels and the companionship and loyalty of their horses. They spoke of the miseries of old age, the pleasures of youth and the carefree days of childhood. In exile they wrote of separation from their children and their longing to see them. They wrote true praise of their countrymen, friends and contemporaries, and elegies when they died. They told their own life stories and their real hardships and joys. They boasted of the courage and generosity of their families and tribes. They described the toils and hardships of travel that they themselves went through. They named one by one the places and sites, towns and villages, rivers and springs of their travels, and described in a moving way the good or bad things that befell them there. They showed the moment of parting from wife and children or from friends. In the same way every natural feeling that can arise in the heart of a passionate poet is found in their verse. But gradually attachment to the court and flattery stopped up all those springs and sources, and in general only two fields were left in which poets could display the gallop of their pens: one, praise-poetry, whose aim was to please the persons praised; the other, love-poetry, which inflamed their passions. Then, when after a long time no more taste was left in either subject than in a gnawed bone, and more fuel was needed to warm the assemblies of kings and nobles, the book of jests, buffoonery, lampoons and ribaldry was opened. Many poets gave up everything else and took this road, and gradually this colour spread over the whole of society. From first to last, in every class and every age, there have been some poets, more or fewer, worthy of honour, whose poetry Muslims can be proud of; but in general the poets we mostly see are those who made the field of poetry very narrow for those who came after, or left them very bad models.

# What Became of Islamic Poetry in the Middle and Later Periods

When later poets opened their eyes and saw little in their elders' legacy except odes of praise, love *ghazals* and *masnavis*, and lampoons and ribaldry, they took poetry to be confined to these few subjects. But even in these subjects, once the birds had eaten up the field, what was left? If praise is true and love real, the poet has no lack of material. Just as no two things in creation are alike, so the virtues of one man are not like those of another, and the experiences of one heart are not like another's. But when praise is wholly false and love merely imitative, poets must always repeat the same things that their predecessors wrote.

# Imitation in Poetry

Now that the later poets began to imitate the earlier, they chose to follow in their footsteps not only in subjects but in thoughts, words, phrases, styles, comparisons, metaphors, metre, rhyme and *radif* (refrain), in short in every matter and every thing. Then, when beating the same track became unbearable, very clumsy innovations began, to which the proverb applies: "Plain rice with turpentine resin: foul, yes, but my own invention."

# What Harm Bad Poetry Has Done to Society

Although at first it is the corrupt taste of society that spoils poetry, once poetry is spoiled its poisonous air does very severe harm to society too. When false poetry becomes the fashion in the whole nation, everyone's ears grow used to lies and exaggeration. The poet whose verse holds more lies or greater exaggeration gets more applause. He exaggerates further and goes to greater extremes to get still more applause. On one side his nature moves further and further from truth; on the other, as society keeps hearing false and baseless things in the charming dress of metre and rhyme, poison seeps into its taste. Day by day people have less affinity with facts and events. Their hearts begin to delight in strange and wondrous things, supernatural stories and impossible fancies. Hearing the plain events of history makes them restless. False tales and fables seem more interesting than real facts. Minds grow estranged from history, geography, mathematics and science, and quietly but very firmly bad morals take root in society. And when ribaldry and mockery enter the very substance of poetry along with lying, the morals of the nation are wholly eaten away as if by worms.

# What Injury Bad Poetry Does to Literature and Language

The greatest harm the country suffers when poetry is spoiled or narrowed is the ruin and destruction of its literature and its language. When lying and exaggeration become the badge of poets in general, their effect reaches the writing of authors, the speech of orators and even the everyday idiom and talk of the cultivated people of the land; for the most prominent and select part of every language is held to be those words, idioms and phrases that are used in poetry. So whoever wishes to excel in the writing, speech or idiom of his country's language must of necessity follow the language of the poets. In this way exaggeration seeps into every vein and fibre of literature and language. Through the ribaldry of poets a great many coarse and obscene words enter the language, because in dictionaries only those words are held authoritative and current coin whose authority is confirmed from the verse of poets. So whoever sits down to write a dictionary of the language of his country must first of all search through the *diwans* of the poets. Then, when poetry is confined to a few subjects and comes to rest wholly on imitation of the past, the language, instead of widening its circle, loses even its old breadth. That small part of the language through which the poet expresses his few ordinary subjects is mostly counted familiar and eloquent, and the rest of the words and idioms are thought strange and uncouth. So, apart from some of them being used in the speech of native speakers, they lie shut up in dictionaries, and some after a time fall out of use and serve no purpose at all. They give no help either to authors in writing or to orators in speech. The result of imitating the ancients is that no one can take any liberty with any word except those with which they took liberties for the needs of verse. Idioms they used in one sense can never be used in another. Not a hair's breadth can be departed from the comparisons found in their verse. In short, the poetry of a country stands to its literature as the heart to the body, of which it is said: "When it is sound, the whole body is sound; and when it is corrupt, the whole body is corrupt."

[1] Hali's note: Ubaid Zakani of Qazvin is a famous ribald poet. He was learned in many sciences. He wrote a book on Arabic grammar and went to Shiraz to present it to Shah Abu Ishaq Inju. When he wished to go to the king's court, he learned that the king was busy with his jesters and had no time to see anyone. Ubaid said, "If the king's favour can be won by buffoonery, then acquiring learning is pointless." From that day he took to ribald verse, and in it he became famous.
