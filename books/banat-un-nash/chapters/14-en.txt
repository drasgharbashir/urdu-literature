# The Size[1], Shape[2] and Division[3] of the Earth

Mahmuda: "And shall I tell you how big the earth is in miles? Its circumference is twenty-four thousand miles. Among men a stage of twelve *kos* is the rule; that is, when men travel, they go twelve *kos* a day; and in fact, if one travels at ease, twelve *kos* is plenty for a day's walking. At that rate, if a person set out walking straight ahead, in five years they would be back standing where they started, and would have made just one round of the earth."

Husn Ara: "God is great! Now that I think about it, the earth is very big. But how did you know that its circumference is twenty-four thousand miles?"

Mahmuda: "I learned it from books. Bold people took the trouble to travel for years and measured the whole circumference. By land it is hard to go straight; in some places there are hard-to-pass[4] roads climbing great mountains two or three *kos* high; in some places there are forests hundreds of *kos* across, with no place to stop, no hope of water and no road. But people have travelled on the sea in ships, and with the help of the compass they kept on in a straight line, and in the end they arrived back where they had started. Is there still any doubt that the earth is round?"

Husn Ara: "If there are mountains two or three *kos* high to climb, how can the earth be round?"

Mahmuda: "Yes, the earth is not round like a cast ball; it is round exactly like an orange, flattened at both ends, north and south; and just as there are little raised bumps on the peel of an orange, so these mountains are on the earth. Anyone who doubts that the earth is round because of the mountains has no proper idea of the size of the earth. If you put a mustard seed on a pitcher, what difference will it make to its roundness?"

Husn Ara: "I did think before that the earth was big, but I didn't know the real size."

Mahmuda: "You didn't think it big at all. Just because you went to Meerut and Panipat, you thought you had seen the whole face of the earth."

Husn Ara: "If the earth is so big, there must be thousands and hundreds of thousands of cities on it."

Mahmuda: "Certainly; but don't think from this that the whole face of the earth is inhabited. Three parts of it are sea; in the one part that is open, in it all the nine hundred million people are settled here and there, and there are forests and mountains and rivers too."

# The Reason for Civilisation

Husn Ara: "Why don't all the people live together in one place? If they built one big city and all lived in it, it would be great fun."

Mahmuda: "Fun, my foot; they would all start dying of hunger." Husn Ara: "Why?"

Mahmuda: "The grain for food grows in the open country; that is why people live apart in the world. Around every settlement they keep some open land for ploughing and sowing and growing grain. If everyone lived in one place it would make a city thousands of *kos* long and wide; where would they go to plough and sow? But a person cannot provide the necessities of life alone, so a few . . . . . people always live together. Where a few people live, that is a village; bigger than that, a small town; bigger than that, a city; bigger than that, a country and a land. Some villages have only four or five houses, and in big cities there are hundreds of thousands of people." [TN: Some words are missing in the printed text where the dots stand.]

Husn Ara: "Where there are only four or five houses, how do the people manage?"

Mahmuda: "They manage better than all of us."

Husn Ara: "How on earth can they manage? No sweet-seller, no druggist, no perfumer, no bangle-seller, no cloth-merchant, no nothing."

Mahmuda: "These things are the senseless luxuries of a rich life, and the stuff of boasting, show and swagger; [illegible: the last line of the page is cut off in the scan] to fill the stomach one needs some food, lentils or porridge, and to cover the body some coarse rough cloth; that much is certainly necessary, and apart from that it is all humbug of human vanity[5], self-pampering[6] and love of ease. So the things that are really necessary, the villagers produce with their own hands. Grain for food, fruits and vegetables, cotton, tobacco, safflower, indigo, everything grows in the fields; in fact, the kind of good, clean food and drink that is to be had in the village, we city people never see even in our dreams."

Husn Ara: "But if someone falls ill in a village, where do they get medicine, and who treats them?"

Mahmuda: "By God's grace, villagers have no need of medicine and treatment."

Husn Ara: "Why is that?" Mahmuda: "The reason is cleanliness, good air and water, and daily work."

# A Comparison of the Air and Water of the City and the Village

Husn Ara: "Surely the air and water are the same all over the world?"

Mahmuda: "They are the same, but where very many people live, filth[7] collects too, and because of the stench[8] the air and water go bad. Epidemics keep coming every other day, and even when there is no epidemic, city people are often ill."

Husn Ara: "If villagers don't fall ill, how do they die?" Mahmuda: "Dying is another matter. Villagers at least enjoy life; they are not always ill[9] like city people. And if now and then there is some pain or ache, why shouldn't villagers use some simple remedy? A herb from the jungle, the bark and leaves of trees, ground and rubbed, they drink it and they are well. Not drinking a preparatory dose[10] for weeks, and wasting away for months on a whey-purge[11]. The one medicine worth a hundred thousand medicines is fresh air, which city people never get all their lives, and villagers have all the time."

Husn Ara: "That's all very well, but how lonely one must feel in a village; no neighbourhood, no neighbours; whom can you talk to, whom can you visit?"

Mahmuda: "In the city who visits whom every day? Just as city people are busy with the work of the house, villagers have plenty to do with farming and looking after the cattle[12]. When they are free from that, they too work in their houses and amuse themselves with one another."

Husn Ara: "Why are these villagers so boorish, rough and uncouth?"

Mahmuda: "Khair-un-Nisa dear, look, Husn Ara Begam calls villagers boorish, rough and uncouth; you're from outside too, answer her."

Khair-un-Nisa: "The Begam Sahib doesn't know about villagers; she just speaks ill of them from hearsay; what answer should I give to that?" Husn Ara: "Khair-un-Nisa, where do you come from?"

Khair-un-Nisa: "There's a village called Sharifpur in the Moradabad district; my humble home is there."

Husn Ara: "How long have you been in the city?" Khair-un-Nisa: "About a year and a half."

Husn Ara: "What work is done in your house?"

Khair-un-Nisa: "Pounding, grinding, cooking, preparing food, spinning, sewing, stitching, sweeping the house, bathing and washing the children."

Husn Ara: "That's not what I mean; I'm asking what the men of your house do."

Khair-un-Nisa: "The boys study; the young men earn a living; the old men teach the boys and girls of the house and are busy with prayer and fasting."

Husn Ara: "Oh dear, I'm asking what goes on in your house."

Khair-un-Nisa: "Day in the daytime, night at night."

Husn Ara: "Stones fall on such a thick head! Not one sensible answer."

Khair-un-Nisa: "Fire take such clumsy talk! Not one sensible word."

Husn Ara: "You wretched country bumpkin, you're asking for trouble! Has your doom come for you? You ill-mannered thing, you don't mind your tongue; I'll beat you to pulp this minute."

Khair-un-Nisa: "Get along with you, you good-for-nothing[13] townie! If you're a rich lady, you're one for yourself; God forbid, are we anybody's maids or slave girls? If you say one thing you'll hear ten, and if you hit you'll be hit back too. Well, really, does she think I'm a city girl, that she can bully me?"

Husn Ara had never in her life been answered back, and such a harsh answer at that. The moment she heard Khair-un-Nisa she lost control of herself; beating her to pulp was only an empty threat, but she got straight up, went and complained to the teacher, and began to cry. She cried till she choked with sobs. As long as she cried, the teacher sat silent; and if a single word of comfort had come from her mouth, Husn Ara would not have recovered even by evening. At last, sobbing, she stopped of her own accord. Meanwhile Mahmuda came by several times and deliberately passed close to Husn Ara, but Husn Ara turned her face away. Seeing this, Mahmuda did not dare speak to Husn Ara, or she would have made up the quarrel[14]. Now evening was near; the teacher said, "Girls, that story of Masih-ul-Mulk has been left unfinished for ages; you might finish it; whose turn is it?"

Mahmuda: "It's Khair-un-Nisa's turn."

The teacher: "Well, Husn Ara Begam dear, if Khair-un-Nisa tells the story, will you listen?"

Husn Ara lowered her eyes and began to smile, and said, "Why, what's wrong with listening? Only that I won't speak in the middle."

Mahmuda: "Why won't you speak? If there's no talk in between, what kind of story is it? It becomes a regular lesson; no fun at all. Speak as much as you like, talk."

Husn Ara: "Oh, you're a sly one too; you must want to watch another fight?" Mahmuda: "Could anyone be so wretched as to enjoy a fight between two people? Never mind people, even setting animals to fight is written down as a great sin."

Husn Ara: "Elder sister, why do you deny it? It was you who set Khair-un-Nisa on me."

Mahmuda: "Did I set her on you, or did I give an occasion for conversation? You started running down villagers from the beginning; you couldn't hold yourself back for a moment or two, and you started a quarrel."

Husn Ara: "I started a quarrel?" Mahmuda: "You are fair-minded; you tell me, who began the harsh words first?"

Husn Ara: "What people are, they are called. Am I the only one who calls villagers boorish, rough and uncouth? The whole city says so; whose mouths will Khair-un-Nisa go round stopping?"

Khair-un-Nisa: "Praising yourself doesn't make you good, and someone else speaking ill of you doesn't make you bad. City people call villagers boorish, rough and uncouth; village people think city folk idlers, good-for-nothings, work-shy, faint-hearted[15] and taken up with appearances[16]."

The teacher: "When the two of you were arguing about this, why did you start quarrelling with each other in the course of judging other people's quarrel?"

Khair-un-Nisa: "Ma'am, the Begam Sahib began straight off by calling villagers boorish, rough and uncouth; I didn't like it at all, but I kept quiet, and . . . . . . ."

The teacher: "Husn Ara Begam didn't call you boorish, rough and uncouth; what she meant was that city people think villagers so." Khair-un-Nisa: "What of it? Even so, such ugly[17] words did not become the Begam Sahib, and I didn't say anything about that anyway."

The teacher: "Did Husn Ara Begam say anything harsher than that to you in particular? That doesn't seem to be her habit."

Khair-un-Nisa: "Well, I'm ashamed to repeat[18] it in front of you now. It was my fault; after all, I'm only an ill-mannered country girl, and where would I get good manners from? There's no doubt that I gave a harsh answer too; afterwards I was very sorry."

The teacher: "If it was your fault, why didn't you apologise?"

Khair-un-Nisa: "I'm ready to apologise a hundred times; I don't mind folding my hands and falling at her feet. But please make the Begam Sahib understand this much too, that she shouldn't pick quarrels with the girls at every word; it doesn't befit her dignity at all."

Husn Ara: "If you want me to live as everyone's equal, I never have and I never will. You people should have some consideration too, that I am the daughter of a rich family and that God has made me great."

The teacher: "That is unreasonable of you. Are the girls of the school your maids or servants? Or do you share out your wealth among them?" Husn Ara: "They may not be servants, but they're poor."

The teacher: "If they are poor, let them be; they don't care anything for your wealth."

Husn Ara: "And when do we care for them?" The teacher: "There you are: you don't care for them, and they don't care for you; quits."

Husn Ara: "What of it? Even so, they ought to respect me."

The teacher: "Why ought they, when they need nothing from you and want nothing? And if they don't, what do they lose?"

Husn Ara: "Oh, not 'ought', but it would be proper; and what they lose is bad feeling between us."

The teacher: "On that reckoning, you ought to as well."

Husn Ara: "Ought to what?" The teacher: "Respect them."

Husn Ara burst out laughing, and everyone laughed with her. The teacher: "Listen, Husn Ara Begam dear, among girls of the same age, what talk can there be of respect and honour? All of you should love one another, and every girl should take care that nothing happens to cause bad feeling among you."

Husn Ara: "God forbid, is there any bad feeling between me and Khair-un-Nisa? No, no; if we quarrel, people will think there's enmity between us." With that Husn Ara went and threw her arms round Khair-un-Nisa's neck.
