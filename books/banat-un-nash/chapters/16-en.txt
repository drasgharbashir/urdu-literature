# A Long Story about the Superstitions of Women

"There is much irreligion in the notions of villagers. What is the reason? Lack of knowledge and shortness of understanding. We had a grandmother, a distant relation; she died some four or five years ago at a full hundred years of age. If you heard her notions you would be amazed. In the first place she was a person of the old days, and in the second she had lived out in the country for a long time; the ways of the village women had so affected her that she had become a bundle of superstition. But the poor woman was always grief-stricken: her husband, her brother, her grown sons, her grown daughters had died one by one before her eyes. When she came to live in the city in her last days, she had only one great-nephew with her. Of a large family one child was left; he was the apple of her eye, and on top of that there was Grandmother's carefulness (may God grant her Paradise); I cannot tell you what misery that boy lived in. Whatever the ailment, the poor thing never knew what medicine was; his life depended only on spells and charms. When Grandmother brought him to the city he was a child of about four months. The poor boy had dried up to a thorn; arms and legs like sticks; his colour as if someone had rubbed turmeric on his face; a regular swelling on his cheeks, his eyes, his hands and feet; his spleen so enlarged that there was hardly room in his belly for breath; and with that a cough, such a cough that it would not let him rest day or night. That was his state, but not a ha'penny's worth of medicine was given him. God forbid that it was meanness about money; Grandmother would not have grudged her very life for that boy, and who else had she besides him? She was sitting with her feet dangling in the grave; whatever goods and wealth she had were the boy's. The moment she got down from the palanquin with that half-dead[1] boy, we were all frightened at the sight of him.

I: "Oh, Grandmother, what is the matter with this boy, and how long has he been ill?"

Grandmother: "Ever since he saw the moon of the month of *Tezi* and fell ill, he hasn't recovered. That's just the trouble with the children of a mother who has lost children[2]: obstinacy at every word, stubbornness at every word. His stubbornness brought him to this; and I'm worse than a corpse with his illness; I don't think of food, I don't know what's happening to my own body; my heart is in my mouth."

I: "But wasn't there any doctor, any *hakim*, in that town?"

Grandmother: "Plenty of *hakims*, plenty of doctors; but only if he were within their power."

I: "Doesn't he take medicine? Doesn't he keep a diet?"

Grandmother: "No, he takes his medicine all right, and this is the fifth month of his diet; may it be forbidden if anything but boiled *khichri* has touched his tongue."

I: "Then hasn't the treatment done him any good?" Grandmother: "We haven't had him treated by *hakims* at all."

I: "Why, what are *hakims* kept for, some other day? The boy has come to this, and still you haven't given him any medicine at all! Here in the city there are *hakims* each better than the last, and the best medicines are to be had; in God's name start the treatment from tomorrow."

Grandmother: "I didn't come here to get *hakims* to treat him; there are some vows that have to be fulfilled."

I: "Why do you hesitate to give him a *hakim*'s medicine?"

Grandmother: "These are not illnesses for *hakims*. This boy's mother had a trouble in the womb; the child would reach his fifth year and be taken. This boy is the tenth. Heaven knows where I haven't sifted the dust[3], and I've made my blood and sweat one[4] for this boy. The rule of this affliction is that its power lasts for twelve years; there are just four more months of misery; if they pass, I can be at ease."

I: "I too hear people speak of afflictions like this, but at heart I don't believe in it. I can't understand how the mother can have an affliction and the children feel its effect for twelve years. Whatever the illness, there is some remedy for it; what sort of incurable affliction is this, that physicians do not believe in it, the *vaids*[5] do not accept it, doctors do not admit it, and nobody can tell what sort of curse it is?"

Grandmother: "Yes, this new wisdom has been invented in this thirteenth century; otherwise, what a great Maulvi my father-in-law was; his *fatwa* ran through the whole world; and he himself practised these things. Now the people of blessing and learning are gone; there are only half-baked mullahs[6] left who don't even know how to make the intention of prayer; they have brought out new rulings. They call the blessings and *fatiha* for the saints and the Prophet forbidden, they forbid the bridegroom's veil of flowers and the wrist-thread; at weddings the drums and kettledrums are all stopped; the feasts and festivals that have come down from the prophets are all done away with; the sherbet of Muharram is forbidden, the *manda* bread and *halwa* of Shab-e Barat are forbidden, the vermicelli of 'Id is forbidden. The men had gone wrong already, and they have spoiled the women along with them. It's the old saying, the half-line:

I am drowning, but I'll drag you down with me as well.

Now married women are worse than widows: no colour in their clothes, no *missi* on their teeth, no nose-ring in their noses, no bangles on their wrists."

I: "That may all be so, but no harm has come of it; in fact for the present[7] there is one benefit, that people are spared the trouble that keeping up the customs used to cause."

Grandmother: "Since the customs went, splendour, blessing, love, everything has gone from the world; what is left? There isn't the old-time devotion[8] between husband and wife. There isn't the old love between brothers and sisters. There aren't those cheap times, there isn't that ease; now in every house there's a struggle[9] for bread."

I: "Grandmother, a great many new things for show and luxury have come in; that has raised everyone's expenses. And because there is peace on every side in the country, the produce of one place spreads over the whole country. Two years ago there was drought in this region, and grain was being brought in from as far as Calcutta. And besides, the number of people has greatly increased; how could grain be cheap?"

Grandmother: "Get along with you, girl! I don't understand such humbug. There's farming on my own land; where a *bigha* used to give ten maunds, now it doesn't give two."

I: "Grandmother, I have never farmed, but I have looked at one or two books on the art. There is no doubt that the yield of the land has gone down these days compared with former times; and the reason is that in former times the government was badly ordered; for fear of plunder less was farmed and a lot of land lay fallow, and by lying fallow its strength increased; when it was sown it gave great crops. Now no land lies fallow any year; the yield is bound to go down."

Grandmother: "Child, the rains aren't like they used to be. I've lived to this age; apart from the famine of the *chalisa* year, we never heard the word 'famine'. Now famine has become an ordinary thing, and what you call cheapness was famine in the old days; even in the year ninety-four it was selling at eight or ten seers."

I: "Grandmother, as far as I know, the rains are just as they have always been; in fact, with the canals running, there is plenty of water in many places. But in former times we didn't know what was happening in other towns; now if there is the slightest trouble in one place, it is drummed through the whole country."

Grandmother: "It isn't just the rains; the days and nights (the times) have changed so that there's no heat in the hot weather and no cold in the winter."

I: "What wonder is that? Forests thousands of *kos* across have been cut down and settled; canals run in many places; the population has grown by half again; these things must certainly have affected the climate."

Grandmother: "Affected it indeed! Diseases we never heard the names of come round twice a year; not a year goes by without cholera and smallpox."

I: "Grandmother, weren't there cholera and smallpox before?"

Grandmother: "There was cholera, but it was cholera from heaviness and indigestion, and that only rarely. Now there are epidemics that cover the world. Smallpox, certainly, has come down from of old; nobody born of woman has escaped smallpox; it comes out even inside the grave."

I: "Grandmother, for that the English have found vaccination, a sure remedy that never fails."

Grandmother: "Oh, fire take that vaccination! I've been suffering that very trouble for five months. What other illness has this boy got? That idiot had him vaccinated without asking me; to this day there's been no escape from misery."

I: "Did the vaccination take?" Grandmother: "Take indeed; his whole arm festered for months."

I: "Then he can't have had smallpox?" Grandmother: "He didn't have the big kind; and if he'd had the big kind, these good days wouldn't have come."

I: "But measles isn't so very dangerous."

Grandmother: "The mismanagement of the people over him spoiled everything. First they had him vaccinated; second, when *They*[10] came out they didn't keep the precautions."

I: "Are there precautions in measles too?"

Grandmother: "Why not? No frying with spices in the house; nobody in the house to wear white clothes washed at the washerman's; in the first place nobody from outside should come in, and if they must, they should stop and take breath first; no scent of any kind should come near; no medicine at all should be given in this illness; the sound of thunder should not reach the child's ear. There are plenty of precautions like that, for those who keep them. But his father is one of those same wrong-headed Maulvis; with them there are no precautions and no care; in fact they call it idolatry and unbelief. When this boy had measles, they stubbornly broke all the precautions. When the measles was over his eyes got sore, and he was treated by an oculist. I kept on telling them, these are measles eyes, don't treat them; they wouldn't listen to a word. The remedy for measles eyes is just this: gram lentils are waved over him and put aside, seven flowers are waved over him and kept; when the eyes are well, they are thrown into the canal. Well, they cured his eyes, but no sooner were his eyes well than fever began. Then I said, 'Never mind; if it's idolatry, it's we who commit it; don't you interfere in our affairs.' His father quarrelled over this very thing and went off to his estate; since then he hasn't asked whether the boy is alive or dead. I've gone mad over this boy; I've tried every remedy in the world, but the fever won't leave him for a single day."

I: "But Grandmother, you say you haven't had a *hakim* treat him; then what were all the remedies in the world that you tried?"

Grandmother: "For months I had pots of sherbet waved over him and put at the crossroads; I made an elephant of tobacco and put it at his head every night without fail; I hung chariot tassels round his neck; hundreds of times I waved water and live coals over him."

I: "Grandmother, how do they wave coals?"

Grandmother: "Water and seven live coals are waved over him from head to foot, and taken to the drain of the house and put out, and as you put them out you say aloud, 'If you're hungry, eat fire, and if you're thirsty, drink water.'"

I: "Well, then, you have done all this and it has done no good; the boy's state has got worse day by day; so now try a *hakim*'s treatment too."

Grandmother: "All this trouble came from treatment in the first place; if I had him treated again, I'd lose the boy."

I: "It seems that the heat of the measles has filled him inside; he has not been given any cooling."

Grandmother: "This boy's constitution was spoiled in his mother's womb. The girls of today think old people fools, just as you do; she too never paid any attention to what I said; she brought an affliction on an untouched womb for nothing."

I: "Was she careless in some way about eating and drinking?"

Grandmother: "No, she couldn't guard against this affliction."

I: "Well, Grandmother, tell me, what are the things that guard against it?"

Grandmother: "This affliction comes from stepping over the ring of flour that forms on the ground when flour is sifted; from going anywhere at all at the time when day and night meet; from embracing someone while standing level with them; from letting the end of one's scarf trail on the ground; from the hand of the lamp touching one's belly; from bathing under a tree; from stepping over the bath-water of a woman who has the affliction."

I: "It seems that this is no bodily illness."

Grandmother: "Heaven forbid! It's a kind of possession, and it jumps from person to person."

Grandmother: "God help us, girl! You're a terrible chatterbox. Didn't I say that this affliction also arises of itself?" [TN: Asghari's question that this answers is missing from the printed text.]

I: "Grandmother, you're getting cross; if we don't ask you, whom should we ask?"

Grandmother: "Get along, you sly thing! I understand very well; you're making fun of me with your talk."

I: "Oh, Grandmother! Would I make fun of you?"

Grandmother: "This boy's mother had exactly your nature; she too used to raise needless arguments at every word; and living under the control of her short-lived[11] parents, heaven help us, what a life! From the day this soul came into being[12] it fell to me alone to look after her every way; amulets and offerings and vows and gifts, I left nothing undone."

I: "Grandmother, your belief is very wrong; repent, repent! Now that the days of death are near, what answer will you give God? Is life and death in anyone's power besides God's? This is idolatry."

Grandmother: "God is true, and His power is true; He made these things too. Show me in which Qur'an it is written that when a child is in the womb and there are double eclipses[13], the woman with child may walk about in the courtyard and work? Or in which Hadith does it say, leave children alone in the house, and give them the breast under the eaves and under trees without a thought?"

I: "In the Qur'an and the Hadith it is written in hundreds of places that death and life are in God's power alone, and the servant is helpless. A verse:

There is power in none but Him alone
To come to the help of the helpless one."

Grandmother: "Well, is it the work of fire to burn, or not?" I: "It is, and God has put this property into fire."

Grandmother: "There you are; God has put the property into the evil eye and into the shadow too."

I: "You have forced yourself to believe in these false properties; no ground for them can be found anywhere."

Grandmother: "Oh girl, is there any doubt about the power of the evil eye? The evil eye is well known; it splits stone; the evil eye of people and of animals strikes."

I: "Of what animals?" Grandmother: "Of dogs, of lizards."

I: "If the walls and doors start giving the evil eye, that's terrible; where can one go to eat, into the depths of the earth?"

Grandmother: "One needn't go into the depths of the earth, but one shouldn't be so careless as to start eating in front of all and sundry. You keep calling out 'treatment, treatment'; look, there is the evil eye alone; give a hundred thousand treatments, but until that thing reaches the one who gave the eye, no treatment will do any good."

I: "But is there any way of warding off the evil eye?"

Grandmother: "They burn in the hearth some earth from under the feet of the one who gave the eye, or garlic, onion, chilli and salt; or they put that same food at the crossroads, or feed it to the one who gave the eye, or have the person struck by the eye touch some meat and give it to the kites. Some people set aside the evil eye's share before they eat. Whatever you do, it's alms; and alms given turn away misfortune; reward and remedy at once."

I: "Neither reward nor remedy. There is reward only when a thing is given purely for God's sake; giving like that is a kind of offering to an idol; and it has nothing to do with remedy."

Grandmother: "Think what you like; but if there is a charm for drawing out the poison of the evil eye, this is it."

I: "Grandmother, you take all these precautions, but there's no sign of any effect. We have always seen you weeping. People who don't care about these things at all are a thousand times happier than you."

Grandmother: "Child, don't ask about my weeping; ever since this wart came up under my eye, the tears have never stopped."

I: "Then will you keep the poor boy suffering like this, or will you do something?"

Grandmother: "He has two ailments, the cough and the fever; and I won't touch the cough for another four days."

I: "Why?" Grandmother: "His cough is whooping cough, and the very best remedy for it is to ask a rider on a black horse and do whatever he says. Eleven days ago a man was riding by on a black pony; I asked him, and he said, 'In two weeks it will get better by itself.' As for the fever, fulfilling the vows comes first. You see, he has four tufts of hair left on his head; round his neck there's a heap of collars and moon-pendants; a sheet is to be given at one shrine, a goat has been vowed at another. Let these vows be fulfilled, and you may take it the fever is gone. Only I know what he suffers, but my mind is at ease; I have seen him dead in a dream, and when you see someone dead, their life is long."

So, though a thousand attempts were made to get him treated, it was not done. With the change of air and water the boy's health had improved a great deal of itself; suddenly we heard that Grandmother was leaving the next day.

I: "Oh, Grandmother, so soon?" Grandmother: "Yes, my dear, the house isn't good; what can I do?"

I: "Yes, it is rather shut in; there can't be much air. Sleep in the upper room at night."

Grandmother: "Fire take this house and its upper room! Is it fit for people to live in?"

I: "Grandmother, it isn't so very small, and the upper room is very airy; the courtyard below, certainly, is a bit cramped."

Grandmother: "You keep going on about air; all night the child keeps starting up, and there's something so frightening about it that I'm afraid myself; all night I see bad dreams."

I: "Have you ever seen anything with your own eyes?" Grandmother: "How can I tell a lie? I haven't seen anything, God forbid; but no, my dear, it's a bad house." I: "Why, what's wrong with it?"

Grandmother: "All night the wretched cats wail; at the back there's a banyan tree, and an owl lives in it; when you wake at night you hear the dogs howling in the lane. The roof room is the worst of all."

I: "A tenant lived in that same roof room with his wife and children for two years; we never heard any complaint."

Grandmother: "It must have gone bad after he left." I: "Why, does that happen too?"

Grandmother: "Why not? If no lamp is lit in a good house for forty days, *They* take possession of it."

I: "Nonsense! The city air has suited the boy very well; look, God be praised, how different he is from before; if you stay another month or so, the boy will be quite well."

Grandmother: "Why, it was only the vows that had to be paid, and I've done that; now there's nothing to fear. If, God willing, his real birthday comes and goes safely, then I shall have no worry at all. I'm uneasy at being away from home; when his birthday is over I'll come back and bring everyone with me."

In short, such a superstition took hold of her heart that, whatever we did, she would not stay. Look what notions this grandmother of ours had, which had nothing to do with religion or reason; and all this was the effect of living in the country. The greatest fault of the villages is another: there is such harshness and confinement over women that it cannot be described. Married eight or ten years, mothers of three or four children, and still the veil is pulled right down; they are not allowed to talk, they are shut out of conversation. In short, the freedom that women ought to have along with the seclusion the Law requires is not to be had in the villages; the poor things live their lives in a state of slavery."

Since Husn Ara's betrothal had been made in Jhajjar, she was so struck dumb at hearing this that she said not another word. When evening came, the teacher said, "Girls, God bless you, what is the matter with you! You broke off the story of Masih-ul-Mulk at such an unlucky moment that you have never even mentioned it since. Whatever the routine, if it is missed for even one day, the blessing of forty days flies away. You think of stories as play, but I consider them more necessary than the lesson. Go and bring the book."
