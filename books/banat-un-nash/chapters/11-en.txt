# Interesting Things about Arithmetic

Mahmuda: "Kulsum, how much do three and seven and nine make together?" Kulsum: "Nineteen." Mahmuda: "And eight and two?" Kulsum: "Ten."

Mahmuda: "If eight rupees out of twenty-five are spent, how many rupees are left?" Kulsum: "Seventeen."

Mahmuda: "Now tell me, how many rupees is a hundred and a quarter?" Kulsum: "A hundred and twenty-five."

Mahmuda: "And how many is four hundred less a quarter?" Kulsum: "Three hundred and seventy-five, or four hundred less twenty-five."

Mahmuda: "Well done, my dear, well done! I'll believe you're clever if you tell me this: Amina was seven at the time of the Mutiny, and it is now six years since the Mutiny; how old is Amina now?" Kulsum: "Thirteen."

Mahmuda: "Right. Sister, tell me one more thing: Amina's brother is four years older than her; how many years before the Mutiny was he born?" Kulsum, after thinking: "Eleven years."

Mahmuda: "Now, Zubaida, you have studied more than Kulsum; tell me, if twelve girls each put three pice into the kitty for a doll's wedding, how many annas will it all come to?" Zubaida: "Nine annas."

Mahmuda: "If a seer and a half of vermicelli is shared equally among six girls, how much will each girl get?" Zubaida: "A quarter of a seer each."

Mahmuda: "If there are two hundred mangoes and ten girls, how many each?" Zubaida: "That's very easy: twenty each."

Husn Ara: "This rose bush planted in the courtyard gives fifteen flowers every day; how many flowers will there be in a month?" Zubaida: "Four and a half hundred, that is, four hundred and fifty."

Mahmuda: "Well, madam, at seven annas a yard, what is the price of seven yards of drill for a pair of trousers?" Zubaida, after thinking: "Three rupees and one anna."

Mahmuda: "If a bolt of eight yards is priced at two rupees and is bought for three rupees, what did the cloth cost a yard?" Zubaida: "Let me write it down and add it up."

Mahmuda: "No, madam, think it out in your head and say; it isn't so hard as all that."

Zubaida, after hesitating a little: "Six annas a yard."

Mahmuda: "Now, if ghee is an anna and a half a *chhatank*, how much is half a seer?" Zubaida, writing something on her palm with her fingers: "Twelve annas."

Mahmuda: "How many *girahs* in a yard?" Zubaida: "Sixteen."

Mahmuda: "And how many seers in a maund?" Zubaida: "Forty."

Mahmuda: "Good, sister, good! Now, Rabi'a dear, you come forward; you're very good at sums. Tell me: drill nine *girahs* wide takes just nine yards for a pair of trousers; if it were a full yard wide, how much would it take?" Rabi'a: "Let me write it on my slate."

Mahmuda: "Very well, but answer quickly, or the cloth-seller[1] will go away."

Rabi'a, after a moment or two: "Five yards and one *girah*."

Mahmuda: "Now tell me this: this hall where we are all sitting is six yards long and two and a half yards wide; how much long-cloth will it take for a floor-cloth?" Rabi'a: "And the width of the long-cloth?"

Mahmuda: "The usual, one yard." Zubaida: "Exactly fifteen yards."

Mahmuda: "Answer one question and we'll give you great praise. The tank of the Great Mosque is six yards square, that is, its length and breadth are equal, and two yards deep. If one square yard holds three water-skins of water, one water-skin twenty-five *lotas*, one *lota* fifteen glasses, and one glass half a quarter-seer of water, how much water is there in the whole tank?"

Rabi'a (after a quarter of an hour): "Two hundred and three maunds and five seers."

Husn Ara: "Oh my, what things these wretched creatures have learned! Are they girls or demons?" Mahmuda: "They know far more wonderful things than that. I asked them easy things to make you understand. Well, Hajira, can you measure the minarets of the Jama Masjid without a yardstick, without a rope, and without going up?"

Hajira: "Certainly, I remember that sum about shadows. About two months ago a wedding party from our family went to Palwal, and I went with them. On the way we stopped near the Qutb Sahib's column to have breakfast. I had been very curious about that rule; at once I took a straw and measured its shadow, and worked out the sum right there on the ground. The women with me started teasing me: 'What's she picking up straws for in broad daylight[2]?' Anyway, I remembered the measurement that came out of my sum; when I came back home I looked in the *Sanadid-e 'Ajam*[3], and it was exactly the same height, perhaps two yards out."

Rabi'a: "Hajira dear, will you teach me the sum about shadows too?"

Hajira: "Why, it's a very easy thing. Take a straw and measure it, then stand it straight up in the sun and measure its shadow, then measure the shadow of the column; then by the rule of proportion[4], which you know, the height of the column will come out like this: if a straw so long casts a shadow so long, how tall will the column be whose shadow is so long?" At this hint Rabi'a jumped for joy, but Husn Ara knew nothing about proportion and the like; how could she understand this riddle[5]? Turning to Hajira she said, "Oh, you liar! Oh, you fibber! You're not even four cubits tall yet, bless you, and you go and measure a column thousands of *kos* high! As if I'd believe you just because you say so! We haven't seen God, but we know Him by reason."

Mahmuda: "Now, now, Begam Sahib, what is this habit of yours of flaring up for no reason in the middle of a conversation?"

Husn Ara: "By the holy God, I haven't flared up at all, and I didn't say anything."

Mahmuda: "Swearing so quickly is even worse." Husn Ara: "If you're going to pick on every word like this, then speaking at all is a crime."

Mahmuda: "If you'll listen to me fairly for a moment, I'll say something, and if it's wrong I'll admit it." Husn Ara: "Well, go on, say it."

# The Wrong of Swearing Oaths

Mahmuda: "First tell me, why did you swear by God?"

Husn Ara: "So that you would believe what I said."

Mahmuda: "That is where your understanding goes wrong; if a person's word is not believed, their oath is a hundred thousand times less believed."

Husn Ara: "All right, I just swore without thinking; what harm did I do?"

Mahmuda: "Certainly you did harm. Have you made God a girl's doll, or taken Him for a child's toy? Aren't you afraid to take the name of the Master and King of both worlds so carelessly? Look at the faithlessness of the world: when people respect a person, they don't use their name; does anyone use the name of their mother or father, or elder brother or elder sister, or any other elder? And yet such disrespect and irreverence towards God that His name is used at every word! When I hear anyone swear by God, my hair stands on end, and I stare at them in amazement at how such a word came so boldly off their tongue."

Husn Ara: "If it were forbidden to take God's name, why do people take it in the call to prayer and in prayer?"

Mahmuda: "Taking His name in worship is another thing; making God's name a filler in your talk and blurting it out in and out of season is quite against reverence."

Husn Ara: "But people say 'by God, by God' at every word."

Mahmuda: "If a thing is bad, it cannot become good even if the whole world starts doing it. And if you take the example of the people of the world, you will find very few truly religious and good servants of God. Just think about this: if the greatness and glory of God are in our hearts, it is impossible that we should treat His holy name so carelessly. A person is a sinner to every hair of their head; let them look at themselves, and then consider the majesty and holiness[6] of that exalted Lord."

Husn Ara: "Yes, swearing is a very bad thing indeed. I repent; if an oath comes out of my mouth again, slap me in the face, I won't mind."

Mahmuda: "Why should it come to that? You be careful from now on, and if it ever slips your mind, I'll remind you."

# Courtesy among Companions

Mahmuda: "Well, that's done with; now I ask you, why did you hurt poor Zubaida's feelings?"

Husn Ara: "My dear, I didn't say anything to Zubaida; you're making the poor girl quarrel with me for nothing." [TN: Husn Ara's remark was in fact addressed to Hajira; the text names Zubaida here and in what follows.]

Mahmuda: "You called her a liar and a fibber, and you say you didn't say anything! This is just like the business with Diyanat again. Don't you know that lying is a very shameful thing, and that the wives and daughters of decent people don't tell lies? To call someone a liar is like accusing them of theft."

Husn Ara: "My dear, I only said it in fun. In friendly freedom such things slip out without thinking[7]. If we stood on ceremony like that with the people we're with day and night, life would be impossible."

Mahmuda: "That's not a matter of fun and friendliness; it's a matter of quarrelling and falling out. If such things are not minded among the people you're with all the time, it will become a habit; and perhaps that is why you took such liberties with Diyanat that day."

Husn Ara: "Well, was it only my fault, or was it Zubaida's too, for going off joining earth and sky together[8]?"

Mahmuda: "Poor Zubaida was not at fault at all; she was saying something perfectly reasonable."

Husn Ara: "Reasonable? If that's reasonable, I say goodbye to such reason." Mahmuda: "You haven't studied anything yet, so how could you know about the wide world? To you Zubaida's words are bound to seem unreasonable. But when you called Zubaida a liar and a fibber, who knows what title I'd get; for fear of that I can't say anything."

Husn Ara: "For God's sake, say whatever is in your mind; I promise I won't mind at all."

Mahmuda: "Begam Sahib, being the daughter of a rich family is one thing, and knowledge and good sense are another. You don't even know what sort of creature a *kos* is."

Husn Ara: "Why? I know the *kos* quite well; shall I tell you? The Qadam Sharif[9], one; Humayun's maze[10], three *kos*; the Qutb Sahib, seven *kos*; and have you ever been? Meerut, twenty-five *kos*; Panipat, four stages. I've been a very long way."

Mahmuda: "Right; that's why you said the Qutb Sahib's column was thousands of *kos* high."

Husn Ara: "Why, isn't it thousands of *kos* high? Have you ever stood at the bottom and looked at the column? A person just falls over backwards like a fantail pigeon. Have you ever happened to go up? Even strong men get out of breath."

Mahmuda: "Does it follow that if strong men get out of breath going up, the column must be thousands of *kos* high?"

Husn Ara: "That's how I reckon it must certainly be thousands of *kos* high. I've heard that some men think nothing of walking fifteen or twenty *kos*, and yet they pant and get out of breath climbing the column; so the column must be very high indeed." Mahmuda: "Let me explain the reason for that."

# Notes

[1] "Here Mahmuda slipped in a joke."

[2] "Has she, God forbid, gone mad?"

[3] "The *Sanadid-e 'Ajam* is a book describing the buildings of Delhi and its surroundings." [TN: Probably Sayyid Ahmad Khan's *Asar-us-Sanadid* (1847), which is the book that describes the monuments of Delhi.]

[4] *Arba'-e mutanasiba*: "a rule of arithmetic in which, by working with three known quantities, a fourth unknown quantity is found".

[5] *Mu'amma*: "a riddle".

[6] *Taqaddus*: "purity".

[7] *Be-sakhta*: "without intention, unintentionally".

[8] "Joining earth and sky together means saying something absurd and impossible."

[9] "A small settlement outside Delhi, where the footprint of the Prophet is kept."

[10] "In the tomb of the Emperor Humayun there is a maze built so that a person who goes in forgets which way they came."
