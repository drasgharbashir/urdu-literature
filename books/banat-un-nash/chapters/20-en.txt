# On the Study of History and the Different Customs of Mankind

Husn Ara: "People are all the same people; it is very surprising that their customs should differ."

The teacher: "Customs alone! Why, faces, height and build, dress, manners, speech, ways of life, religion: they differ in all of these. There are some two thousand languages in the world. Read the histories of countries and you will find the strangest customs. I have read in a history of China that there a small foot is thought a great beauty; in childhood they put iron shoes on the girls so that the foot cannot grow; when they are grown, the tiny feet cannot bear the body's weight, and the women fall over as they walk, and this they count as delicacy. Among the Chinese a flat nose is much admired; they say a face should be smooth, and that if the nose stands out the face loses its evenness; with this idea the people of China press down the bridge of the nose by all sorts of devices. The Marathas shave a widow's head. The Rajputs kill girls as soon as they are born. The women of Arabia marry several times. In Japan they greatly admire blackened teeth, and when a woman is married she plucks out the hair of her eyebrows entirely. In Africa the women have their teeth filed and filed into points. In the Himalaya, on the border of China, there is a place where the men do the housework and the women do the marketing and farming outside; in Burma shopkeeping is mostly the women's business. In short, every country has its own custom."

Husn Ara: "But what is the cause of these differences? In the beginning all are the children of one Adam."

The teacher: "When the children of Adam had multiplied greatly, they could not live in one place; bands of ten thousand and twenty thousand went and settled in every direction, and kept no connection with their original home; little by little the differences reached such a pitch that the people of two countries seem not to be of the race of one Adam at all."

# The Heavenly Bodies[1] and the Principles of Astronomy in Brief, with a Little about Eclipses of the Moon and Sun

Husn Ara: "Reason fails before God's power; what a huge earth He has made, and how many people He has settled on it!"

The teacher: "Before God's power the earth is very small; that Almighty One has created countless worlds such that beside them the earth is of no account."

Husn Ara: "What are they, the world of the hereafter?"

The teacher: "Not the hereafter; these stars that you see in the sky."

Husn Ara: "Are they bigger than the earth?" The teacher: "Much bigger."

Husn Ara: "That's very surprising; I haven't really gone blind, have I!"

The teacher: "God forbid."

Husn Ara: "These stars that twinkle in the sky, you say they are bigger than the earth; to me they look very tiny and little."

The teacher: "Not only to you; they look small to everyone; but in reality they are very big. It is the rule of the eye that it sees a distant thing as small; to remedy this defect wise men invented the telescope; that too is a kind of glass, but through it a distant thing appears large. The books that describe the moon, the sun and the stars are called books of astronomy. I remember well that when my father taught me his own treatise *Sair-e Asman*, at every point I was more astonished than you are; indeed I even asked my father whether these things only seemed strange to me, or whether they really were strange. My father said that man, imperfect in understanding, from what he sees on the earth supposes in his small comprehension that God's power is confined to that, that these are all the wonders of His workmanship, and that he has understood all the workings of the Divine. Man is exactly like the gnat in the wild fig, which is born inside the fig and thinks it the whole world; but the truth is that all that man has known and understood from the creation of the world until now is, in the workshop of the Lord of the worlds' power, like one tiny drop beside the ocean; indeed, even less."

Husn Ara: "Well then, teacher, is the earth really smaller than the sun?"

The teacher: "Yes indeed, and how small! Like a pea beside a big pitcher."

Husn Ara: "And how far from us will the sun be?"

The teacher: "Forty-seven and a half million *kos*."

Husn Ara: "Forty-seven and a half million *kos*! Oh my, it doesn't even go into my head."

The teacher: "Let me explain the sun's distance to you another way. How fast a cannonball travels; have you any idea in your mind of its speed?"

Husn Ara: "About twice as fast as the railway."

The teacher: "No, a mile and a half in a minute, that is, about a hundred miles in an hour; and I have never heard of the railway going more than thirty miles an hour; perhaps in the English people's own country it goes somewhat faster."

Husn Ara: "Mahmuda Begam did tell me how hours are reckoned, but it has slipped my mind; dear teacher, do explain it again."

The teacher: "The day and night have twenty-four hours, and a minute is the sixtieth part of an hour."

Husn Ara: "Yes, so a cannonball goes a *kos* and a half in a minute? (Then, thinking) A *kos* and a half in a minute!" [TN: The teacher said a mile and a half; Husn Ara repeats it as *kos*, as the text has it.]

The teacher: "If a cannon were fired from the earth, the ball would reach the sun in 90 years."

Husn Ara: "Oh my, God preserve us! What a distance! And how much bigger than the earth is the moon?"

The teacher: "The moon is not bigger; it is smaller." Husn Ara: "Then it must be close too?"

The teacher: "Yes, it is a hundred and twenty thousand *kos* away."

Husn Ara: "Dear teacher! God must have made these great balls of light just so that their light should reach the earth."

The teacher: "The sun is bright of itself, but that is not so with the moon; it is without light, like our earth."

Husn Ara: "Just as the eye is mistaken about the size of the stars, is it mistaken about their brightness too?"

The teacher: "All the stars are bright, but those stars that have no brightness of their own, just as the sun's rays fall on the earth and the earth lights up, so those stars too look bright to us when the sunshine falls on them."

Husn Ara: "From what you say it seems that some stars are without light, like the moon, and some are bright of themselves, like the sun."

The teacher: "You have understood rightly; that is how it is."

Husn Ara: "But none of them has brightness equal to the sun."

The teacher: "The sun is near, after all; the stars are so far away that it cannot be told."

Husn Ara: "Well, the stars that are not bright of themselves, does the sun's light fall on them all the time? It doesn't fall on the earth all the time."

The teacher: "It falls on the earth all the time too."

Husn Ara: "Teacher, at night, when the sun has set, there is no sunshine anywhere."

The teacher: "The earth is round; on whichever side it faces the sun, there it is day, and on the other side it is dark, which is called night. In the same way one side or another of the stars too is always facing the sun."

Husn Ara: "The earth can be seen even without sunshine, but all the stars always look shining; what is the reason for that?"

The teacher: "The reason is their distance. The stars are so far away that we see them, twinkling at that, only by means of their light; otherwise what hope would there be of seeing them?"

Husn Ara: "No stars are seen by day."

The teacher: "The sun's own blazing rays are on us; the faint light of the stars is not seen; just as a lamp's light looks pale by day."

Husn Ara: "What you said, that one side of the earth is light and the other side dark, is right; if you set a round thing before a light, the side facing it will be lit and the other side dark; but then where it was day on the earth it ought always to be day, and where it was night, always night."

The teacher: "Do you know what attraction is?" Husn Ara hesitated.

Mahmuda: "What, forgotten already? The attraction by whose force things fall to the earth."

Husn Ara: "Yes, I know; what then?" The teacher: "That attraction is not in the earth alone; rather, everything is pulling everything else. The moon, the sun and the stars are all pulling one another towards themselves; and the result of this pulling to and fro is that eleven stars, counting the earth, go round the sun."

Husn Ara: "Is the earth a star too?" The teacher: "Certainly."

Husn Ara: "Very well, say the earth does go round the sun; that still doesn't make day and night change places."

The teacher: "What do you mean, 'say'? That is just how it goes round. And day and night change because the earth is also turning over on itself as it goes; one turn is called a day and a night, and one circuit round the sun is called a year. By reckoning it has been found that in one hour the earth travels fifty-eight thousand miles on its circuit; and just as people sitting in a train or a boat do not feel the motion of the train or boat, we too have no idea where the earth is going."

Husn Ara: "Only eleven stars go round the sun, and the rest?"

The teacher: "The rest are fixed; and who knows, perhaps each of those fixed stars is a sun in itself, with other stars going round it that we cannot see."

Husn Ara: "What if, going round and round, these balls knock into one another; dear teacher, what would happen then?"

The teacher: "It would be no wonder if the Day of Judgement came like that; indeed the English have newly seen four stars going round the sun; people think that the four were once one; nobody knows when and how it broke and became four."

Husn Ara: "We get no great light from these stars; the sun and moon, well, are natural torches; why has God made these stars?"

The teacher: "Why has God made you? He alone knows the secrets of His power. Just as the earth is a world, every single star is a world in itself; perhaps people like us live in them too."

Husn Ara: "Do you say this only by guesswork, or has it been established that people live in the stars?"

The teacher: "It is a guess, but a reasonable guess, not an unreasonable one. In some stars mountains, seas, snow, clouds and air have been established; so what wonder if there are people too? Do you know what that sort of spot is that one sees on the moon?"

Husn Ara: "I have heard that some old woman sits in the moon spinning at her wheel." (Everyone began to laugh.)

The teacher: "That old woman is made of mountains."

Husn Ara: "Everything you have said, my heart has accepted; and astronomy is a very interesting thing; and I will certainly read that treatise *Sair-e Asman*." Rabi'a said softly in Husn Ara's ear, "The moon and sun are sometimes eclipsed; ask the teacher the reason for that too."

Husn Ara: "What need is there to ask? The whole world knows the reason, that it is a kind of divine punishment."

Rabi'a: "Yes, people do say so, but perhaps by asking we might find out the right answer."

Husn Ara: "I don't want to make a fool of myself by asking such a thing." The teacher, hearing the two of them whispering, asked, "What is it?"

Husn Ara: "Ma'am, nothing at all; Rabi'a was asking the reason for eclipses of the moon and sun, and I told her."

The teacher: "What?" Husn Ara: "Divine punishment."

The teacher: "Not punishment, but God's power and His majesty; and the cause of an eclipse is that the sun's light is screened off."

Husn Ara: "I didn't quite understand."

The teacher: "I have told you that the earth and the moon are not luminous of themselves. As they go round and round, when the earth comes between the sun and the moon there will be an eclipse of the moon; and when the moon comes between the sun and the earth, an eclipse of the sun. But these things are very difficult, and it is hard for you to understand them yet; God willing, when you are able to read the treatise *Sair-e Asman*, what I say will settle well in your mind."
