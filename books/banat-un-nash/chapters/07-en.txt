# Early Rising

Husn Ara: "But I can't come early at dawn. I get up from sleep only about a quarter of the day after sunrise." At the words "a quarter of the day" Mahmuda could not help laughing.

Mahmuda: "Do you get up a quarter of the day after sunrise every day?" Husn Ara: "Every day."

Mahmuda: "What time do you go to sleep?" Husn Ara: "At nightfall[1]." Mahmuda: "What a dreadful lot of sleep you have let yourself get into!" Husn Ara: "I have let myself? Is sleep something in one's own control? My eyes start closing while there is still some daylight left. Mother keeps me amused to make me stay up for supper; when she sees I keep falling asleep, she has no choice but to have me fed. Even a quarter of the day after sunrise my eyes don't open by themselves; they pull me up and sit me up by force while I'm still asleep. When they wake me before I've had my sleep out[2], the drowsiness[3] stays for hours. That is why I sleep again for an hour or so at noon."

At the mention of sleeping at noon Mahmuda laughed again and said, "If you were allowed to sleep as much as you liked, perhaps you would sleep day and night."

Husn Ara: "What can I say? This wretched sleep has fallen on me so that I somehow never have enough[4] of it. The whole house teases me, and whatever the illness, Father always says it is all the fault of sleeping. But what can I do? I have no control over sleep. Every day I resolve that today I will go to bed with everyone else, but when the time comes I feel so wretched with sleep overpowering me that I can't help it. When the signs[5] of sleep begin, I think, today I have made a very firm promise; if I go to sleep now, people will tease me; and out of that shame I steel myself and hold out for a while. But when sleep comes and surrounds me, I cannot sit up. I lean over onto the bed, and Mother says something from this side and Father from that; but before they have even finished speaking, yours truly is snoring as soon as she lies down. Whatever they say after I have lain down, I know nothing at all about it."

Mahmuda: "If you really want to cut down your sleep, it is not difficult. I can tell you a very easy[6] way."

Husn Ara: "Yes; since the whole house teases me about sleeping, I too want, if not more, at least to go to bed with everyone and get up with everyone."

Mahmuda: "Make two things a rule[7] for yourself. First, to beguile sleep you need some occupation[8] in which your mind is busy[9]. Second, tell whoever gets up early that they must wake you by any means they can, by shaking you or splashing water on you. And as soon as you are up, wash your face and hands, pull yourself together and set to some work. At first, for eight or ten days, getting up early against your habit will give you a slight heaviness in the head, but then it will become a habit; your eyes will open by themselves and the heaviness in the head will stop as well. In fact, by getting up early and taking the cool morning air your spirits will be so cheerful that you will feel fresh all day. I too used to be a dreadful sleeper; I could outsleep the men. The teacher used to advise me every day that people are not sent into this world to pass their days sleeping and lying idle. God made the day for work; is the night so short that we must sleep in the day as well? Too much sleep makes a person lazy and dull[10], and the mind thick and blunt. A person's time is a very precious thing. Every single moment of leisure is a godsend. In that time, if we can, we should work hard and gain knowledge and skills, so that both this world and the next are put right. So little by little I cut down my sleep, until now I go to sleep after everyone and get up before everyone, and I find myself healthier than before too. But the girls of the school are amazing; their houses are four or six pice by litter from here, and they arrive here before it is light. They have made a bet among themselves to see who gets to school first."

Husn Ara: "You'll see, God willing, now I too will certainly arrange it, and however I manage it, God willing, the frying pan won't even be on the fire tomorrow before you see me here."

Mahmuda and Husn Ara were talking like this when the teacher called out: "Mahmuda, you have become so friendly with your new friend so quickly that you have been talking for ages, and still your talk is not finished. What sort of consultation is this on the very first day?"

Mahmuda: "The Begam Sahib is a very nice person. Within a few words my heart went out to her. I showed her my dolls, and told her from *Mirat-ul-Uroos*, the *Chand Pand* and so on about the benefits of hard work[11] and early rising."

The teacher: "You haven't upset Husn Ara Begam by saying such things, I hope?"

Husn Ara: "Teacher, Mahmuda Begam has told me such sensible, wise and useful things as I had never heard, and her talk has pleased me very much. There is only one thing she says that I somewhat dislike: she speaks very badly of the rich."

The teacher: "Of the rich, or of their conduct?"

Husn Ara: "If she speaks badly of their conduct, she speaks badly of the rich; it is the same thing."

The teacher: "No, there is a great difference between the two. If the rich are condemned simply as rich, that means condemning wealth as such, whereas wealth is a thing of great value and worth (hearing this, Husn Ara looked at Mahmuda); but if a person who has gained wealth becomes conceited and proud, and thinks that they are the greatest of all, and that all the poor are mean and contemptible and were created to wait on them, so that they need not lift a hand themselves but can take their ease from other people's labour, and that wealth was given them only for their own ease and comfort, and does not think it their duty to give to the poor and help the needy, then such wealth is a nuisance in this world and a burden in the next."

Husn Ara: "I have some doubts about that." The teacher: "God willing, I will clear up all your doubts thoroughly; but there is very little time now, and all the girls are waiting for the stories." At the word "stories" Husn Ara was even more delighted and asked eagerly, "Oh, who is going to tell stories? You or Mahmuda Begam?"

The teacher: "Neither I nor Mahmuda, but whoever's turn it is."

Husn Ara: "Do all these girls know stories by heart?" The teacher: "Perhaps none of them knows any by heart." Husn Ara: "Then where will they tell them from?"

The teacher: "Very good stories are written in books. They can all read; whoever's turn it is will tell the story by reading it out of the book."

# Hearing of the Benefits of Reading, Husn Ara Begins to Long for It

Husn Ara: "Anyone who can read can tell stories?" The teacher: "Certainly."

Husn Ara: "Then reading is a good thing. Once you can read, you would know hundreds and thousands of stories."

The teacher: "That is only a small benefit of reading. There are hundreds of benefits, and very fine ones, which an educated person enjoys. Take stories alone: sometimes you long for someone to tell you a good new story, and it happens that either nobody knows a new story, or if they know one they have no time; and the longing just stays in your heart. If you can read, you pick up a book, and a score of storytellers are there before you with folded hands. And are wretched stories even worth counting among the benefits? Why, reading is the thing from which every kind of good sense comes. Those who have no eyes in their faces are blind outwardly; the blind at heart are those who have no knowledge. There are only two things, this world and religion, and without knowledge this world is wasted and religion is spoiled too. Whatever state a person is in, knowledge will only do them good. If they are in trouble, knowledge will comfort them as no sympathiser could; and if they are happy, knowledge will make that happiness untroubled and lasting. If there is ease, then [illegible] and independence and contentment such as come from knowledge come neither from wealth nor from power. Bless reading and long live books! An occupation for leisure, an amusement for the heart, travel without leaving home, a teacher and a friend in one. Women who cannot read pass their time so badly, God help us[12]: backbiting this one, speaking ill of that one, quarrelling with me, quarrelling with you, or lying sulking in bed[13]. If you can read, you take a book in your hand and go travelling to whatever country you like. Reading is a wonderful kind of *hazirat*[14] (summoning of spirits): you call up anyone you please and start talking to them."

Husn Ara: "Really, teacher, does reading give you that miracle too?"

The teacher: "Certainly. Look, these girls reading books now are, as it were, talking with the authors who wrote them. In short, knowledge is a fruit of Paradise; only one who has eaten it knows its taste well; its nature cannot be shown by telling and describing. Things of thousands of years ago seem as if the scene were set before your eyes."

Husn Ara: "Teacher, will I be able to learn to read?" The teacher: "You and your maids too; 'learning belongs to the one who does it'[15] is a well-known saying. Knowledge is nobody's inheritance; whoever works at it will get it."

Husn Ara: "In how many days?" The teacher: "People have spent their whole lives and never found the bottom of knowledge. As you go on reading, the taste for it grows so strong that a person cannot hold back or keep away. Whatever the pleasure, sooner or later the heart has its fill of it; the only thing it never has its fill of is knowledge."

Husn Ara: "Will I have to work very hard?"

The teacher: "Not at all. For a few days, until you can read connected text, you will certainly get bored; but once you can read a passage and get going, you will start to enjoy it, and you won't have a moment's peace without reading."

Husn Ara: "How many days does it take to learn to read a passage?" The teacher: "You are clever, God bless you; if you learn with all your heart, four months."

Husn Ara: "Oh, then please start me on reading." The teacher: "Reading? What's the hurry yet?" Husn Ara: "These days are being wasted for nothing." The teacher: "You have already wasted a good many years; a few more days won't matter."

Husn Ara: "Please, teacher, for God's sake start me on reading." The teacher: "Now, what's the hurry? You'll start; watch how the school goes on for a few more days. When you are quite sure that reading is a useful thing, there will be no shortage of reading. That is what the school is for, and that is what I am for. Well, girls, whose turn is it, and which story is it?"

Zubaida: "Me, it's my turn now, and it's the story of Nawab Masih-ul-Mulk's daughter. It has got to where the marriage was arranged of Zamiran, the daughter of the Bedouin who held this girl captive. If you say so, I'll go on." Husn Ara: "Oh teacher, please, from the beginning."

The teacher: "Yes, Zubaida dear, for Husn Ara Begam's sake tell it again from the beginning, explaining it well." Zubaida began the story.
