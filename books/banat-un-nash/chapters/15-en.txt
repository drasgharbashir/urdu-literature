# A Judgement between Townspeople and Villagers, in Which the Way of Life of Both Is Described and Each Is Warned of Its Faults, and in Which Speech, Dress, Condition, Caste and Skill Are Discussed and Many Lessons Drawn

The teacher: "Well, what did your argument begin about?"

Husn Ara: "All it was, was that I asked Khair-un-Nisa what goes on in her house; the lady began listing the women's work; I asked again, and she came out with the story of the men; I asked a third time, and just see what she says: 'Day in the daytime, night at night.'"

The teacher smiled when she heard this and said, "Listen, my dear, she gave you a fine tit-for-tat answer. You should have asked what their means of living is, or what trade their father and brothers follow."

Khair-un-Nisa: "I understood what she meant, but she was showing off about her way of talking; to put her in the wrong I dug in my heels too."

Husn Ara: "All right, now tell me, what is your means of living?"

Khair-un-Nisa: "Land-holding and farming; and since the Mutiny two or three men of the family have taken service too."

Husn Ara: "Well, tell the truth, do you like living in the city better, or in the village?"

Khair-un-Nisa: "The truth is that I don't feel at home in the city. If it weren't for this school, I couldn't stay in the city a single day."

Husn Ara: "But what is it that troubles you in the city? Aren't there girls in the neighbourhood to play and talk with?"

Khair-un-Nisa: "There are so many girls that perhaps there aren't as many in the whole city as there are in Shah Tara's lane alone; from morning to evening there's a stream of them: this one comes, that one comes."

Husn Ara: "Then there's no reason to feel lonely?"

Khair-un-Nisa: "My nature doesn't agree with those girls. Among city people [illegible: the last line of the page is cut off in the scan] they just change, as if they had never known you. You must know Badshah Begam well; her house adjoins ours. Wazir Begam, her younger daughter, became so fond of me and so friendly that day and night she wouldn't leave me for a moment. There was a wedding at the house of Daroga Musahib-us-Sultan in the Khanum ka Bazar; we were invited too; and Badshah Begam is the sister of the Daroga's own father's sister; her whole household was staying there as guests for weeks beforehand. When Wazir Begam was leaving she wanted to take me with her by force; as she was getting into the palanquin she took hold of my hand: 'Come with me'; but with great difficulty I made her understand that we are only distantly connected with the Daroga, and that it would not be proper to go without being invited. When there were three days left before the wedding, I went too. Wazir Begam was sitting with her friends; she saw me getting down, but she didn't even stir from her place. I thought she was taken up with her game and hadn't noticed. As soon as I got down I didn't even go to the people of the house; I went straight towards Wazir Begam. I stood beside her for ages; that creature of God didn't even raise her eyes to look at me. Feeling foolish, I went and sat in the hall opposite where our party was staying. My younger elder sister teased me too: 'You went off like an arrow the moment you got down, and, curse her, she didn't even speak to you.' Hearing this I was so ashamed that I broke into a sweat, and I said to myself, is this the same Wazir Begam? What has come over her? A little while later I felt thirsty. In the alcove[1] there was a new unglazed water-flask; I thought the people of the house had put it there for the guests; I got up quickly and drank some water from it; and then Wazir Begam, going red and yellow, says, 'How dare you drink from our drinking flask without asking?' With that she dashed the flask down on the floor. All the guests stared, and she disgraced me in front of the whole gathering."

The teacher: "Did you find out any reason why Wazir Begam flew into a rage for nothing?"

Khair-un-Nisa: "I thought and thought, but I couldn't make anything of it; if anything had happened I could understand it."

Mahmuda: "I'll tell you the reason; I know Wazir Begam's temper well too. In the neighbourhood she can't find girls of her own kind to play and talk with; out of that need she made friends with you. There at the wedding she found rich girls like herself; she thought it beneath her to know you."

Khair-un-Nisa: "She thought it beneath her to know me only at the wedding, but, God willing, I shall think it beneath me to know her all my life."

The teacher: "It is a strange sort of business; the rich are often proud and think everyone nothing beside themselves. Wealth too is a very bad thing; it makes a person a devil. Someone has put it very well:"

The moment the wine of wealth goes to a wicked man's head,
Another devil climbs on the head of the devil instead.

Husn Ara: "Well, all right, if Wazir Begam behaved badly to you she did a very unworthy thing; what room is left for rich and poor in love and friendship? [illegible: the last line of the page is cut off in the scan] the beauty of the ceremonies; you must certainly have liked those things."

Khair-un-Nisa: "There's no doubt that I had never had the chance to be at a city wedding, and that was the very longing that took me there; but in the end it gave my heart no pleasure. God keep me from lying, thousands of women were gathered there; but when I looked closely, they were all of one colour; whoever I looked at I found a picture of conceit and show. So many guests filled the house; not all of them were rich; whoever couldn't afford it had brought hired clothes, borrowed jewellery and made-up servants, and was showing off with those. One lady comes along with her trouser-legs hitched up to her knees to show off her silk stockings; another, on the excuse of the heat, keeps opening her neckline to show her jewellery; a third sits with her head uncovered without a care, so that people will notice how her plait is done and the cut of her hair-braid. One lady, to let people hear the sound of her anklets, must have changed her seat, God keep me from lying, about fifty times in an hour. That was the state of the ladies who had something, their own or borrowed, and were showing it off on purpose; and some were showing off with nothing at all. One lady was sitting in a scarf of coarse muslin; of her own accord, with nobody asking her anything, she says, 'Oh, just look, my dear, how the colour of a black embroidered Benares scarf runs! It was drizzling and I'd only just put it over my shoulder, and all my clothes got stained; I took it off and threw it away at once.' One lady was sitting loaded with jewellery, and a poor woman was talking to her; and that lady, whom I took for a poor thing, what does she say but: 'Look, God has made the flesh of my ears so delicate that they can't bear jewellery at all; jewelled earrings and leaves, but the moment I put in my ear-studs they began to ache; it felt as if they'd tear; so I had to wear plain rings.' At this another woman, after thinking it over, said: 'Fie[2] on the gold that tears the ears! Take care they don't swell up like pomegranates.' In short, whoever I looked at, I found suffering from the disease of conceit. When the ladies talked among themselves, it was backbiting about one and complaining about another; nothing else was mentioned. All of them were wrapped up in the colours of clothes, in cut and fashion. Hearing of the wedding, poor people had come to beg too; there were so many provisions that the cauldrons clattered day and night, but perhaps not one grain of rice was given to any poor person in God's name. Maunds of food were wasted, stolen, left to rot, but given to the needy it was not. Instead of being given anything, the poor were pushed and abused. One poor old woman had come begging, heaven knows how, right into the inner apartments; the lower half of her body was useless; heaven knows with what misery the poor thing had dragged herself there. For hours she lay in the courtyard crying out, but nobody so much as spoke to her. Everyone went on with their own eating, and I was in such a state that the old woman's voice kept reaching my ears and the morsel would not go down my throat. At first I kept watching to see whether some lady of the house would see to the old woman; when a long time passed and nobody even spoke to her, I put a handful of rice on a leavened loaf and gave it to my younger brother Ahmad: 'Go, that old woman is sitting in the courtyard; give it to her.' The moment Ahmad got up with the bread, the lady who was serving us our food noticed. Heaven knows who that lady was, but she must have been a close relation of the people of the house; she ran, snatched the bread out of Ahmad's hand, and said, 'Have you no fear of God? Such a crime at the dinner-cloth, before our very eyes?'

I said, 'It was in fear of God that I sent this bread to give to that beggar woman.' Then what does the lady say but: 'Grandfather's *fatiha* at the sweet-seller's shop! Lady, if you have so much fear of God, go home and give out a free kitchen.'

I don't mind at all that she said such a harsh thing to me; but the trouble that came on the poor old woman because of me still grieves me. When that lady saw the poor beggar woman, she was so angry with the maids, God help us, and shouted, 'Throw her out, throw out that carcass of an old woman! Who let her in here?' Without a thought the maids dragged the old woman along and threw her outside the door. I was in such a state that I wanted to scratch that lady's face, but what could I do? I got up from the dinner-cloth at once. I had a four-anna piece with me to give at the *sharbat-pila'i*[3]; I sent that same four-anna piece to the old woman by Ahmad, and told her the way to our house, and at once I sent for a litter and came home."

The teacher: "Was there not one among all those guests who felt pity for the old woman's state?" Khair-un-Nisa: "Ma'am, what pity? When the maids began dragging her, they were all roaring with laughter; after the meal the girls made up a game of mimicking the old woman."

Husn Ara: "Beggar women are often cheats too; to deceive people they pretend to be blind, lame, crippled, helpless."

The teacher: "If we suspected like that, the really needy would be left with nothing too, and the stream of charity would be cut off[4]. What business has the giver with so much inquiry[5]? And begging is a very shameful thing; no one asks without need; in the end even those who beg by trickery have been forced to it by need."

Husn Ara: "Why? Some go about begging without any need; they have no shame left; there is no easier way of earning than begging. In our neighbourhood a beggar woman died a few days ago; heaven knows how many gold coins and how many bags of rupees were found in her room. So was it need that made her beg? No, it was greed."

The teacher: "Well, is any human being free of greed?"

Husn Ara: "Everyone has greed, but there's no need to help the greedy[6]."

The teacher: "Beware lest the Merciful Lord, who gives to everyone, should act on that rule. Certainly the right of the needy comes first; it is better to give to those who are really in need; but do not go suspecting everyone without reason just so as not to give. If you give without inquiring, what will happen? Some undeserving people will take, but among that crowd[7] hundreds of deserving people will get something too. Such arguments are usually brought out by people who have no wish to give in God's name."

Husn Ara: "Well, Khair-un-Nisa dear, these faults you find in city people, aren't they found in the village? Is it only God's saints who live in the countryside?"

Khair-un-Nisa: "No, there are good and bad everywhere; it doesn't depend on village or city. But this much I can say: among villagers there is never, never so much conceit, so much show, so much pretence."

Husn Ara: "Well, city people may have bad tempers, but what elegant[8] fashions city people have!"

Khair-un-Nisa: "Perhaps the fashions of city people are elegant in your eyes; there is no modesty in them at all. Isn't this Ahmad my younger brother[9]? He saw the city boys, and he too had to grow his hair; now there's such a dreadful fuss: every other day his head is washed with emblic myrobalan; he's combed ten times a day; oil is put on morning and evening; as long as the hair was let down, sometimes it was washed with turnip water, sometimes rubbed with *mash* lentils. Mother used to say, 'The day your father comes, he'll have your head shaved on the spot; do all the beautifying you can manage. In the end this hair is bound to go to the barber's house.' As God willed, on his way to Agra Father turned up. You should have seen Master Ahmad, with a turban[10] tied round his head all the time so that Father wouldn't see his hair; but foppery had gone to his head, so how could it be hidden? Father did see it; he was very angry: 'Wretch, will you turn into a eunuch[11] like the city people?' What barber, whose hairdresser? He took the scissors from his pen-case and made a heap of the hair with his own hands. Now there goes Master Ahmad with a bald head. After that he said to Mother, 'You have brought the children here in the hope of getting them educated, but take care you don't take them back as city dandies. Look, mind you don't let Khairan[12] sit among the city girls. Never mind the ways of the city men, but the ways of the women, God protect us[13], are quite against the Law and against modesty.'"

The teacher: "Your father was quite right; but look, Khair-un-Nisa dear, the city women tease me too, but I don't imitate[14] their ways."

Khair-un-Nisa: "Ma'am, you count yourself among city people for no reason; you have neither the temper of city people nor their habits. You wear more modest clothes than even villagers; it's from seeing you that my mother has started wearing a bodice with long sleeves."

The teacher: "There, Husn Ara Begam! It is very wrong that Khair-un-Nisa should go on finding fault after fault with city people."

Husn Ara: "What can I say? I don't know much about villagers, or I'd dig up seventy generations of them this minute. And just look at these city girls; with all this pelting going on, does a single one of them say a word? How silently[15] they sit listening!"

Mahmuda: "Well, Khair-un-Nisa dear! Shahr Banu, let Khair-un-Nisa first answer one question of mine. Tell me, Khair-un-Nisa dear, is the speech of city people better, or that of villagers?"

Khair-un-Nisa: "If all you city girls take one side, what great thing is it to put me in the wrong, all alone? But will someone explain to me what makes speech good or bad?"

Mahmuda: "The beauty of speech is that there should be no harshness in it; that the words should come easily off the speaker's tongue, and should not grate on the hearer."

Khair-un-Nisa: "Villagers don't find their own speech harsh either."

Mahmuda: "How could they? They don't know the softness of city speech. You tell me, of the two kinds of speech, which do you like?"

Khair-un-Nisa: "I don't know about liking or disliking; but the same work that city people do with their soft and delicate speech, villagers get done with their rough speech; none of their purposes is left undone."

Husn Ara: "That is exactly what being a bumpkin is, not being able to tell good from bad. To me village speech sounds as bad as if someone had thrown a stone at me; they break the bones and ribs of even a plain word." Shahr Banu: "There's no doubt that villagers bring great trouble on words; no word is without a doubled letter; when they say *nun* it's *nunn*; *pani* is *panri*; *gari* is *gadi*. Khair-un-Nisa's speech has improved a lot from living in the city, but still the twist hasn't gone from her tongue; she twists and turns words in a strange way. Elder sister Mahmuda, do you remember what kind of speech Khair-un-Nisa used when she first came?"

Mahmuda: "Khair-un-Nisa is not so ungrateful; she will certainly remember this kindness of city people, that thanks to them her speech has been put right."

Khair-un-Nisa: "Never mind my speech being put right, every hair of my body is grateful to city people. The little I know of writing, reading, sewing, stitching, cooking and preparing food, everything is thanks to the city; but I say that city people have spoiled my speech."

Husn Ara: "Listen to that! It's the old saying: the donkey was given salt, and it said, 'My eyes are sore.'"

Khair-un-Nisa: "She's a great city lady, and very proud of her speech; she doesn't stop to understand; she just says things."

Husn Ara: "It's a plain thing, not a riddle or a puzzle; what is there to understand? Didn't you yourself say that city people have spoiled your speech?"

Khair-un-Nisa: "Yes, yes, spoiled it. Now, God willing, when I go home again, the people there will laugh at what I say and mimic me."

The teacher: "Khair-un-Nisa is right. It is a great nuisance: speak city speech and the villagers laugh, speak village speech and the city people tease."

Husn Ara: "Among ten noseless people[16] the one with a nose is the odd one out. Should a person cut off their nose for fear of the noseless? Khair-un-Nisa, go on speaking this way by all means."

Khair-un-Nisa: "No, my dear; it's 'as the country, so the dress'. In the city I say *kuta-bili*[17]; at home it's *atta-rotti*[18] again."

Husn Ara: "You'll go to the village all right, but it's impossible that you'll feel at home there. God willing, if you don't come straight back the very next month, then you can talk."

Khair-un-Nisa: "May God not make us village girls so flighty that we don't feel at home in our own homes. Is there anything besides this school that I will miss when I go to the village?"

Husn Ara: "There are thousands and hundreds of thousands of things to miss; if there were only one I'd tell you. You're hardly out of bed early in the morning when the cries of the gram and parched-rice sellers begin, and ......"

Khair-un-Nisa: "There is no might or power but in God[19]! Is gram food for people, or fodder for animals? Just look at the fastidiousness of city people!"

Husn Ara: "The gram you're talking about is village gram. City gram, glory be[20]! Being roasted, piping hot, fragrant, crisp[21], not a hard unroasted grain[22] among them; so soft that a toothless[23] woman can eat it without trouble."

Shahr Banu: "And the fun of it is that you can get gram in exchange for cowries and iron nails, old sacking and rags."

Husn Ara: "And the gram-seller has hardly left the lane before the tray-seller turns up: fresh *halwa-puri*, fresh crisp *kachauris*, fresh sweets, every good thing there is. One goes and another comes, one goes and another comes. It goes on like this till a watch after nightfall. Pots, cloth, gold braid and edging, ice, fruit, flowers, vegetables, whatever you want, you can buy sitting at home. What a great comfort that is! Kebabs, each spicier than the last; delicious sweets, each a greater treat than the last. Buy six cowries' worth and they'll still give it to you in a leaf-cup; it isn't that when you go to buy something you have to take a begging bowl from home. The cries of the sellers charm the hearts of those who hear them. The truth is that the city is the paradise of this world. God keep me in the city; rather than live in a village I'd sooner die."

Khair-un-Nisa: "My, what a greedy girl! A mouth like a needle and a belly like a well; she's just dying for food. Among us villagers the wives and daughters of decent people won't even put bazaar food on their tongues; among us it's counted a great fault."

Husn Ara: "Oh! You're very respectable, very noble; why not? You live in Sharifpur, after all, and we city people are low and base."

Khair-un-Nisa: "Why not, then? Is there any doubt about the nobility of people from outside? We are nobles of the true coin."

Husn Ara: "What is your caste?"

Khair-un-Nisa: "Poor Shaikhs who eat thin lentils."

Husn Ara: "I'm a Mughal. Well, is there no other Shaikh in this school?"

Halima: "I am." Kulsum: "I'm a Shaikh too." Zubaida: "We go by the name of Shaikhs too."

Khair-un-Nisa: "I don't know anything about Halima and Kulsum; but what sort of Shaikh Zubaida 'goes by the name of', I know very well. And Zubaida put it rightly; she didn't call herself a Shaikh, she said she goes by the name of Shaikhs. Zubaida! Which Shaikhs among the Shaikhs are you?"

Zubaida: "I don't know which Shaikhs; I've only heard that we're Shaikhs."

Khair-un-Nisa: "Why, are you Qureshi? Usmani? Siddiqi?" (laughing) "Dafali?"

Zubaida: "I don't know that; but you're a Dafali."

Khair-un-Nisa: "What's your mother's brother's name?" Zubaida: "Mirza Yawar Ali Beg."

Khair-un-Nisa: "And your mother's sister's husband?" Zubaida: "Mir Taqi." Khair-un-Nisa: "Your sister's husband?" Zubaida: "Dilawar Khan."

Khair-un-Nisa: "My dear, you're a regular hotchpotch of a Shaikh, all mixed up: four castes in one house! Begam Sahib, have you seen what city Shaikhs are like?"

Husn Ara: "Is it forbidden to marry into another caste?"

Khair-un-Nisa: "It isn't forbidden in the Law, but the nobles from outside think it worse than forbidden. The rule in the city is that they don't look at caste or community at all; they look at looks and at money, and then there's no objection to taking a daughter-in-law nor any shame in giving a daughter. And among villagers the bone (the stock) must be good, whether there's wealth or not."

Mahmuda: "But what is gained by that? When it is not forbidden by God and the Prophet, caste is nothing."

Khair-un-Nisa: "I don't know what's gained or not gained; it's something that has come down from our elders."

The teacher: "No custom in the world began without a reason. Caste too had great benefits and still has. There is no custom in the world older than caste, and there must be some benefit or other in it, since to this day it has not stopped. From the beginning of the creation of the world, for several thousand years, the order of kingship could not be established; on every side there was plunder and pillage, there were robberies every other day, and people were always fighting among themselves. In those days both life and property were unsafe. So people at first lived in bands, and the descendants of one grandfather or great-grandfather became one group. The group with the most people was counted the strongest. So in every group there was a pact that marriages should take place among themselves, and that the strength of the group should not be allowed to decrease. That is how the custom of caste and brotherhood spread in the world and has gone on to this day. Some castes were also separated according to trades, for example weavers, shoemakers, blacksmiths, carpenters and so on; and the benefit of this was that the people of that caste, regarding themselves as holding that trade by contract, could work in security, and outsiders would not lay a hand on that work; and this custom still goes on. Little by little the order of kingship became well established; now neither a band nor a group is needed to protect life and property; so the thought of caste and brotherhood has grown less, and among city people it has gone altogether. The distinction of caste according to trades has also grown less."

Khair-un-Nisa: "So caste is nothing to be proud of?"

The teacher: "All people are equal. If there is anything to be proud of, it is skill."

Khair-un-Nisa: "But if caste has come down from of old, pride in caste has come down from of old too."

The teacher: "The people from whom the castes began were people of great distinction and chiefs in their groups; if anyone may be proud, it is they. And indeed people have always been proud of caste, for there has never been an age without its braggarts. When the able elders who had made a name died, no one of name and distinction arose among their descendants; now what are these to be proud of? Poor things, they lie gnawing the bones of the dead."

Khair-un-Nisa: "Whatever you say, one can't be the equal of cotton-carders and weavers."

The teacher: "Then you have no business finding fault with Husn Ara Begam's wealth. Her pride in wealth has at least some ground; God has given her wealth. What have you but empty boasting? And in God's court there is no question about it at all. Look how the Sayyid ladies of today hold themselves aloof[24]; yet the Prophet called his daughter Hazrat Fatima, from whom the Sayyids take their root and origin, and said, 'O Fatima! Do not be deceived because you are the Prophet's daughter, but make provision for the next world.' When this was so with Hazrat Fatima herself, what count are others? How good a Hindi *doha* (couplet) is. The couplet:"

Nobody asks about caste or creed[25];
Who worships Hari is Hari's indeed.

Husn Ara: "Well, Khair-un-Nisa, you'll never mention caste again now, will you?"

Khair-un-Nisa: "And you won't keep flaunting your wealth at every word?"

The teacher: "It isn't only caste and wealth; one should not be proud of anything at all."

Husn Ara: "Villagers may be nobles of the true coin, but they have such strange coarse, clumsy and ungainly figures that you can't help wanting to laugh. Not one of them has the least touch of delicacy; they spoil a perfectly good face."

Khair-un-Nisa: "I have already answered about the fashions and the cut of city people's clothes; if fashion means immodesty, then I say goodbye to such fashion. And just explain to me what delicacy means."

Husn Ara: "I don't know how to do such fancy talk."

Mahmuda: "Delicacy means being thin and feeble, with withered hands and feet, eating little, unable to bear work or hardship."

Khair-un-Nisa: "Well, Begam Sahib, that's what delicacy means, isn't it, what Mahmuda said?"

Husn Ara: "Certainly."

Khair-un-Nisa: "Then I lose and you win. May God not make us villagers sickly and crippled. What topsy-turvy sense! Proud of being disabled and vain of disease!"

After this everyone was silent, and Khair-un-Nisa said: "If anyone else has the courage to find fault with village girls, let her say it."

Husn Ara: "There are plenty of faults still left. Is there any doubt that village girls lack *saliqa*?"

Khair-un-Nisa: "I don't understand much of the city girls' vocabulary[26]. First tell me, what is it called *saliqa*?"

Husn Ara: "Manners in sitting and rising and the proper way of talking are called *saliqa*."

Khair-un-Nisa: "'By God, by God', and '*qibla* and *ka'ba*'[27], and '*mujra*'[28] and '*kornish*', and 'your holy[29] temper', that's it, isn't it?"

Husn Ara: "Yes, that's part of *saliqa* too; not like village girls: '*Bobo salam! Bobo salam!*'"

Husn Ara mimicked the speech of village girls in such a way that all the girls burst out laughing, and Khair-un-Nisa herself could not keep from laughing.

Khair-un-Nisa: "That's the same taunt about speech again. What use are false warmth[30], show of longing, put-on affection, love to your face and fondness for display? We people from outside are plain and simple, little on the lips and much in the heart. It was just this pretence that deceived me and did for me at Wazir Begam's hands. A sweet knife dipped in poison; to your face 'auntie, granny', behind your back a mortal enemy. Get along, you cheats! I've seen your *saliqa*: a high shop and tasteless sweets. I know you through and through. Enough, don't make me open my mouth too wide; I'll rip open the envelope of your fine manners this minute."

Mahmuda: "Begam Sahib, stop now; she's angry at Wazir Begam's faithlessness."

Khair-un-Nisa: "I'm not angry at all. By all means let her find fault. I'll put her in the wrong yet."

Husn Ara: "Really?" Khair-un-Nisa: "Really, and really."

Husn Ara: "Well, tell the truth, are village girls without skills or not?"

Khair-un-Nisa: "Forgive me, but that objection doesn't sound well from your mouth; if some other girl said it I'd answer."

Husn Ara (abashed): "What have I got to do with it? Until now I thought wealth was a skill; now, God willing, I'll learn a little. But the city is full of skilled people: the best of sewing, the best of embroidery, the best of work in every lane and street."

Khair-un-Nisa: "True, there are no such skills in the village, and ......"

Husn Ara: "Well, thank goodness, you've admitted one thing."

Khair-un-Nisa: "Just listen. The reason for not knowing these skills is that in the village these things are not valued, and villagers neither need nor are used to such luxuries."

Husn Ara: "No, villagers have only so-so[31] sense too."

Mahmuda: "Villagers don't have the means of improving their minds; growing grain from the earth and raising cattle are their two great tasks."

Khair-un-Nisa: "Farming is a very difficult work in itself. Just look at the *Daulat-e Hind*[32]; what wonderful methods it describes for improving the soil and making the crop of the best quality. But the truth is that nobody does any of it; they plough the land and sow the seed, and that's all there is to it."

Husn Ara: "Do women in the villages farm too?"

Khair-un-Nisa: "Poor people, among whom there is no custom of seclusion, their wives and daughters work in the fields alongside the men; but it isn't so among people like us. This is our farming: we sow vegetables at home; guava, pomegranate, peach, *phalsa*, sour lime, lemon, orange, jujube, mango, fruit trees like that, if there's room we plant them; or a bed or two of flowers to amuse ourselves. But still villagers are not so ignorant of this example of God's power[33] as to be amazed at the sight of a plain-rice tree[34] and a sweet-pilau tree."

The teacher: "I too certainly feel sorry[35] for the village girls when I think that they have no means of improving[36] their minds. Poor things, they are caught up in all kinds of superstitions. Spells and charms, ritual offerings to ward off evil, the evil eye, jinn and possession, ghosts and spirits, witches, omens, auguries, exorcism, magic and incantations, vows and offerings: villagers are mostly given to thinking about such things. And why shouldn't this evil be in the city too? Now at long last the Maulvis, by preaching their lessons, have broken down this unbelief. Here is Khair-un-Nisa herself; what trouble I had getting her younger sister vaccinated against smallpox, God help us[37]!"
