# Giving Charity and Then Expecting Gratitude for It

Husn Ara: "Perhaps? I know her. Every Ramazan she used to come regularly to our house to take food from the free kitchen[1]. Now that she's been in service with you for four days, she's got above herself[2]; she's forgotten those days."

Mahmuda: "Why is the free kitchen given out at your house?" Husn Ara: "It is given out in the name of God."

Mahmuda: "Is that what 'in the name of God' means, that whoever takes anything from it must stay your slave for life, and must respect and honour you as your other paid servants do? There will be precious little[3] reward for such a free kitchen!"

Husn Ara: "If she doesn't respect us, should she beat us with shoes, then?" Mahmuda: "Heaven forbid, what have shoes to do with it?"

Husn Ara: "My dear, when people of such low standing speak up so boldly, it's as good as beating us with shoes."

Mahmuda: "If the free kitchen was in God's name, then you have done her no favour. One act of charity cannot be repaid twice over, so that you take the reward in the next world and also want respect and honour in this. So to give charity and then expect the person to respect you is a wrong expectation; and if you don't give it a place in your heart, then all people are equal: as you are, so is the maid."

Husn Ara: "You are free to think yourself the maid's equal, but I, by God's grace, am the daughter of a really rich family, and even now there must be ten or twenty like her in service at our house."

# Husn Ara Asks the Maid's Pardon

Mahmuda: "It is sheer high-handedness that, because you are rich, everyone in the world must respect you, and sheer obstinacy that, because you are rich, you may abuse whoever you please."

Husn Ara: "I didn't use any abuse."

Mahmuda: "Does abuse have horns on its head[4]? You called her a liar, a wretch, a carcass, blind-eyes, and said you would beat her flat with logs."

Husn Ara: "If that's abuse, then heaven help us! What should I have called her, 'Your Honour'? Should I have made her my lord?"

Mahmuda: "Must you either call her 'Your Honour' and 'my lord', or else go straight to 'liar, wretch, blind-eyes'? If someone, don't be offended, said these same words to you, how bad would you feel?"

Husn Ara: "If I minded, I'd mind, but these people are of that standing[5]; there's no reason for them to mind."

Mahmuda: "Yes, that is just the mistake. This maid is not of that standing; she is poor, but she is respectable."

Husn Ara: "Only you could say that someone poor is respectable!" Mahmuda: "Certainly; to you wealth alone is honour, but to me, and indeed to God and the Prophet and the wise people of the world, goodness is the great honour."

Husn Ara: "Well, let me hear about the good deeds of Diyanat Begam[6] too. What free kitchen does she give out? Has she built a *sarai* for the comfort of travellers? Has she had a well dug in the wilderness for the thirsty? Has she settled an allowance on some widow? Has she set aside food for the travellers at the mosque?"

Mahmuda: "Are those the only good deeds? Those are the good deeds that fall to the share of the wealthy. Now let me count up Diyanat's good deeds. Look, she is as poor as that and works as a maid, but she is so very honest that she thinks a hundred thousand rupees no more than dust. She gets six chapatis here in the morning and six in the evening; she eats five, sometimes four, herself, and always gives one or one and a half to the mosque in God's name. That one chapati of hers is far greater than your free kitchen. Look, she is at an age when she can't even see the eye[7] of a needle; do you know why she is sitting with that bundle[8] open now? She is going to patch the neighbour's children's clothes. Twice a day she fetches the shopping for six or seven households for nothing. If anyone among the neighbours is ill, for God's sake she takes the urine sample to the doctor with her own hands, has the prescription made up at the druggist's shop, strains and prepares the medicine and gives it, and goes to ask after them ten times a day. She never tells a lie; she never tells tales on anyone; she never speaks ill of anyone behind their back; she never makes excuses when someone needs a job done; she gives everyone good advice and good counsel. You may think her of no account; but whenever your grandfather Hakim Sahib came here on a visit, he always asked after this same Diyanat and talked with her for a long time with great kindness (attention)."

Husn Ara: "Oh, so Diyanat is a very good person."

Mahmuda: "Certainly, she is an angel of a person. The teacher shows the maid more respect than anyone would show even their own mother."

Husn Ara: "Will Diyanat really have been hurt by what I said?"

Mahmuda: "What you said was bound to hurt; perhaps, because of her good nature, she didn't take it badly." Husn Ara: "Well, then what will happen?"

Mahmuda: "What could happen? Has the poor woman got an army to take revenge on you?"

Husn Ara: "Well, what else will she do? At the most she'll go and tell Mother; and I'm not afraid of Mother at all."

Mahmuda: "You can rest assured about that; the maid won't so much as mention it to your mother or anyone else; she is a very self-controlled person."

Husn Ara: "Then what is there to fear? I said it, and that's that."

Mahmuda: "Oh dear, that is just the dreadful thing. If her heart has been hurt, it may be that God has been displeased. His blow is a terrible blow; His stick makes no sound; in a moment He can do whatever He wills; He can make a healthy person blind or a leper; He can make a king go begging." Husn Ara: "Oh, then for God's sake get Diyanat to forgive my fault."

Mahmuda: "I had no share in the fault, so I will have no share in the apology either. It was you who abused her for nothing; you must ask her to forgive you yourself."

Husn Ara: "All right, I'll say it when Diyanat is on her own."

Mahmuda: "What need is there for her to be on her own?"

Husn Ara: "Oh yes, now I, the daughter of a rich family, am to fold my hands before a maid in front of everyone!"

Mahmuda: "Justice is just this: since you humiliated her in front of everyone, you should make her happy in front of everyone too. Riches have got so far into your head that I don't know what you think you are. Whenever I hear a proud word from your mouth, I tremble: look out, God keep us."

Hearing this, Husn Ara ran and threw her arms round Diyanat and began to cry. Tears welled up in Diyanat's eyes too, and at once she lifted Husn Ara up, hugged her, and called down thousands of blessings on her. Having been forgiven, Husn Ara went back to Mahmuda: "There, your ladyship, I've made it up with Diyanat."

Mahmuda: "Begam Sahib, tell the truth, how do you feel in your heart now?"

Husn Ara: "I see that admitting a fault is no cause for disgrace at all. If I hadn't gone, I would always have been ashamed to look Diyanat in the eye. What you said left a kind of nagging worry; now I feel a strange sort of happiness in my heart that I can't describe." Mahmuda: "There is no doubt that what you did showed great spirit and true nobility. Everyone who hears of it will be pleased and will praise you; and in the court of God its reward is so great that no blessing in the world can equal it. In all the books I have read so far, it is written that there is no greater sin in the world than hurting someone's heart, and no greater good deed than comforting it." Mahmuda and Husn Ara talked like this and the work went on too. As this conversation ended, the frying pan came off the fire. A little of each thing was taken, and a full basket went to the mosque in the name of God; what was left was first set before the teacher. But the teacher was fasting; she said to the girls, "You eat and drink and enjoy it." So they all ate heartily together.

# Goodness and True Charity

Diyanat too had been given a share equal to everyone's. Diyanat took the savouries in her lap and slipped out quietly, and Mahmuda saw her, and whispered to Husn Ara, "Begam Sahib, come, let me show you the proof of what I said." And taking Husn Ara by the hand she stood her behind the cover of the window. Meanwhile Diyanat reached the neighbour's house. She called all the children lovingly by name, sat them beside her, and fed them with her own hand the savouries she had been given here. When they had finished eating, she washed all their hands and faces, got up to leave, and as she went she warned them all: "Mind, nobody drink water on top of fried food, or you'll get a cough." When Diyanat came home, Mahmuda asked, "Well, maid dear, how were the savouries?"

The maid: "Glory be to God[9]! Delicious; I liked them very much." Hearing this, Mahmuda said to Husn Ara, "Did you see what a good woman this is? However low a person may be, still, when there's frying, when there's something new, the mouth is bound to water; and old people especially have a dreadful craving[10] for food. But look how Diyanat has curbed her desire, and what indifference[11] she shows in her poverty, that she didn't even taste the savouries herself."

Husn Ara: "Are Diyanat and the neighbour related in some way?"

Mahmuda: "Not at all. Diyanat is a Sayyid and the neighbour a Pathan; and the neighbour comes from somewhere near Panipat and Karnal; she is all alone and has no relations here at all. Everyone is kind to their relations; it is Diyanat's large heart that without knowing a person she gives her heart and soul for them. And just look at her care for them, 'Mind, nobody drink water'; and look at her secrecy[12], how quietly she went; and when I asked, how she praised the savouries, as if she had eaten them herself[13]. That is what is called true charity; not giving in the name of God and then wanting a name and show[14] for oneself. What need is there to give out a free kitchen and give to the beat of a drum? The right way to give is so that not even the next ear hears of it."

Just then Diyanat said to Mahmuda, "Young lady, the frying is all done; I have some pice left over from the rupee you gave me tied in the corner of my wrap; they'll come loose somewhere; you'd better settle the account."

Mahmuda: "Do you remember what things you brought?"

The maid: "Six annas' worth of ghee, two pice of sesame, an anna and a half of gram flour, three annas of sugar, one anna of curd, two annas of fine flour; that's all the things that came out of that rupee."

Mahmuda: "Kaniz Fatima dear, look, there are eight pice tied in the corner of the maid's wrap; untie them and bring them." Kaniz Fatima brought the pice and put them in Mahmuda's hand.

Husn Ara: "Let me see how many pice there are." When she counted, there were eight. Then in amazement she asked Mahmuda, "Why, how did you know there were eight without counting?"

Mahmuda: "By arithmetic." Husn Ara: "What arithmetic?"

Mahmuda: "The arithmetic is that there are sixteen annas to the rupee and four pice to the anna. When I added up the spending the maid told me, it came to fourteen annas; two annas were left, which at four pice each make eight pice."

Husn Ara: "That's a wonderful thing! I've never heard of such a thing in my house."

Mahmuda: "It is wonderful and very useful. All the dealings of the world, buying on credit[15], trade, everything depends on arithmetic. It can't be that there's no arithmetic in your house; your house is a very rich house, and even in the poorest of houses there is always a little arithmetic."

Husn Ara: "Is this something to be studied too?"

Mahmuda: "Is there anything in the world that is not studied? But people learn small sums by word of mouth as well. Banias, grocers, sweet-sellers and the like all know as much arithmetic as they need."

Husn Ara: "Do these girls of the school know arithmetic too?"

Mahmuda: "Some of them know a great deal; they work out hard problems that an unlettered person couldn't work out with months of thinking. And even those who are beginners know far more than the people of the bazaar. If you like, I'll ask them some arithmetic questions in front of you; you'll see how quickly[16] they answer."

Husn Ara: "Very well."

# Notes

[1] *Langar*: "grain or food given out as charity".

[2] *Maghz chal gaye*: "that is, she has become conceited".

[3] *Khak*: "this is an idiom, meaning there will be no reward at all".

[4] "That is, is abuse the name of some animal with horns on its head?"

[5] *Auqat*: "standing".

[6] "Calling the maid Diyanat 'Diyanat Begam' is Husn Ara's mockery."

[7] *Naka*: "the hole of a needle".

[8] *Buqcha*: "a little bundle in which women keep their sewing things".

[9] *Subhan Allah*: "God is pure; this phrase is said where one praises something".

[10] *Hoka*: "a fierce craving is called *hoka*".

[11] *Istighna*: "indifference".

[12] *Ikhfa*: "concealment".

[13] "Thank goodness, in spite of this Diyanat did not tell any lie."

[14] *Nam-o-numud*: "show".

[15] "Getting flour and such things from the Bania on credit is called *uchapat*."

[16] *Tar tar*: "quickly".
