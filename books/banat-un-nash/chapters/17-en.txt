# Husn Ara Reads Aloud the Story of Masih-ul-Mulk

Meanwhile Husn Ara too had quietly gained enough ability to read a passage. She could not read fluently, but she did not stumble either; now and then, when an Arabic or Persian word came along, she would pause for a moment and go on. At the mention of stories Husn Ara's heart began to flutter, and she went to Mahmuda and said softly, "Today I feel like reading."

Mahmuda: "By all means." Husn Ara: "I'm shy of telling the teacher."

Mahmuda: "What is there to be shy about? I'll tell her."

Husn Ara: "Nobody knows I've been studying; they'll all be surprised to hear it."

Mahmuda: "So they will." Husn Ara: "They'll all listen hard; I hope I don't lose my nerve."

Mahmuda: "There's no stranger among them; while you read, don't think of anything but the book."

Husn Ara: "Is the next part of the story very hard?"

Mahmuda: "No; you read the *Muntakhab-ul-Hikayat* without hesitating; this is far easier than that."

Husn Ara: "You sit next to me." Mahmuda: "Of course."

Husn Ara: "The teacher won't be cross, will she?"

Mahmuda: "Not at all; is it something to be pleased about or cross about?"

Husn Ara: "Oh dear, I'm frightened." Mahmuda: "Of the teacher being cross?"

Husn Ara: "No, of reading in front of everyone."

Mahmuda: "Why, just keep your eyes down and read on; in a little while you'll get into the flow."

Just then Rabi'a arrived with the book. Just as she was about to read, Mahmuda said, "Teacher, if you allow it, today Husn Ara Begam will read the story." Everyone was amazed to hear this. The teacher: "Really?" Mahmuda: "Husn Ara Begam has been studying with me quietly for several months; now she can read a passage."

The teacher: "At the beginning she asked me once to teach her; I held her back, thinking I would start her when her eagerness had grown really keen. After that she never mentioned it; I thought she did not mean to yet."

Mahmuda: "Ma'am, she began studying that very day. God bless her, she has a mind such as I have never seen: in one day she learned the whole alphabet, and God has given her such a memory that whatever she has read is like a line cut in stone."

# Self-Respect and Pride

The teacher: "Husn Ara Begam! I am very pleased to hear from Mahmuda about your studying; and for having gained the ability to read a passage in so short a time, I give you my praise in front of all the girls. I know that the reason you studied secretly with Mahmuda is that your self-respect did not like to read the alphabet in front of little girls who read books; and I applaud you for that self-respect. God has given people self-respect so that they may take its help in good works. Self-respect is the whip for sloth and laziness; through self-respect eagerness grows keen and resolutions grow firm; self-respect is God's help and unseen support for us; for overcoming every difficulty and removing every obstacle self-respect is a fine weapon. Self-respect turns labour into comfort and weariness into ease. [illegible: most of one line cut off at the foot of the scan] Self-respect is the arrow that never misses its mark; self-respect is the plan whose result is always success and victory. Happy are those whose natures have self-respect, and fortunate are those who have it. Husn Ara Begam, of your thousand good qualities one is this self-respect. Girls! all of you take care that your self-respect does not grow dim and dull. Husn Ara Begam, you must have understood for yourself how fine a part of your life these few months were that you spent in studying. Who knows how many such parts you and I have wasted in talk and sleep; and if we had put them too to useful things, as you did this time, what benefits might we not have gained! Alas that a person should have time in their power and waste it! Husn Ara Begam! now that you have begun this good work, carry it through to the end with vigour. A person who is eager but stops half-way, and resolves but falls short, is worse than one who has no eagerness at all. It would be a great shame if the people who have heard of your studying should one day hear that Husn Ara Begam has given up studying. Husn Ara Begam, no one knows the full extent of their own ignorance; everyone, with however much they know, thinks themselves a great scholar, like the rat that found a knob of turmeric and thought itself a druggist, and is proud of a little knowledge, so that . . . It would be no wonder if you too were proud of the state of being able to read any book that comes before you, and thought you had learned everything. Beware! Never, never let such a thought into your heart. I have told you before that no one has found the bottom of the river of knowledge. Reading a passage is not called knowledge; it is only the means of gaining it. Knowledge is the things that are written in books: arithmetic, geography, history, ethics, natural science, medicine, grammar, syntax, logic, geometry, mathematics and so on. Husn Ara Begam! there is not much benefit in knowing many things and reading many books. The fruit of all the sciences is that a person should find out the root of every thing and the bottom of every matter. Form the habit from the start of thinking and reflecting. Whatever thing you see, you should think about what it really is, and whatever you hear, about the reason for it. The things we see day and night, as if we knew nothing at all about them, water, air, fire, trees, grain, cloth, jewellery, pots, indeed all the things of need and housekeeping; the sky, the stars; has anyone ever thought what they are? And those who have thought, have understood that every single thing is a science in itself. Sa'di, God's mercy on him[1], has put it very well:"

The leaves of the green trees, to the eye of one who's wise[2]:
Each leaf a volume where the Maker's knowledge lies.

"In short, let the mind keep the habit of thought and reflection, and let the intellect catch the fever of inquiry[3]; that is just the meaning of 'for one maund of knowledge ten maunds of sense are needed'[4]; otherwise, what is the use of learning like a parrot:"

Humanity is one thing, and good sense another;
How much the parrot was taught, yet a beast it remained.

"Well now, let the story begin." Husn Ara began to read. For two or three sentences her voice faltered, but then she began to read clearly.

# The Rest of the Tale of Masih-ul-Mulk: His Going on the Hajj after His Dismissal[5], and How His Pampered Daughter Nazparwar, Who Had Been Brought Up[6] like a Rich Man's Daughter, Fell into the Hands of the Bedouin Together with the Slave Girl[7] Hoshmand, and Suffered in That State for Lack of Skill, and Was Set Free through Hoshmand's Efforts

When Masih-ul-Mulk's doom came, he set about marrying off his daughter. It was the first such event in his house; he gave no thought to what might come after[8], and spent everything he had gathered by violence and oppression, by robbing people of their rights; indeed he took on a debt of thousands, and ruined himself for the sake of name and show. Seeing the preparations for the wedding, His Majesty[9] grew suspicious, and the oppressed[10] got their chance to speak up; in short, his name was struck off the royal rolls. No sooner was his name struck off than his creditors began to harass him; the King's dependants were angry with him already, and they began to jeer at him as he went about. Masih-ul-Mulk could think of nothing better than to go to the Ka'ba of God: the cat set off on the Hajj after eating nine hundred rats. When they heard of the journey, his servants and attendants gave him a flat refusal[11]; the slave girls and slaves of the house slipped away[12]. Out of that great crowd only one slave girl, named Hoshmand, went with them. Because she had played with the Hakim Sahib's younger daughter Nazparwar and was of the same age, she was very fond of her, and it was for that bond that she chose to be Nazparwar's companion[13]. Hoshmand was the daughter of a slave, but she was very wise and truly lived up to her name ("the Sensible"). Though her good sense set her apart among the household servants and she was capable and discerning[14], her intelligence longed for freedom; she hated her condition, and would think to herself that there are three kinds of people in the house: first, the family themselves, who have every comfort and all the power; second, the servants, who wait on the family but take their wages as they please, and when anyone is unhappy with their service they leave and go; third, we people who are called slave girls and slaves; there is no end to our labour and misery; we can neither leave and go anywhere, nor have any right to wages; we wretches are the lowest of all. Hoshmand kept searching for the reason: what wrong have I done, that in punishment for it I am a prisoner for life? Think as she might, she could find nothing. Once or twice she meant to talk about it with her fellows, but she found none of them with the heart and mind for it. They all had only so much sense that if one day there was too much work, or they were beaten, they would cry for a little while, and then, washed clean, be just as before. A half-line:

A drop fell on a greasy pot and slid away.

But Hoshmand always kept herself in check[15]; never mind being beaten, if anyone said a harsh word to her she would grieve over it for months. Her condition was always before her eyes, and so she was always sad; when she was alone she would sometimes weep over her misery. The idea of freedom had so filled her mind that nothing pleased her; and the more Hoshmand longed for freedom, the more contemptible she was in the eyes of the family; Nazparwar in particular was furious at her airs, and used to say: "A slave girl with such . . .[16] notions! Living in huts and dreaming of palaces!" Hoshmand quietly found out about herself that in the famine of the year ninety-four her mother's father had sold her mother for two loaves; her mother was six or seven years old at the time. When she grew up, the Hakim Sahib married her to one of his slaves; Hoshmand was their only daughter, and then both mother and father died. When Hoshmand learned this she said to herself: "Certainly this house has done me a very great kindness in bringing up me and my mother; but the mere right of having brought me up does not mean that I must be kept in such disgrace and misery all my life. The right of upbringing is the same over me as over the children of the house themselves; then why should I, when I grow up, remain a slave girl, while they are counted as people of equal rank? Only because my grandfather was in want of two loaves in the famine, and these people saved his life by giving two loaves at the time; but since they had the means, it was their duty too to help my grandfather. In the world people do far greater kindnesses than that, but nobody makes a slave of anyone; and I can't understand either how my grandfather could sell my mother. Of course my mother was his daughter, but it does not seem right that anyone should have the power to sell anyone." In short, Hoshmand's mind was full of a score of such thoughts. When the Hakim Sahib's fortunes failed and all the slave girls and slaves were seen going about like camels without nose-ropes[17], nobody felt sure of Hoshmand either; in fact her staying on after all the rest, and being keener in her work and service than before, astonished everyone. At last, when there were two days left before the departure, Nazparwar herself said, "Well, Hoshmand, the freedom you have longed for for years, now is the time for it; go where you like, and welcome." Hoshmand said: "Certainly I value freedom very highly, but that did not mean that I would leave this house and part from you. I have no tie with anyone in the world except this house; if in these bad times even my life can be of use to you, and the right of my upbringing be repaid, then, God willing, I shall not grudge giving it." So the Hakim Sahib, taking his wife, his younger daughter and Hoshmand, reached Bombay, and there sold the precious jewels[18] he had with him, put the necessary goods and cash aboard the ship, and on the sixteenth day arrived at Jidda[19]. The Hajj was still a long way off; it was decided to go first to Medina. On the way the Bedouin surrounded them; they plundered their goods and wealth bit by bit, and a Bedouin named Jabir seized both Hoshmand and Nazparwar and took them away, and taking them home handed them over to his wife: "There, make these two your slave girls, and get them to do the work of the house; when we marry off Raihana and Zamiran[20], we'll give these slave girls in their dowry." For poor Nazparwar it was as if a mountain of misery had fallen on her: home gone, country gone, mother and father gone, kin and friends gone; from a lady she had become a slave girl, and on top of that a useless and despised slave girl. In Jabir's house there was no betel nut to cut, no betel to prepare; otherwise perhaps Nazparwar might have managed somehow, the beggar's anger falling on the beggar's own head[21]. Here the work was herding sheep, goats and camels, watering them, milking, and grinding and cooking for the house; and none of these was within Nazparwar's power. Nazparwar did nothing but weep day and night; at the sight of her misery Hoshmand's heart[22] too came into her mouth. For two or three days nobody asked anything of them; perhaps Jabir said something about them to his wife and daughters, but they did not understand. Nazparwar went on weeping, but Hoshmand began to lend a hand in the work of the house. One day Jabir was talking with his wife and kept glaring at Nazparwar. Hoshmand understood that now he was displeased with Nazparwar's weeping and not working; she was frightened, and went and said to Nazparwar, "What was written in our fate has happened, and whatever more is written will happen; but what is gained by weeping? It is five or six days now, and not a grain has passed your lips; your eyes are all swollen; take heart a little." At these words Nazparwar began to weep even more helplessly. After a while Hoshmand began again: "The weeping won't be finished today, after all; this is a grief that will last all our lives; if we live, we shall have plenty of time to weep."

Nazparwar: "What can I do? My heart keeps welling up from inside."

Hoshmand: "True, it is misery indeed; however much you grieve, it is not too much; but what I say is, what will come of it?"

Nazparwar: "I shall give up my life like this."

Hoshmand: "Ah, if giving up one's life were in one's own power, wouldn't that be a fine thing? I would gladly die, but I cannot bear to see your suffering."

Nazparwar: "I've begun to have fainting fits already; in a day or two my life will go too."

Hoshmand: "All this has happened, but so far God has not let us be dishonoured; now I fear that too."

Nazparwar started at this and asked, "What?" Hoshmand: "The Bedouin who seized us and brought us here is called Jabir; today he was talking with his wife and kept glaring at you; I don't like the look of him."

Nazparwar: "But what was he saying?" Hoshmand: "He went on for a long time in his own tongue, heaven knows what, but it was certainly about you."

Nazparwar: "What did you make out of what he wants?" (Today, for the first time in her whole life, Nazparwar spoke to Hoshmand as "you" in the polite form.)

Hoshmand: "I think what he wants is for you to stop weeping and do the work." At these words Nazparwar broke down[23] again, and after a long while she pulled herself together and said, "If I don't do as he wishes, he'll kill me, that's all; and I'm ready to give up my life myself."

Hoshmand: "I am bolder than you about dying; but that is just my fear, that perhaps he won't kill you but will dishonour you."

Nazparwar: "Then what should we do?" Hoshmand: "The stone has come, and it is heavy[24]; we must lift it."

Nazparwar: "You know I can't do any work."

Hoshmand: "I'll do the work; you just go about with me."

Nazparwar: "Let's slip away at night and run."

Hoshmand: "A strange country, strange people; we don't know the names of the towns or the way to anywhere; our feet haven't the strength[25] to walk; where could we run to?"

Nazparwar: "Is there no news of Father?" Nazparwar: "This Jabir must surely know." [TN: Hoshmand's reply between these two remarks seems to be missing from the printed text.]

Hoshmand: "But who will ask him? In the first place we don't know his language; in the second, he seems such a harsh-tempered man that his own daughters are terrified at the sight of his face; out of fear they don't even go in front of him."

Nazparwar: "Is there any kind person among the women?"

Hoshmand: "How can we know yet? But the elder daughter, Zamiran, seems rather friendly; when she looks at us, there is pity in her eyes."

Nazparwar: "Come, let's tell her our misery." Hoshmand: "In what language?"

Nazparwar: "Let's make her understand somehow by signs."

Hoshmand: "We shouldn't hurry yet."

Nazparwar: "What trouble not knowing the language has brought on us!"

Hoshmand: "I think that not knowing the language is doing us a great deal of good just now. In the first place, if we cannot do some work as these people wish, not understanding is a reasonable excuse; in the second, your intentions and mine cannot become known to them; we can talk freely, and they will not know a thing."

Nazparwar: "Jabir's wife and daughters do all the work with their own hands; will they now throw all the work on us and stand aside?"

Hoshmand: "No, it seems a very fine custom among these people that they keep their slave girls and slaves equal with the family in work, food, clothes and everything." So with Hoshmand's encouragement Nazparwar too began to get up and move about; but she was not used to work at all, and her heart was full of grief besides; nothing got done; and because of her lack of skill, whatever work she put her hand to she spoiled. Jabir's family thought her an utter fool and a shirker. It was only because Hoshmand joined her in every task that Nazparwar's failings stayed covered; otherwise heaven knows what would have happened. Hoshmand would wear her bones out[26] and bear all the misery single-handed, but she would not let Nazparwar suffer, and as far as she could she would not let her lay a hand to any work. In Jabir the Bedouin's house the whole truth about herself became clear to Nazparwar. When she compared her state with Hoshmand's she felt small[27] in her own eyes. Now she knew that those she used to look down on were the really useful people, and that she herself was the useless, worthless one, dependent on others, looking to others' hands[28]. Now she understood what freedom is, and what a great hardship it is to live as someone else's slave girl. Now she came to value Hoshmand, and saw that her longing[29] for freedom had not been wrong. Even so it was a mercy that in Jabir's house the two of them were not as despised as the slave girls in her own house had been. Here Nazparwar and Hoshmand lived just as Zamiran and Raihana, Jabir's two daughters, lived: the same food, the same clothes, all the work equal. It was not that Jabir's wife and daughters lolled on their beds like the Begams of Delhi and Lucknow and would not stir even to drink water. And it was not only Jabir; such is the custom of that country: however great a nobleman may be, he does not think work a disgrace. Jabir was a robber, but he was well off: he had a hundred pack[30] camels; there must have been about a thousand sheep and goats; that was his wealth and riches; and whatever plunder came his way once in a year or two was extra. For all that, his life and his family's was very simple and without pretension. Every one of them was content, hospitable[31], generous, brave, hard-working, enduring[32], true to their promise and firm in their word. Although for a long time these things seemed strange to Nazparwar, since there was a reflection[33] of goodness in them all, little by little Nazparwar came to like them, and would sometimes say to Hoshmand, "These are wild, desert savages, but many things about them are better than in city people."

Hoshmand: "There is one thing about this country I like very much too; it is that women are valued more in these parts."

Nazparwar: "What do you think is the reason?"

Hoshmand: "One is that women marry by their own choice. Look now, offers for Zamiran come from here and there, and Zamiran talks about them without hesitation. In our Hindustan, in the first place, they marry girls off at such a young age that they have no understanding of such things; and even if a girl grows older, she cannot say anything about her own marriage; they have made that out to be shameless. Second, there is another great reason for women being valued more, and that is that in the matter of marriage women have the same freedom as men. Men here marry several times, and so do women; divorce is no disgrace here; a second marriage is not forbidden to women here. You know about 'Azra[34]: this Jabir is her seventh husband; and yet see how all the women of the village respect 'Azra. In this country the tie of marriage is not as strong a tie as in our country. The dowers are small; if the man is unhappy, he divorces at once; if the woman is displeased, she quickly obtains a *khul'*[35]. And then it isn't that, if she is a divorced woman, anyone finds fault with her; no, thousands want her, hundreds seek her. In our Hindustan the men have kept their own freedom; whoever can afford it takes two, three or four wives; but the women are bound, and cannot marry a second time in any circumstances. That is why the woman is so much kept down compared with the man."

Meanwhile Zamiran's marriage too was settled. Mughira was a chief of these Bedouin; the match was agreed with his son Sabit. In Jabir's house there was great rejoicing, but Hoshmand's and Nazparwar's griefs were renewed; for Jabir had brought Hoshmand and Nazparwar with the very intention of giving them in his daughters' dowries; so now the time had come for Hoshmand and Nazparwar to be parted from each other. Jabir gave Zamiran the choice to take whichever of Hoshmand and Nazparwar she liked. Zamiran took Hoshmand. Zamiran was so good-natured that if Hoshmand had asked, she would have taken Nazparwar instead; but although parting from Nazparwar was very hard, Hoshmand thought it best to go with Zamiran herself; for in all the time she had lived at Jabir's she had never for a moment forgotten the thought of freedom, but no way[36] had appeared; and though there was no ground for hope, Hoshmand's heart told her of its own accord that in Mughira's house some way of release would surely appear; and Hoshmand described this hope to Nazparwar with such confidence[37] that she too was comforted. When Zamiran was married, that too was simple and without fuss: it was a marriage according to the Law, and the hospitality and the dowry were so modest that if Jabir, with such means, had married off his daughter like that in Delhi or Lucknow, the world would have cried shame[38].

So Zamiran took leave of her mother and father and came to Mughira's house; Hoshmand was with her. A little while later it happened that Hoshmand was serving Sabit and Zamiran their food; her eye fell on Sabit's hand, and she saw him wearing a ring exactly like the one the Hakim Sahib used to wear. She looked at it closely for a long time; it was the same band, the same stones. Once or twice, finding a chance while Sabit was asleep, Hoshmand looked at the ring again, and made quite sure that it was certainly the ring from the Hakim Sahib's hand. Now she set herself[39] to find out how this ring had come to Sabit. The Bedouin are great fighters, and over small matters they are ready for bloodshed. Zamiran had been at her in-laws' for three or four months when suddenly preparations for a fight began at Mughira's, and he decided to send the women to the house of the Shaikh of Basra. It was not the sort of thing whose reason Hoshmand would have had any trouble finding out; with a little inquiry she learned that Mughira was the chief of a great band of Bedouin, and wherever they plundered, they sent Mughira at home the *'ushr*, that is, a tenth share. Last year before the Hajj a caravan from Hind had been plundered on the road to Medina, and a man of Mughira's band named Shaddad had taken part in that plunder. In place of the tenth of the share he had got from the plunder, he had given Mughira a ring, the one on Sabit's hand. Now a few days ago news had reached Mughira that Shaddad had also seized the leader of the caravan and wanted to make him a slave. That man was old; he said, "I am weak and not fit for work or service; what will you gain by making me a slave?" Then Shaddad made this condition with him: "Give me a thousand dirhams and I'll let you go." The old man was also an Indian physician; so he came to Mecca, earned something by his profession and took something from his fellow-countrymen, and gave Shaddad a thousand dirhams. Mughira sent to Shaddad demanding the tenth of that thousand dirhams; Shaddad refused; over this the dispute grew till it came to fighting. At first Shaddad denied the thousand dirhams; Mughira had sure news that the Indian physician was still in Mecca; he had it inquired into through his friend the Sharif of Mecca, and the payment of the thousand dirhams was true. Mughira pressed hard for the tenth. Now Hoshmand had got exact news of the Hakim Sahib; she was overjoyed, and said to herself: "Oh, if I had wings I would fly this minute and tell Nazparwar the good news!" As soon as she heard the truth of it, Hoshmand began to make plans: if the Hakim Sahib is in Mecca, people go there every year from every side for the Hajj; sending word will not be hard. The fight that was to take place between Mughira and Shaddad was also put off because the days of the Hajj were drawing near.

When Hoshmand inquired, she found that a guide named Mutawakkil, who lived in Mughira's village, went to Mecca every year to teach Indians the rites[40] of the Hajj. This man was a kind of shrine-keeper[41]; he would meet the Indians as they came off the ships at Yalamlam[42] and see them through the Hajj in ten days or so; whatever they gave him in return[43] for this service was Mutawakkil's living. Mutawakkil was a very good-hearted and God-fearing man, and the Bedouin had great faith in his piety[44] and goodness, especially Mughira. Whatever Hoshmand got at Mughira's house, she would stint herself and take it to Mutawakkil's house. Little by little, when Hoshmand had got well acquainted[45] with Mutawakkil and came to trust his religion and honesty, she said to him, "I have a favour to ask of you: when you go to Mecca, find out through the Sharif of Mecca where an Indian physician, Masih-ul-Mulk, is, and tell him only this much: that Nazparwar, who is with Jabir the Bedouin at Bir-ul-A'rab[46], sends him her greetings." Mutawakkil promised very firmly: "God Almighty willing, I will certainly, certainly deliver this message of yours to Masih-ul-Mulk." So as soon as Mutawakkil arrived he looked for Masih-ul-Mulk and quickly found him, for Masih-ul-Mulk was himself physician to the Sharif of Mecca. The moment Masih-ul-Mulk heard Nazparwar's name, tears came from his eyes in spite of himself. Being a God-fearing man, Mutawakkil, seeing Masih-ul-Mulk weep, asked: "If I can help you in your trouble in any way, God willing, I will not hold back." Then Masih-ul-Mulk told the story of his being plundered and held captive, and said, "Nazparwar is the daughter of none other than wretched me; just tell me what is the best way to free her." Mutawakkil said, "All the Bedouin[47], though headstrong, respect the Sharif of Mecca; if the Sharif will exert himself[48], your daughter's release is very easy." Masih-ul-Mulk was overjoyed to hear this, and went at once and laid his case before the Sharif of Mecca. The Sharif of Mecca wrote a letter[49] there and then, and sent his own personal servant with Masih-ul-Mulk. Masih-ul-Mulk took the Sharif's servant with him to Bir-ul-A'rab, and gave Jabir the Sharif's letter. As soon as he had read the letter, Jabir wanted to take Masih-ul-Mulk into his house with great courtesy; Masih-ul-Mulk hesitated.

Jabir: "It would not be at all just that your daughter should have been one of my family for a year, and I the guardian[51] of her honour[50], and yet that I should treat you as a stranger[52]."

So Jabir took Masih-ul-Mulk inside the house. The moment she saw her father, Nazparwar ran and clung to his feet; and when they both remembered all that had happened while they were apart, father and daughter wept so loudly that the hearts of Jabir's whole household were shaken. A verse:

So weeping, weeping, the two of them met,
As Sawan meets Bhadon[53], when the rains are set.

As soon as she had calmed down, Nazparwar asked after her mother. Masih-ul-Mulk: "In separation[54] from you she is buried alive[55]." Then each told of their own sufferings. When he heard Nazparwar's greeting and whereabouts from Mutawakkil, Masih-ul-Mulk had been so overcome with joy that it nearly killed him[56]; at the time he had asked Mutawakkil nothing else; so up to now Masih-ul-Mulk knew nothing of Hoshmand; indeed, when he did not find Hoshmand with Nazparwar, he thought she must be somewhere else. Nazparwar asked Masih-ul-Mulk, "How did you find out where I was?"

Masih-ul-Mulk: "A guide named Mutawakkil gave me your greeting and told me where you were."

Nazparwar: "I don't even know the name of Mutawakkil. Perhaps God Almighty, pitying my misery, sent one of the Hidden Men[57] to you; or Hoshmand was here, and she may have said something to someone, but I don't know."

Masih-ul-Mulk: "Was Hoshmand with you too?"

Nazparwar: "From the beginning; this is now the fifth month since she was given in the dowry of Jabir's daughter Zamiran and went away with her."

Masih-ul-Mulk: "Where was Zamiran married?"

Nazparwar: "There's a place called 'Imrana six or seven stages from here; to Sabit, Mughira's son, there."

Masih-ul-Mulk: "This business of Mutawakkil is very strange."

Nazparwar: "It really is strange. Ask Jabir; perhaps there is someone of that name in Bir-ul-A'rab." Masih-ul-Mulk asked Jabir, and he said: "Not here; there is a guide at 'Imrana." Then Masih-ul-Mulk and Nazparwar were sure that Hoshmand had set her release in motion. Then Nazparwar told Masih-ul-Mulk of all Hoshmand's faithfulness, her kindnesses and her care. Masih-ul-Mulk said to himself that honour and decency would never allow him to take Nazparwar away and make no effort for Hoshmand's release. Thinking this, he resolved to go to 'Imrana, and began asking Jabir about the stages. Jabir said, "By this evening a messenger is due from 'Imrana; we shall learn the exact state of things from him." An hour after nightfall the messenger came, and Hoshmand was with him. The moment she saw Masih-ul-Mulk she laid her head at his feet. When Masih-ul-Mulk asked, she told her story: "When Mutawakkil came back from the Hajj I asked him about my message; I learned that you had been found, that a way had been made for the release of the young lady (Nazparwar), and that you had set out for Bir-ul-A'rab with a letter from the Sharif of Mecca. Mutawakkil asked me your story; I told it from beginning to end. Then he asked me, 'Have you given no thought to your own release?' I answered, 'I have no need of release; I am a slave born; may God grant it to those who need it.' Heaven knows what came into Mutawakkil's mind, or what he said to Mughira; anyway, they set me free. I said, 'I cannot take this kindness on myself until I see my young lady free.' A messenger was due to come here, and they sent me with him." So God brought about the release of both Nazparwar and Hoshmand, and Masih-ul-Mulk, taking both of them with him happily, took his leave of Jabir. Masih-ul-Mulk made Hoshmand his daughter, and Nazparwar made her her sister.

When the story was finished all the girls praised it: "Glory be! What a fine, delightful story; a thousand cheers for Hoshmand's good sense and faithfulness." Husn Ara: "People go to Arabia to make the Hajj; that suggests there is a great deal of religion there; then why did the Bedouin plunder those poor people for nothing, and how could they seize other people's wives and daughters and make them slave girls?"

The teacher: "Kulsum, you have read a good deal of the geography and history of Arabia; tell Husn Ara Begam something about it."
