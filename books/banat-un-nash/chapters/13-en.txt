# A Proof That the Earth Is Round

Husn Ara: "Well then, have these things proved that the earth is round?"

Mahmuda: "Not yet; be patient a little. Tell me one more thing: when you went to the Qutb Sahib, from how far away did the column begin to be visible?"

Husn Ara: "Why, go a little beyond Humayun's tomb and the column comes into view. And if it weren't for trees and houses in the way, the column, God is great, is so high that it would be no wonder if its top could be seen even from here."

Mahmuda: "Only the top?" Husn Ara: "And now you want me to see the whole column sitting at home?" Mahmuda: "What is the reason for not seeing it?"

Husn Ara: "The reason is just the distance, what else?" Mahmuda: "Because of the distance the column may look ever so small, but it should all be visible. What is the reason that at first only the top is seen? Where does the lower part of it vanish to?"

Husn Ara: "Something must be in the way, a tree or something."

Mahmuda: "But if a tree were in the way, the tree would be seen. I too have been to the Qutb Sahib no fewer than six or seven times. Beyond Humayun's tomb there is a plain as flat as the palm of your hand, and the road runs dead straight right to the foot of the column. And never mind the column; on a road you can see many distant trees plainly in front of you with nothing at all in between; but still at first you see the upper branches, and as you get nearer, little by little your sight reaches down, until the whole tree is seen entire[1] from top to root."

Hearing this objection of Mahmuda's, Husn Ara was at a loss[2]. Mahmuda: "Shall I tell you the reason?" Husn Ara: "Please do."

Mahmuda: "It is just the roundness of the earth getting in the way." With that Mahmuda took Husn Ara to the water jar and showed her clearly how the roundness gets in the way, and how people go right round the earth and come back.

Husn Ara: "Is that the only proof that the earth is round?"

Mahmuda: "No, there are many other proofs, but at present they would be hard for you to understand; when your knowledge has grown, I will certainly explain all the proofs that the earth is round."

Husn Ara: "Very well; if the earth is round, why don't we slip off it?"

Mahmuda: "It is certainly round, but you must understand this too: the smaller a round thing is, the greater its curve. For example, a mustard seed, a gram, a jujube, a peach, an egg, a cup, a pitcher, a water jar, a dome are all round, but in a small thing the roundness shows at once. Take a piece of peel from a jujube no bigger than a fingernail and it will be curved; but break off a potsherd a span across from a big water jar and it will seem flat. Try to balance a good round jujube on an egg, and use all the cunning you like, the moment you take your hand away it falls; but on the water jar you can set ten or fifteen jujubes wherever you like. If that is so with a water jar, it is more so with a dome; and the earth, beside these domes and jars, is heaven knows how many hundred thousand crores of times bigger. And when the pull of the earth . . . . is holding us, where could we fall to? It is not easy to give an idea of the size of the earth, but think of it like this: you see this courtyard of our house; how long and wide it is?" [TN: Some words are missing in the printed text where the dots stand.]

Husn Ara: "Is it a courtyard or the Devil's gut? Walk from one end of the wretched thing to the other and your legs give out. Why would they leave so much open ground? It isn't a courtyard, it's a wilderness."

Mahmuda: "A pavilion is to be built in the middle; that is why the space is left. Well, anyway, how far would it be from this hall to the entrance?"

Husn Ara: "I don't know." Mahmuda: "Guess." Husn Ara: "About twenty and twenty; oh dear, heaven knows how many score yards it is."

Mahmuda: "It is fully fifty yards." Husn Ara: "How many is fifty?"

Mahmuda: "Twenty and twenty and ten." Husn Ara: "Oh my, it's a very long courtyard!"

Mahmuda: "Well, how many times can you walk from this end of the courtyard to that?"

Husn Ara: "How many times! Why, even once would be a lot."

Mahmuda: "Is that all the strength you have?"

Husn Ara: "Yes, that's all the strength my legs have. God forbid, I'm not a palanquin-bearer's wife; I am a real rich man's daughter . . . . . . . Rich men's daughters walk only that much on their own feet. On days when the teacher is sitting in front, I get down from my nurse's lap near the platform out of respect, but by the time I reach the hall I'm quite out of breath; and when the teacher isn't in front, or is teaching someone with her eyes lowered, I have my nurse bring me right into the middle of the hall and set me down in my place." [TN: The Urdu reads "on days when the teacher is not sitting in front", which contradicts the rest of the sentence; the sense has been followed. Some words are also missing where the dots stand.]

Mahmuda: "If being a cripple is an accomplishment of the rich, then bravo, you are doing very well. But I, God willing, could do a hundred rounds at one go, and neither get out of breath nor have my legs ache."

Husn Ara: "With your mouth or with your legs?" Mahmuda: "Why, with these very legs; and if you don't believe me, come, I'll have the teacher tell you."

# Bodily Exercise[3], and Its Benefits Shown in a Story of the Days of the Mutiny

Mahmuda: "It is the teacher's rule all the year round to get up when about four *gharis* of night are left, and say the *tahajjud* prayer; by then it is about two *gharis* before dawn. From that time she keeps walking up and down this courtyard reciting her portion of the Qur'an, until the half-light[4] comes. Then she says her prayer, and sometimes she has finished her usual litany and sometimes she is still saying it when I wake up. Last hot weather one night I happened to wake; I saw the teacher walking up and down. When she knew I was awake she said, 'Mahmuda, it is morning now, don't go back to sleep, you'll feel unwell. Come and see what delight[5] there is in the moonlight at the end of the night! The stars are twinkling as if they had been awake all night and are now dozing as morning comes. What a cool breeze is blowing; it makes the heart glad! The flowers that have opened give off a faint sweet scent. The birds are singing the praise[6] of God in sweet voices. It is the hour of the dawning of light and the time of blessing. Raise your eyes to the east and see how the light of morning charms the heart.' At once I got up, washed my hands and face and began walking up and down with the teacher. That day I paid close attention and counted, and I did eighty, or let us say four score, rounds of the courtyard. I asked the teacher, 'Why do you get up so early and walk up and down?' And she said, 'There is no more refreshing time in the whole day and night; and my real purpose in walking is that a person needs a little bodily labour and physical exercise to keep in health[7]. You see that, by God's grace, I seldom fall ill; the outward means of it, as I understand it, is just that every morning I walk enough to bring out a good sweat[8] on my body. And once I saw a special benefit of this habit too. In the Mutiny, when the whole city fled[9], we stayed behind in the hope that the nobleman in whose service Father was had always been a great well-wisher of the Government; his own sons and grandsons were fighting with the Government's army. Through the nobleman Father had obtained a promise from the Government that when Delhi was taken, no man of the Government's army would harm us. When the uproar[10] began in the city, the people of the quarter kept telling us, "Why stay in the city and lose your lives for nothing?" But we did not leave the house, relying on that promise. For fear, we did not tell people about it, but in our hearts we were praying for the day when Delhi would be taken and we could sit in peace. As God willed, on the day Delhi was stormed, oh, may God not show that day even to an enemy, it was the Day of Judgement. All day long it rained bullets, and as for the cannon-balls, God help us, our ears were deafened and the ground shook[11]. By evening the English had come up to the far side of the canal[12], and right below this wall of ours, at the corner of the lane, the wretched sepoys had set up a gun. Who expected to live till morning? We had given up hope of life and were hiding in the cellars, calling on God. Who ate and who cooked? Everyone just looked at everyone else. About a watch after nightfall we heard a man's voice calling Father by name. Who would answer, for fear? At last I said to my brother, "For God's sake go out in the courtyard and find out; this man has been shouting for so long; perhaps he is someone from the Government's army come to protect us." So my brother went out, climbed onto the roof and listened for the voice. At that time the fighting had stopped too. The man was in the street; my brother called him under the roof and asked what it was. He said, "The Captain Sahib has sent me, and he says: 'We have tried every way to protect your house, but nothing can be done. The rebels have not left the city; they are on that side of the canal, and our control reaches up to the canal. At two o'clock tonight we shall attack the rebels; your house is right in the line of fire; before the attack, get out and save your lives; when the government is settled, we shall see.'" At this news everyone was struck dumb. One said, "Stay where you are; we'll die this way or that way anyway; what is the use of dragging the women through the bazaars for nothing?" One or two fools even began to say, "Come, let us make an end of the women ourselves, and then whatever happens, happens."'

'In short, as many mouths, so many opinions. In this talk half the night went by. My brother saw that the time was slipping away and the fighting would begin before long; then he stiffened and said, "All this is just talk and sheer cowardly thinking. It is a duty to save one's life; we should flee as far as we can; and as for fate, there is no remedy for it anyway." With that he said to me, "Get up, girl, you come along at least; let these people do as they please." I had been saying "Flee, flee" from the start; the moment I heard "go", I took off all my jewellery with my own hands and threw it into the courtyard, tore two widths from the hall's floor-cloth to make a scarf, and said, "There, I'm off." The moment I set out, the whole family followed like sheep. If you had been there that night you would have seen the fun of those wretched women: one lady had left all her money and wealth at home and was going along loaded with her betel box; one woman's shoes kept slipping off her feet; another's drawstring kept tangling round her feet. That day whoever had the widest trouser-legs had the hardest time walking. Even then my brother would tease them: "Wretched women, make two pairs of trousers out of a bolt of *nainsukh* muslin, and make them still bigger by sewing more strips onto your Lahore silk drawstrings." It was half laughter, half misery. When had those poor things ever had to walk through the bazaars? Though it was night, and in any case they could not walk the road, their feet felt like a maund each[13]; they would take two steps and fall. At long last, as morning came, we reached the Kaghzi Mahalla[14]. What refuge was there? The English said that if they were to take the Fort, it would be today. As soon as we came level with the water-mills, we saw hundreds and thousands of white soldiers and Sikhs coming along in ranks. The moment we saw them our breath left us; we turned and fled towards the Kauriya Pul. The poor women were in a terrible state. One lady lay down on the road: "I can't go any further; for God's sake leave me here." I carried her pick-a-back a little way; meanwhile two or three more fell down; now whom was one to carry on one's shoulders? One's own life was burden enough. My brother said, "Good people, for God's sake take heart and go just as far as the Phul Mandi; if there's somewhere to stop there, some transport will be arranged." With a thousand difficulties we reached the Phul Mandi about a quarter of the day after sunrise. What transport was there to be had? Grain had come in from outside on donkeys, and the donkey-man was going out with his donkeys. My brother asked him, "Where are your donkeys from?" He said, "From Sultan-ji." My brother begged him very humbly: "Brother, just let these women ride as far as the city gate, and we'll pay whatever you say."'

'The donkey-man: "Oh, sir, what hire! You can see the English have reached the Fort; worse luck, I couldn't get away in the night; now let's see how I'll escape. Take the donkeys, load them as much as you like and drive them along; hand the donkeys over to me outside the gate." So my brother kept four donkeys and said, "There, ladies, whoever is tired, get on one; and it would be disastrous to delay." At first, hearing of riding donkeys, they all hesitated, but what could they do? They had to get on the donkeys. My brother said to me too, "Girl, you're very tired, [illegible: the last line of the page is cut off in the scan; it ends with Asghari answering "I have"] not tired at all, and I could walk ten times as far again on foot." My brother: "You'll have to get on in the end; do you think we shall stop once we are in the city? Not at all; there is no shelter this side of the Arab Sarai." I said, "God willing, I'll walk to the Sarai quite well." So God saved me from that disgrace, and the ladies did ride; they are laughed at for it to this day. And I, by God's grace, thanks to my walking, kept on walking; I was neither tired nor worn out.'"

Husn Ara: "Why, the Mutiny was a sudden calamity, and it's over; God forbid, is there a Mutiny every day, that wretched women should get into the habit of running and practise fleeing for it?"

Mahmuda: "I told it as one thing led to another; I don't mean either that women should hold horse races in their houses. But it is not right to be so lazy that they pant when they go as far as the entrance, and can't get their breath when they climb onto the roof. One should be brisk in getting up and sitting down and walking about."

Husn Ara: "Well, now prove that the earth is round; do you want to put it off?"

Mahmuda: "Yes. This courtyard is fifty yards long; walk thirty-five rounds, that is two score less five, from this end to that, and it makes a mile; and two miles make a *kos*."

Husn Ara: "Oh my, is a mile so big, and a *kos* so big!" Mahmuda: "Now tell me, how many thousand *kos* high is the Qutb Sahib's column?"

Husn Ara: "Now I see that by this reckoning it won't even be a full mile."

Mahmuda: "Certainly; never mind a mile, it isn't even a tenth of a mile."
