# The Ninth Majlis

Atu-ji's account

They say that Sayyid Abbas was in his sixteenth year when he had to leave the school. What happened was this. Of Zubaida Khatun's uncle, Khwaja Kumail, who has been mentioned before, there had been no news for a very long time; at the age of thirty or thirty-five he had given up his post in Delhi and gone off somewhere. Now news came that his son, Khwaja Huzail by name, was living in Aurangabad in the Deccan. The news was not very certain, but as soon as Zubaida heard it she grew restless. People tried again and again to make her see that putting faith in news of this kind was far from wise, but when did the stirring of the blood listen to reason? At last it was settled that Sayyid Abbas should be sent to Aurangabad.

The whole family argued with her and sat back convinced that to send a boy of fifteen to the far ends of the earth (*kale koson*, 'black leagues away') was in no way proper; but Zubaida Khatun would not listen to a word, and said: "This has simply become an occasion for his travels; even if this had not come up, I would never have kept him in the city; I would certainly have sent him off in some direction or other, for a thing like true manhood (*admiyat*) can never be acquired within the four walls of a city. Of course I too am sorry that he must leave school, but when I look again, there is nothing to be sorry about; travel is a man's greatest teacher; it is as though I am taking him out of one school and putting him into another." So she said, and began to make ready for the journey. In a few days she got everything ready [1] and sent her son off; she sent two men with him, Ghulam Imam, the son of his wet-nurse (*anna*), and Islam Beg the musketeer. And at the time of leaving she told him that if Khwaja Huzail had gone somewhere from Aurangabad, he should look for him in other cities too, and wherever he found him, he should bring him back with him at once.

In short, Sayyid Abbas reached Aurangabad. The three of them put up at a *sarai* (inn) and began to search the city for Khwaja Huzail. After several days they learned that a man of that name had come there, but that twenty or twenty-two days earlier a caravan had left from there for the *hajj* (the pilgrimage to Mecca), and he had gone with it.

Sayyid Abbas at once resolved to make the hajj. Apart from cash and necessary clothes they kept one horse each and one gun each, sold off all the rest of their belongings there at auction, and set off straight for Bombay. When they reckoned it up at the time of setting out, there were still six or seven months left until the season of the hajj. Sayyid Abbas said: "Friends, if we have to go a hundred or two hundred *kos* out of our way, so be it; but the great cities that lie thirty or forty *kos* off the road, let us see them too as we go; who knows whether we shall ever have occasion to come this way again. If God wills, we shall join Mamun-jan in the season of the hajj [TN: *Mamun-jan*, 'dear uncle', is Khwaja Huzail, his mother's cousin]."

In short, he, Ghulam Imam and Islam Beg, all three, left the Bombay road and turned off to the left. They would travel all day, and in the evening, seeing some settlement, they would halt. For a month and a half or two they wandered like this, to right and left; when they had had their fill of wandering, they took the road to Bombay.

On this road a strange thing befell them. For two or three days a river had lain on their left hand; they had to cross it, but they found no chance to get across. One day, as they went along, when a *pahar* (a watch of three hours) of the day had passed, the ground along the bank began to grow somewhat soft. From above it looked quite dry, but where the horses' hooves fell, wet mud came up; but they paid no attention, and went on in the same heedless way [2].

Suddenly Sayyid Abbas's horse got stuck in quicksand. Its legs sank a span each into the ground; but the horse was very powerful, and heaving itself up [3] it got out. Ahead the quicksand was deeper still; this time it sank to the knees. Islam Beg and Ghulam Imam, seeing this, reined in their own horses right there. Sayyid Abbas's horse sprang up a second time, and shaking all four legs free landed eight or ten cubits further on; but this time, once stuck, it could not get out again. They said to themselves that the horse's escape was out of the question, but somehow Sayyid Abbas must be got out. A little way off a village could be seen; the two of them reassured Sayyid Abbas, gave their horses the rein, and quickly reaching that village, took five or seven men and some ropes and poles and so on, and came back to the river.

Coming there, what did they see but that there was no Sayyid Abbas, nor any trace of the horse; only a bag of clothes and some other belongings lay scattered here and there. When they went and looked, it was Sayyid Abbas's own belongings, but of their owner there was no sign anywhere. The two of them began to look this way and that. The very people who had come with them said: "What are you looking for? Just give up hope of your companion. We have never yet seen anyone come up out of this quicksand. It seems that rider and horse have both stayed here. And we told you at the time that by the time we got there, there would be no trace of horse or rider."

No sooner had they said this than the two lost all heart, and their wits left them completely. Islam Beg still kept some hold on himself, but Ghulam Imam was after all only a boy; he burst into helpless tears, and at last, weeping, fainted. In this state Islam Beg took him to the village. When they got there he came to himself somewhat; but what senses, what wits? For a little while he would stay silent, then when he remembered Sayyid Abbas he would lose all control of himself. Islam Beg, for his part, was worried how they could go and show their faces to the Begum Sahib now.

In short, how much shall I tell? The next day the two of them gave away all their belongings to the villagers and set off from there with nothing but themselves [4], and resolved in their hearts to give up homeland and household and spend their whole lives as *faqirs* (wandering mendicants). For a year they wandered here and there like this, aimlessly. At last a caravan was going to the hajj, and they joined it. For three months they were with it. In the fourth month, when they had completed the hajj and the *ziyarat* (the visit to the Prophet's tomb at Medina), it came into their minds to go to Rum (the Ottoman lands) and spend a few days there. At that time they had not a cowrie for expenses, but trusting in God they set out just as they were. On the way they endured great hardships. At last, somehow or other, they reached Istanbul. They stayed there three or four months. Although they had no acquaintance there, they became so attached to that city that they could not bring themselves to leave that land.

One day the two of them were walking unawares in the bazaar, when what did they see but a young man on an Arab horse, coming towards them at a trot. When he came near, as soon as he saw them he leapt from the horse, and giving the horse to the groom, came and threw his arms around them both. When they looked, his face and features were all just like Sayyid Abbas's, but they thought to themselves, where are we and where is Sayyid Abbas? At last, when they asked and he gave his name as Sayyid Abbas, such was their state that both of them wept until they were choked with sobs. At last [5] Sayyid Abbas comforted and consoled them and took them to his house. When they went and saw the house, it was a nobleman's establishment [6] in every way. In the stable four horses were tied as well; two or three carriages and buggies stood there too; servants, attendants, cooks, stewards, all were busy at their work. Further in, the living quarters too were most elegant; table, chair, couch, canopied bed, chandelier, glass shades, everything was there. Now they were bewildered: O God! is this a dream or waking?

In short, as soon as they got there, they first asked: "How did you get out of the quicksand?" He said: "You galloped off over there towards the village, and here my horse began to sink into the quicksand. When the horse had sunk up to its chest, I said to myself: Give up the horse now [7], or else in a moment it will drag you down with it.

With that I got clear of its back right there; but I did not set my foot on the quicksand, or I too would have stayed there. What I did was this: whatever gear was fastened at my waist I quickly unfastened and threw onto the dry ground, took my gun in one hand, slid off the horse's hindquarters and fell flat on my back in the quicksand. In this way my weight did not stay in one place but was spread out over a distance. The firm ground, where you had stopped your horses, would have been twenty-five or thirty paces from there. Now I spread out my arms and from there rolled and rolled until I reached the dry ground; but in between, wherever I stopped even for a moment, I began to sink; but God gave me such quickness at that time that, rolling and tumbling, I did get out. My clothes were all smeared with mud. I said to myself: Until you come back from the village, let me go to the river and wash my clothes and bathe too. I left the gear right there and, with the gun in my hand, came to the river. When I came and looked at the river, it was running with such force and roar that one was afraid to go into it. The fact is that the ground there was rather low, so the water came with great force.

But this boyishness too spoils a man; I said in my heart: I know how to swim well enough; if the water pushes like this, it will carry me off a *kos* or half a *kos*, then I shall get out; if God wills, I am not going to drown. With this thought I jumped into the river clothes and all; but the rush of the water drove the senses out of me; for seven or eight *kos* it did not let me draw breath. At last, when the force of the water lessened somewhat and my wits came back a little, the far bank was very near; with great difficulty I reached it and gave thanks to the Almighty that He had saved me from two calamities.

But now I thought: how am I to meet you? I did not find strength enough in myself to go into the water again and cross the river, and no bridge or boat was to be seen anywhere. In this perplexity I sat there drying my clothes. When my clothes were dry, I went along the bank in search of a boat; but no boat was to be found anywhere. Helpless, when evening came, there was a sort of village a *kos* or half a *kos* from there; I went there and spent the night. In the morning I got up, came back to the river, and taking God's name jumped into the water. Here the water had no such force, and the river was not very broad either; in a short while I came across to this side. But between where I had left you and where I now was, there were now leagues of difference; for nine or ten *kos* the water itself had carried me off, and I must have gone five or seven *kos* looking for a boat.

In short, walking from morning, I reached that same place again at about five in the evening. First I looked for you to right and left; when I found no trace anywhere, I went and searched for my gear; of that too I found nothing. Then I reached the same bank of the river where I had first gone to wash my clothes; there my gun was lying just as I had left it. I picked it up and went to the same village where you had galloped your horses. From the people there I learned this much of you, that you had certainly spent one night there, and then there was no knowing where you had gone.

For five or seven days I looked for you in that neighbourhood, but when I found no trace [8] anywhere, I gave up hope and took the road to Bombay. The five or seven rupees that were left in my pocket, take it that that was all; apart from that, whatever money there was had all gone with that gear. There was only me and my gun, or the support of God; but by God's favour I did not have to beg of anyone or ask anyone for anything. Of those few coins I had left, I had two sets of clothes made, bought some bullets, shot, powder and caps, and got one or two flints; I tied up some salt; I bought a knife. On the way, wherever I felt hungry, I would shoot some creature or other, roast it as *kababs* and eat it. In the evening, if some village came along I would halt there, and if not, I did not care about that either; I would lie down in the jungle itself.

At last, somehow or other, I reached Bombay. There, what money did I have? There was only some powder and shot, and that too was finished. I sold my gun for fifty rupees and put up in a shop; I had two or four respectable sets of clothes made, took a servant into service, and having put my appearance in order began to meet the notable people there. There is a Yemeni merchant there named Shaikh Faruq, very, very rich, and his good manners and learning were on everyone's lips there too. In time I came to him too. He treated me with great kindness, and when I left he told me with great insistence: As long as you are here, be sure to keep coming to see us; and he asked me my address and all.

In the evening, what did I see but his man coming along with a tray of food set on a porter's head, until, asking his way, he arrived at my very door; and having had me eat in front of him, he said on leaving that as long as I stayed there I should not have food cooked for myself; food would come for me from there twice a day. When I saw such hospitality from him, I was obliged to go to his house every day.

The next day, when I happened to go there, he was sitting with some three emeralds in his hand, asking his steward which men here were masters at engraving seals. I said: 'What do you wish to have engraved?' He said: 'These three stones have come to me from Zabid [TN: a town in Yemen], and the person who sent them is named Hasan, and his younger brothers' names are Muhsin and Ahsan; these three names are to be engraved on them. And these days a very great calligrapher from Iran has gone there; they have had him write out a model of the three names and have sent it, and they want the names engraved exactly according to it, without a hair's difference.' I said: 'Then send for some seal-engraver and show him?' He sent that same steward and had a seal-engraver called. Looking at the model, he first refused, saying that he could not engrave such letters; then he began to say that if he were given an *ashrafi* (gold coin) a letter, he would take as much pains over it as he could. The Shaikh said: 'Will you take anything less than that?' He said: 'If you give even a rupee a letter less, I will not be responsible for the stones being spoiled; if they are spoiled, the loss is yours, and if they turn out well, the gain is yours.' In short, no bargain could be struck with him.

When he had gone, I asked the Shaikh's men whether there was any other seal-engraver there. They said: 'Sir! there is no one better than him in this city at all; but a Kashmiri came here a few days ago, and we hear great things of him; if you say so, shall we fetch him?' I said: 'The seal-engravers of Kashmir are famous all over the world; no doubt he will be a master; bring him.' The man went to fetch him, and I took a knife and scraped off the dots of the *nun* (the letter *n*) in the three names of that model, and, cutting a pen to match those very letters, made Muhsin into 'Mujash', Hasan into 'Jash' and Ahsan into 'Ajash' [TN: the altered spellings are only partly legible in the scan; the trick is that, with the dot of *n* gone, *s* and *n* run together and look like a single *sh*, so each name loses a letter, while new dots are added]. When the Shaikh saw it, he cried out in alarm: 'Ah! What a disaster you have made!' I said: 'Your letters I have not spoiled at all; the dots, it is true, I have changed; but I am your guest, so forgive me this fault.' He laughed and asked me: 'Tell me truly, what is this about?' I said: 'Just let the seal-engraver come; you will see, the whole thing will become clear to you at once.' He picked up the paper and looked at it carefully for a long time, but could make nothing of it.

Meanwhile the seal-engraver came too; he was shown the three stones and the model. He was in truth a very great master; as soon as he saw the model, he said that these letters seemed to have been written by some great, accomplished master, but the dots did not seem to be from his hand. In short, when he was asked, he too named the same price, an ashrafi a letter. I said: 'If a letter is an ashrafi, then a dot should be at least a rupee?' He said: 'Is there any doubt about that? Placing dots to match these letters is no laughing matter.' I said: 'As many dots as we take away from it, will you deduct that many rupees too?' He thought perhaps I was joking, and said: 'With all my heart' (*ankhon se*, 'on my eyes'). I said: 'Very well. Now you count the letters of these three words, take away the three stones and these models, and do not put the dots in just yet. In these three words there are eighteen dots; as many of them as are taken away, that many rupees will be cut from your fee.' He said: 'Very well.' And he took the three stones and the models and went off.

When he had gone, I said to the Shaikh: 'There you are, sir! By scraping off three of your dots I have saved you four ashrafis.' He said: 'How?' I said: 'By removing the dots of the *nun*, one letter has gone from each of the three names; that alone saves three ashrafis. Now when he brings the seals engraved, he will be told: Of these eighteen dots, put one dot each in the bowls of the *shin*, and for the fifteen dots that remain, deduct fifteen rupees. Before, you would have had to pay eleven ashrafis; now you will have to pay only seven.'

The Shaikh, by God's grace, is as large-hearted as he is rich; so what would he care for savings like this? But this witticism pleased him greatly. Besides this, however, God Most High caused another great gain to come to him through my words, and at that he was indeed delighted.

The fact is that ships of cloth often come to him from England; if he gets the profit he wants, he sells them off just as they are, fully laden, to the Bombay people, and if not, he sends them straight on to Calcutta, where they sell well. In this way there was news of a ship coming; its *bijak* (invoice) had reached him, but the ship had not yet arrived when there was a crowd of buyers. Many customers came to take it at a profit of one anna in the rupee, but he said that he would never let it go for less than two annas in the rupee. I said: 'What a mistake you are making! Here even a small profit should be counted a great deal; it is a matter of the sea, who knows what mishap may befall? And in my opinion you should always sell here for whatever it will fetch, as far as you can.' Although it was not to his liking, he had come to regard me very highly; for my sake he settled the bargain then and there at one anna in the rupee. There was fifty thousand rupees' worth of cloth in it, and the profit came to something over three thousand rupees. He secured the whole payment and handed the invoice of the cloth over to the buyer.

As God would have it, when the ship had sailed from Aden and four days had passed since it sailed, suddenly news came that the ship had sunk; the men all got away in boats, but all the goods, whatever there were, went down with the ship. Hearing this news, the Shaikh was very sorry for the buyer's plight, but he gave great thanks that God Most High had not let any harm come to his own goods. When the news came, I too was there at his house. He said to me: 'Sir! this is all owing to you' (literally, 'the blessing of your shoes'). Hearing this I melted with shame, and I said: 'Quite true, you are right; it was revealed to me in a vision that this ship would go down after leaving Aden! Good heavens, sir, fear God! When God Most High willed that your goods should not perish, if I had not said it, the thought would have come into your heart of itself. And such words should never pass your lips; whatever profit or loss reaches a man is all from God; to attribute His work to a mortal is a great error.' After this I had a great place in his heart.

In the same way, a question had come from England: on what things does the civilisation of India depend, and does it depend mostly on the attention of the Government or on the efforts of the Indians? He had had me write the answer and sent it. By God's grace that answer was written at such a lucky hour that not only did he like it very much, but from England too praise of it came back printed in a newspaper.

In short, to cut the story short, I happened to stay there four months. In the fifth month, when the season of the hajj came, I asked the Shaikh's leave. He said: 'Where do you intend to go after the hajj?' I said: 'I intend to go to Rum.' He gave me ten thousand rupees and saw me off. From there I came first to Mecca the Exalted; I stayed there a month; having completed the hajj I went to Medina the Radiant; I stayed there about a month and a quarter; and there I met Mamun-jan too. (Ghulam Imam asked: 'Where is he?' He said: 'He is here; he has gone to the bazaar just now; he will be back within the hour.')

From there Mamun-jan and I came to Istanbul. As soon as we got here, I heard that at the Sultan's court a post of interpreter had been vacant for several years, and its salary is two thousand rupees a month. I asked people what the reason was for its staying vacant so long. They said: 'The condition for it is that only a person who knows at least five languages can be appointed to the post; and the five languages are these: Turkish, Arabic, Persian, English, French.' As soon as I heard this, I began to learn Turkish; working day and night for six months without a break, I gained a good mastery of speaking it, and in writing too I can express what I mean quite well; and for four or five months I learned French, but this was not a language one could learn in four or five months.

Taking God's name, I put in a petition that in four languages I could be examined now, and that though I knew French somewhat, I could not be examined in it; that I wished to be examined in the languages I knew and have the post assigned to my name for the present, and be given six months' grace; after that I would be examined in French too. As soon as it reached the court my petition was granted, and after the examination I was appointed to the post. The French examination has not yet taken place, and for that reason I do not get the full salary either; but by God's favour I have gained a fair command of that too. But the six months have not yet passed; a month and some days remain; after that the examination will be held."

Ghulam Imam and Islam Beg were so happy to hear all this that they quite forgot the hardships of the journey and the pain of separation. Every moment they gave thanks to God and blessed His providence [TN: a word appears to be missing in the print here], and they said: "O Lord! If You had not brought us out with honour, we would not have been fit to go and show our faces to the people at home. You have given us a place in the world and heard the prayer of our aching hearts." After this Ghulam Imam said: "Well, sir! Did the Begum Sahib send you for this, to take Mamun-jan off to Istanbul, sit an examination there and take a post as an interpreter?" Sayyid Abbas smiled and said: "No doubt I have committed a very great fault, but I hope Amma-jan will surely forgive me. Now my intention is to write my whole story to Amma-jan, and send money for the journey and call her here. She has long intended to make the hajj anyway, and now this is a fine opportunity; but you two gentlemen will have to go there, and I am sure Mamun-jan will go with you too." They said: "What objection could we have? Whenever you say, we are ready to go at once."

In short, after two months Sayyid Abbas sent Islam Beg, Ghulam Imam and Khwaja Huzail off to India, and they brought Zubaida Khatun from Delhi and went to Arabia. First they made the hajj, then visited holy Medina, and from there took the road to Istanbul. When Zubaida Khatun had arrived there, a few days later Sayyid Abbas was married to the daughter of a very great nobleman; and they all made their home there.

So this story is ended. May God Most High grant every child such a mother, and every mother such a child. Amen.

The End
