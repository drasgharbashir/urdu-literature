# The Third Majlis

The rest of Zubaida Khatun's account

When I had grown a little older, Amma-jan began to instruct me in other and different ways. I remember well that one day, after supper, she sat down at seven o'clock and kept giving me the same advice until twelve: that, my child, knowledge is great wealth. Through knowledge one finds God; through knowledge comes salvation. Before knowledge, property and riches are worth nothing. A poor man who has knowledge is better than a king without it. One person's knowledge and a thousand people's worship cannot be weighed as equal. A person without knowledge is not a person but an animal; a house with no one learned in it is not a house but an animal pen; and a country where learning is not talked of is not a country but a jungle full of cattle. The honour of knowledge is far greater than the honour of wealth. A rich man's honour comes from his clothes, or from his cushion and bolster, or from his servants and attendants, or from his elephants and horses; once he is parted from them, there is the world and all God's creatures, and he is just one more of them. But a learned person keeps the same honour whatever state he is in, wherever he goes and whomever he meets.

Why look so far? The respect you show your *ustani-ji*, how much you defer to her and mind her, do you behave like that with anyone else? I said, "Amma-jan, but she is also my teacher, and everyone respects their teacher." She said, "My child, the Mughlani is your teacher too, isn't she? The cook has taught you cooking too, hasn't she? Why don't you respect them as much? Dear girl, all of *ustani-ji*'s honour comes from her learning. Very well, you are her pupil; but why do we show her so much regard? After all, she is only in our employ. But these four letters are such a thing that a person takes respect from the very one whose servant she is. Besides, if you want the truth, we people are quite blind next to those who can read and write. We do not recognise our own gain; we do not understand our own loss. We have no skill in talking and no sense in understanding what is said.

As for the way husband and wife do not get on in some houses, and the women sit together and blame it all on the men, if anyone asked me, should I tell them the answer? Oh, oh! What a bad age it is! There is not a trace of fairness anywhere. You fools! How on earth could men's hearts be in tune with yours? First, God Almighty put more sense and understanding into men's very nature than into you; second, reading and writing is their business. Even the most worthless of them [1] will have some skill or other; and if nothing else, he will have learned thousands of things by heart just from hearing educated people talk. After all, he is a man; he meets everyone, goes to everyone's house, sits and rises with everyone. He sits with a *maulvi* (religious scholar) and learns a few points of religion, and comes to know the good and bad of ten things. He meets a *hakim* (physician of the Greek school) and learns a few things about medicine. He goes to the court and hears new orders and new laws. He sits with his elders and learns the ups and downs of the world. In the end, little by little, he becomes a proper human being.

Tell me, where did you learn to be human? And how? Of household sense you have none at all; crookedness is part of your nature; and you have nothing to do with learning. In your view, just as going about outside, travelling abroad and earning a living are all men's duties, so reading and writing is their job too.

As for company, by God's grace here the whole kiln-load has come out spoiled. [TN: The proverb *ava ka ava bigarna*, "the whole firing of the kiln is ruined", means that everyone without exception is bad.] In your mother's family, your grandmothers, your mother, your sister, your brother's wife, your aunts on both sides; and in your in-laws' house, your mother-in-law, your sisters-in-law, the wives of your husband's younger and elder brothers, and his aunts on every side: in short, in the whole clan, from the youngest to the eldest, from the young to the old, there is not one beside whom you could sit and learn anything of being human.

Whoever you look at knows nothing except to put her head together with three others and start running down the world. One whines about her mother-in-law and sister-in-law; another weeps over her troubles with her husband's brothers' wives; another spits venom at her daughter-in-law; another brings down on herself the curse of a long-suffering husband; another mocks someone's wedding; another laughs at someone's dowry; another finds a defect in someone's family; and if anyone contradicts anyone in all this, they start fighting among themselves.

Besides that, their tempers are such that the hot-tempered ones quarrel with the very wind that blows. They speak as if they were throwing a stone. With their husbands they pick a sour quarrel [2] at every word; they curse the children for no reason; they wrangle with the servants rightly or wrongly. And those who have a little mildness in their nature are such that they cannot stir themselves even to drink water. They eat whatever the cook makes and wear whatever the Mughlani sews. If too much salt goes into the food, who cares; if the garment hangs badly, it is beneath their notice. The water jars lie uncovered and they know nothing of it; the pots have lost their tinning and they don't mind. Sitting with women like these, far from becoming human, you may lose what little humanity you have. Yes, one thing you will certainly learn there: a new religion, unlike any in the whole world, strange to all of God's creation, not found in the Qur'an nor mentioned in the Hadith."

I said, "Amma-jan, is the women's religion so completely different from everyone's?" She said, "My child, haven't you heard their rules? True, where would you have heard them? If your mother too had taught you these things from the first day, and insisted on them instead of prayer and fasting, you would know what manias have lodged in women's hearts." I said, "Dear Amma-jan, what are these rules? Do tell me too." She said, "My child, if there were only one I would tell it; they have strung together thousands of absurdities.

Don't snap the scissors; don't clack the tongs [3]; don't cut your nails with a knife; don't drink water with one hand resting on the other palm; all these are signs of ill luck. If you drink water bareheaded, put your hand on your head. Don't stand with your hand on the door frame, and if you do by mistake, kiss both hands. Don't pass fire between two people, or they will quarrel. Don't put a sieve on your head, or you will go bald. Don't weigh things standing up, or the blessing will go out of the house. Don't stretch after eating, or everything you ate and drank will go into a dog's belly. Don't let a broom touch your body, or you will grow as thin as a broom-straw. Don't let a ladle touch your body, or you will get a craving for food. Don't drink water that has been kicked or stepped over. Don't lift a bed a child is sleeping on by its side-rails, and if you do, rub your hands together from both sides and kiss them. Water touched by three hands must be thrown away, or touched by a fourth. Don't swing your feet when you sit, or your livelihood will shake. Don't blow out a lamp, or your breath will start to smell. When you feed children milk, curds or rice, make them lick a little ash, or the evil eye will strike. Sewing a garment while it is on your body brings poverty. Keeping a porcupine quill in the house causes quarrels. If the veil-end of a woman whose children have died [4] falls on someone, that person's child will fall ill, unless the corner of the veil is snipped off and burnt. When you go to someone as a guest, don't come home on the third day. Don't visit anyone on a Wednesday. When you sew a bride's clothes, have seven married women touch them.

If a crow caws in the morning, know that some relative is coming from abroad; start saying the names of everyone who is away, and the one at whose name it flies off is the one who will come. On rising in the morning, if you speak of the *hakim*, call him 'the turbaned one', and if you speak of the washerwoman, call her 'the bright one'. Call the moon 'the one above', a snake 'a rope', cholera 'the spitting' [TN: so printed], and *masan* [5] 'the nameless'. If dogs bark at night, know that they can see Satan. If the griddle 'laughs' when it is taken off the stove, know that some happiness is coming. If someone sneezes as you get up, sit down again. Don't stand a cot on end upside down; it is a sign of ill luck. When you cut out [6] cloth, touch it to the ground. Don't look at your face in water. When you take medicine, turn the cup upside down. Don't put one foot on top of the other; it brings ill luck. If one shoe climbs on top of the other, know that you are to travel. Don't hand food across over someone, or it will become a *sadqa* (alms offered to ward off harm). Don't step over anyone, or their head will ache. If your nose itches, take the shoe of a man who has come in from outside and touch it seven times, or you will fall ill. Don't tap on a water pot; it brings ill luck. Don't seat a man who has married a second time at the *sahnak* of Bibi. [TN: The *sahnak* is a dish of food offered in the name of Bibi Fatima, from which by custom only women of unblemished standing may eat.]

The third, thirteenth and twenty-third, and the eighth, eighteenth and twenty-eighth of the month are all unlucky dates; begin nothing new on them. Don't set out on a journey on a Wednesday. On a Thursday don't take a purgative, don't go to the *hakim*, don't have a vein opened. At night don't tighten the foot-cord of the bed. If a lizard falls on your clothes, pour water in which gold has been dipped over your body. If you get hiccups, know that someone is thinking of you. If a fly goes into your mouth, know that you will eat something sweet. If a child's eyes are sore, sweep away the *chhut*. If a child gets smallpox, don't fry meat with spices, don't come in wearing clean clothes, don't come in after bathing. Don't sweep the bed with a broom; it brings ill luck. When a new unglazed water pot is filled, first give some man a drink from it. If your hand touches someone's nose, touch his hand to your own nose, or he will fall ill.

In short, there are hundreds of such things; how far shall I go on? And, my child, in our city, since women have begun to read and write a little, many of these things have been given up; but the women of the small towns still go on believing them. A gentleman who came from somewhere outside settled in our neighbourhood, and we used to visit his womenfolk. When I met them, hundreds of such things were on their tongues.

Besides this, there is no end to the customs at festivals, deaths and weddings. They bring no benefit either in religion or in this world, and lakhs of rupees are wasted on them for nothing. The men grow tired of earning, but the women never tire of spending. Your Abba used to say that the English have written down the customs of this country in a book and sent it home to their own land. Ever since I heard that, the thought keeps coming back to me [7]: oh, oh! a country where every child can read and write, and men and women alike are the very image of good sense; when those people look at that book, how they must laugh at our follies! And this is nothing yet; who knows what else our ignorance will show us? And on top of this our sisters want their husbands to obey them and to wash their feet and drink the water! Let them just look into their own collars and see who we are and whom we want to make obedient to us. If your heart could be in tune with an ox, a cow or a goat, then your husbands' hearts could be in tune with you. Between you and them there is as much difference as between a human being and an animal. But if you too learn to read and write and become human, no one will be a better friend and comforter to them than you.

Listen: one person's heart is in tune with another's only when both have the same kind of nature. However much love and affection you show children, they run a hundred *kos* from the company of grown-ups; but if some grown-up joins them and starts playing their games, it is no trouble at all to win them over. In the same way old people are uneasy in the company of the young, but if a young man becomes grave and weighty and talks like the old, they will mix with him and talk freely. And the hearts of woman and man are joined by God's own power; just as a magnet draws iron to itself, a man's heart is drawn to a woman of its own accord. Now if the woman also has this one thing more, that whatever she does she does to suit his temper, and whatever she says she says so that it pleases him, what more does a man want? Indeed, then he will not bear to be parted from her for a moment.

Think of the man as a thirsty traveller and the woman as a spring. If around the spring there is the shade of trees, and green grass waving on every side, and a cool breeze blowing, then after drinking the traveller will long to sit there an hour or two and enjoy the greenery. But if it is a bare spring and nothing pleasant about the place, he will drink and go his way. In the same way, if the wife of the house suits his temper, the husband will sit with her, talk with her, take her advice. If his mind is cheered, it will be cheered by her; if his heart is content, it will be content in her company. Eating, drinking, sleeping, sitting, laughing, talking: nothing will please him without her.

But to understand a man's temper, to do everything to suit it and to make a home in his heart, is something a woman cannot learn without knowledge, because within the four walls of the house there is nothing except knowledge to teach her to be human. Even women who go about outside cannot gain any good sense without knowledge. In Hindustan there are scores of communities that have no custom of *purdah* (the seclusion of women), yet among them too husband and wife are at odds. It is true that one hears of the English that there is no love anywhere like the love between husband and wife among them. The reason seems to be just this, that their women are educated and capable, keep their husbands' hearts in their hands in every way, and do nothing against their wishes; and the husbands come to dote on them.

My child, knowledge is a great thing. Educated women of former times did greater things even than men. The emperor Shah Jahan had his daughter Roshan Ara Begum well educated. When Alamgir imprisoned his father, Roshan Ara did not leave her father; she went into the prison with Shah Jahan herself, and served her father like a slave girl for the rest of his life. [TN: Historically it was Shah Jahan's elder daughter Jahanara who shared his confinement at Agra (1658–1666); Roshan Ara took Aurangzeb's side. Hali's text names Roshan Ara.] Look: a son like Alamgir, educated, a scholar, a man of prayer, abstemious, treated his father like that, and the daughter did her duty so well that people praise her to this day. All these are the virtues of knowledge.

Well, that is a story of old times, and perhaps someone will not believe it; let me tell you something nearer. Your father knew an Englishman, and we used to visit his memsahib. One day she said that our sovereign princess, Queen Victoria, has written a book, in which from beginning to end she has written about her husband, herself and her children. [TN: Presumably *Leaves from the Journal of Our Life in the Highlands* (1868).] There are scores of things in it that make one exclaim, God is great! God has created such women in the world too.

Well, it is hard to vie with princesses, but a person should at least know her gain as gain and her loss as loss; not cling to whatever she has heard from her elders, whether there is gain in it or loss. Let the child die of smallpox or live, but there's no question of having it treated. At most, they give it gold-water to drink, or sit fanning it with a neem twig. But there will certainly be prohibitions: no meat may be cooked in the house, no dish may be fried with spices, and no one who has bathed or put on clean clothes may let her shadow fall on the child.

As for the vaccination the government has brought out, if it is given to a hundred children, one may hope by God's grace that ninety of them will not get smallpox at all, and the ten that remain are, by God's grace, in no great danger either; if it comes at all, it will come mildly. Your Abba said one day that he had never in his life seen a smallpox mark on an Englishman's face. But here the very name of vaccination fills them with dread. In smallpox season the government doctors go from lane to lane asking people to have their children vaccinated, but these women are as frightened of it as one is of death. The moment they hear the word doctor, they bolt their doors.

Further: *galrali*, *bad*, *aurangzebi*, *kanth-mala*, *kanpere* [TN: names for children's swellings and sores of the glands, groin, neck (scrofula) and ears (mumps)], and all the other kinds of boils and pimples that children get, they leave the children wasting in them for months, trusting to the humbug of Indian barber-surgeons, or having them exorcised by mullas and cunning men; but English medicine, which cures such ailments of years in a moment, they won't let anywhere near.

Then when the child has a fever they will never show it to a *hakim*; deluded that it is the evil eye, they will try quite other remedies. Here they will burn salt and mustard seed or black seeds for smoke; there they will wave alms over the child and have them put out at a crossroads. Then when the child's eyes are sore they will never have a *hakim* or an eye doctor treat it. If they do treat it, this is what they will do: wrap a rag round a cord and have it thrown where passers-by will step over it, then soak it in oil and light it, fill a vessel with water and sweep away the *chhut* (contagion) from the child, asking it each time, 'Has the *chhut* been swept away?' and it says, 'Yes, the *chhut* has been swept away.' The third time they drop the wick into the water.

Further, for a woman whose children do not live there are quite other remedies. Here there is a *guzisht* (a rite of passing over), there a *nahan* (a ritual bath). Here there are prohibitions: she must not eat anything fried in a *karahi*, nor eggs, nor fish, nor jaggery or milk and curds; she must not go where someone has died, nor to a *chhatti* (the sixth-day rite after a birth). Besides these there are scores of other absurdities. No one understands that if God means to give life, the child will live anyhow, and if He does not will it, what use are these silly notions?

Then for a woman who has no children at all, here they lay out offerings for the fairies, there they hold sessions for them. Before those on whom the fairies 'come', *domnis* (professional women singers) sing all night long, while the women themselves keep swaying their heads and ask for whatever they please. The fairies are held in such awe that they will not say their name; when they speak of them, they say 'the ones above'. Someone should ask them what sort of creatures fairies are, that they will give children! Giving children or withholding them is in the Master's hands; He gives to whom He will and withholds from whom He will. Besides this, in a time of trouble, leaving God aside, one makes a vow to Allah Bakhsh, another gives a goat to Shaikh Saddu, another offers a cow to Sayyid Ahmad Kabir; here Bale Miyan is worshipped, there Nanhe Miyan is propitiated, elsewhere Darya Khan holds sway. [TN: Saints and spirits of popular North Indian devotion.] In short, whomever you look at is caught in one calamity or another.

Well, these things are such that little by little they have largely gone from the city, and where they remain it is among ignorant, unlettered people whose men and women are quite untouched by learning. And these faults too are such that anyone with a little sense can understand them when they are explained.

But, my child, besides these there are many hidden diseases which a person thinks nothing of, and yet they are finishing her off all the while. To understand these diseases and to cure them is the work only of someone whose eye is fitted with the microscope of knowledge. All of us sitting here now, whether it is me, or you, or anyone else, carry these ailments; but no one can bear to have her diseases made known to anyone. If you told someone now that she has this disease, she would take offence at once. That is exactly why these diseases are so hard to cure. Just as the weevil gets into wood, they finish a person off from inside. In the outward diseases there is fear for one's life, but in these there is fear for one's faith."

I said, "Dear Amma-jan, what are those diseases?" She said, "My child, the diseases are these: that a person thinks herself better than everyone and thinks nothing of anyone beside her; that she burns with envy on seeing someone well off; that she keeps malice [8] in her heart while outwardly remaining a friend; that she speaks ill of people behind their backs and flatters them to their faces; that she meets people only for her own ends; that for her own profit she is willing to see another harmed; that she does good deeds for show; that if she does someone the smallest kindness she goes telling everyone about it; that she searches out faults in others and shuts her eyes to her own; that when she sees one fault in someone she washes away all their good qualities. In the same way greed, gluttony [9], tale-bearing [10], lying, deceit and treachery: these are all diseases. May God not give these ailments even to one's worst enemy. Theft, gambling, adultery: these faults are at least such that the one who commits them knows them to be faults; but the faults I have told you of never show what they are. The proud person never thinks herself proud. The backbiter is never ashamed of speaking ill of others. The deceiver always counts deception a skill."

I said, "Amma-jan, then how can a person be rid of these faults?" She said, "My girl, they can be cured only by one who has knowledge and reads books of ethics all the time, and knows each fault the way a physician recognises the root of each disease.

People say that our sovereign princess, Queen Victoria, is so merciful and gracious that it is hard to equal her. Pride and arrogance have never come near her. It is said that one day she was driving along a road somewhere in her carriage, far from any town. An old man was walking along with a bundle of grass on his head. The Queen had his bundle put in the carriage and seated him at the back of it. When they came to the place he had to reach, the Queen said, 'Go; your place has come.' He got down, made his salaam and said, 'If my mother were alive today, even she would not have done more for me than Your Majesty has done.'

In the same way, one day there was something of a crowd on the road. When the Queen's carriage came that way, the servants began to push people aside. The Queen saw that the crowd was large; she at once got down from the carriage and did not get in again until she was clear of it.

In the same way, one day a prince, playing, wandered down to the river. A poor man's son was catching fish there. The prince asked him for some fish. He gave him one, but when he asked again he would not give. The prince was a little rough with him. The boy was older than him, and gave him a good beating. Somehow the news reached the Queen. The Queen sent for the boy, praised him warmly and said, 'Well done! You are very spirited and brave, not to be afraid even of the king's son.' Then she had him educated and brought up with the princes, and gave him money to trade with. There is an island in that country where no merchant is his equal.

See, such is the courtesy of learned people. Today when some lowly maidservant's wage goes up from one rupee a month to two, she won't give a civil answer; and she who is heir to two kingdoms behaves like this. All this is the shining forth of knowledge."

Amma-jan was talking in this way when the Mughlani came in. That day it had happened that two or three girls of our family had come over. Once I got talking with them, I forgot to go to the Mughlani. When I remembered, it was already ten o'clock, which was my writing time, so I did not go. Now when I saw the Mughlani's face, I thought to myself, she is sure to complain about you now. For fear of Amma-jan I made some excuse and slipped away. Behind my back the Mughlani filled her ears [11] well. Amma-jan said nothing at the time when she heard it.

Next day, when the same hour came, she took me thoroughly to task [12]. First she was angry, then she began to explain gently: "My girl, knowledge is the king and skill is its minister. Just as a king is useless without his minister, knowledge without skill is of no use in the world. Women are taught knowledge so that they will give up bad habits and take up good ones, know God and the Prophet, run the house well, learn to bring up children, and keep their husbands' hearts in their hands. A woman's knowledge is never of use in a time of need [13]; what is of use then is the skill of her own hands. Are you proud [14] that in your father's house there is everything God has given? Bless you, never think so. May God never bring an evil hour; otherwise in a moment everything is turned upside down. What are we, after all? When bad times come, the heirs of a kingdom [15] are reduced to want for a single loaf. Well, leave aside the *Nadir-gardi* [16] and the *Shah-gardi*; that was an age [17] ago. It was only yesterday that, when Ghulam Qadir put out Shah Alam's eyes, such calamities fell on the Fort and the city. [TN: Nadir Shah sacked Delhi in 1739; *Shah-gardi* refers to the Afghan invasions under Ahmad Shah Abdali that followed; the Rohilla chief Ghulam Qadir blinded the Mughal emperor Shah Alam II in 1788.] Noblewomen who had ten maids at a time ready to work for them at every moment went about lane after lane in their burqas looking for work as maidservants themselves, and no one would take them; while those who lived by earning a few pennies' wages, God had given everything to even then.

And, dear girl, it is great foolishness to learn no skill or craft yourself, relying on men's earnings. No one in the world can make more of their wives than the English, and God has given their men everything today. But one hears that among them there is no woman without a skill; they do such work as no man could. Why, even [18] our princess Queen Victoria knows scores of the world's skills; she draws a picture so well that whoever else draws, draws no better. Well, when the ruler of the country is like this, what account are we?

Listen: when I rack my brains so over every matter with you, I do it not for myself but for your own good. Not now, but when you are grown up you will know the value of these things. At this moment our anger must seem bad to you, but the day is not far off when you will remember this anger. There will be scores of people to agree with everything you say, but after we are gone there will be no one to be angry with you."

In short, she went on with such advice until twelve o'clock. Then everyone got up and went to sleep in their beds.
