# The Second Majlis

Atu-ji's narrative

Zubaida Khatun's only son, Syed Abbas, relates: In Delhi, in the well-known lane called Aqil Khan's Lane, there were two brothers among the sons of the nobility there. The younger, who was my maternal grandfather, was named Khwaja Fuzail, and the elder Khwaja Kumail. I never saw Khwaja Kumail; I have heard that at the age of thirty or thirty-five he left his post in Delhi and went away somewhere. My grandfather felt the wound of his loss deeply. God Almighty had given him wealth and property and everything: there was income from rents in the city besides, four villages held in *jagir* (a grant of land revenue) besides, and he had ten men under his command as well. Great and small in the city all paid him respect. He had all this, but without his one brother it was all as nothing in his eyes. By God's grace, however, my grandmother was a most intelligent and learned woman. She would try in all sorts of ways to take her husband's mind off his grief, and as long as he was at home he forgot his brother. When my mother was born his thoughts were somewhat divided. The wish had been that God would give a son, but God Almighty gave a daughter such that a hundred sons on one side and this daughter on the other. Grandfather and grandmother both doted on her. But there is a great difference between the love of the wise and the love of the foolish. The love of the wise shows very little at the start, but its end is excellent; the love of the foolish begins very well, but ends very badly.

Some people never have a moment's rest from petting their children: now kissing the face, now caressing, now hugging, now laying the child on their breast; whatever it asks for they give it to eat, whatever it wants they give it. The usual end of such things is that the child loses all awe of them, grows impudent and will not do as it is told. If, when it comes to its senses, it turns out a decent person through its own good nature, one can say nothing; but the parents have certainly spoiled its beginning. Someone has well said that loving a child is like watering a field: just as a flood of water damages the crop, so love beyond measure spoils a child.

Those who are wise and think out the end of everything beforehand do not lose control of themselves when love wells up in their hearts. If they love a child they do so only so far that it goes on looking on them as its guardians. In feeding and clothing it they keep its good and harm in mind. Whatever would upset its health they do not let near it; whatever would spoil its habits they do not let even cast its shadow. Until a child is five or seven it does not understand its own good and harm at all; fire, water, poison and antidote are all one to it, and it will only properly understand its own good and ill when it is grown up. That is why those who truly wish their children well never simply do what the children say. Sometimes they put them off with comfort and reassurance, sometimes they frighten them with a threat, sometimes they bring them to understand with a smack. In short, as promising as God Almighty had made my revered mother, such were the parents she was given. They spent all their love and affection on the right formation of her character.

Here is something worth hearing. When my mother was born my grandmother looked for a wet-nurse. One day she said to her husband too that if a nurse could be found she should be engaged. My grandfather said: "Listen, my dear! Finding a nurse is not difficult; ten women could come this very moment. But until I find the kind of woman I want, I will certainly not engage one. I have read in books, and reason too says, that the qualities of the woman who suckles a child are bound to influence it. It is not only because you say so; I am looking for a nurse myself. At the very least a nurse must have these two qualities: first, she must be of a respectable family, and second, she must be good-tempered and have a sense of honour. You keep looking too. Tell these women who go about from house to house that if they find such a woman they should have her engaged; I am ready to pay ten rupees a month."

In short, how much shall I tell? For six months my grandmother suckled the child herself. In that time many women came, but one of the kind they wanted was rather hard to find. At last, after six months, the nurse Husaini Khanum was engaged. To praise her at length would be superfluous, but this much I know: it was my mother's good fortune that a person like Husaini Khanum was engaged to suckle her. My grandmother treated her with the utmost consideration. Whatever she ate herself she gave her to eat; whatever she wore herself she had made for her too. Whatever clothes were made for my mother, a matching set was made first for her foster-brother. In the end she became one of the family and spent her whole life in that house.

One day Husaini Khanum had come to visit my revered mother. Heaven knows what led to it, but she began to say to me: "The way your grandmother brought up your mother, who else would do it? Sir! The great regard I was shown was all thanks to your mother; otherwise my sisters are wandering about by the thousand and nobody even asks after them." (My revered mother's name was Zubaida Khatun, but the nurse used to call her Bi Zubaida.) She was still talking to me like this when my mother came in. The nurse said: "Bi Zubaida! I don't suppose you remember those toys of your childhood?" She said: "Why, nurse! Why shouldn't I remember? I must have been a good six or seven years old; until then I still played with them." I said: "Mother dear! Were they elephants and horses of clay?" She said: "No, son! Not of clay, of cloth. The Mughlani who was in service with us was very skilled at sewing. Mother had her sew cloth toys for me and then had them varnished. But they had the oddest names. The ones that were very ugly, and that you felt revulsion just to look at, were named one Anger, one Abusive Tongue, one Backbiting, one Lying, one Mischief, one Tale-bearing, one Slattern, one Stubbornness, one Sharp Tongue, one Shirker, one Sloth, one Idleness. Among them one toy was in the shape of two witches; its name was Quarrelling. From its look you could plainly see that two hags were fighting; this one's locks [1] in that one's hand, and that one's locks in this one's hand. There was a horse among them too, of a strange shape: staring eyes, a frightful face, mane all tangled, tongue hanging out. It had a spring fitted in it; when you pressed it, off it went. For two yards it went like an arrow from a bow, and then it fell flat on its face and rolled about. Its name was Hasty. And of the ones that had pretty faces, one was named Modesty, one Self-respect, one Good Sense, one Cleanliness, one Virtue, one Forbearance, one Patience, one Contentment, one Kindness, one Concord, one Obedient. Among them was one toy in the shape of a deer; from its bearing you could plainly see that it was about to bound away. Its name was Briskness. And among them was a most beautiful fairy; she looked as if she were smiling, and you could not help wanting to go on looking at her. Her name was Cheerful."

I said: "Mother dear! Why did Grandmother have these toys made?" She said: "Son! She had them made so that I would come to hate bad ways and take up good habits." I said: "And did you find they did you any good?" She said: "Son! What other good they did I can't say, but this much is certain: to this day their faces have not faded from my heart. When anyone flies into a rage, the face of that toy named Anger rises before my eyes. Or when two women quarrel and fight, the figure of those two witches comes and stands before me. Or when someone does a thing in a hurry, the picture of that horse comes before me. Understand all the others the same way."

Then she went on: "Son! It was not this one thing only; Mother trained me in all sorts of new ways. If I had been fit for anything, I would have become a real person in her company. I remember that when my *Bismillah* was done, Mother first of all taught me the prayers that are recited in *namaz* (the ritual prayer) and the rules for performing it. When I had learned them well, she stood me up to pray, and for ten or fifteen days she had me pray in front of her. Then whenever she had leisure she would take me with her, and in all sorts of ways set the fear of God in my heart, saying: 'My child! God Almighty is near us and with us at every moment and every hour. Whatever we do, He sees; whatever we say, He hears. If someone goes and hides behind seven curtains, before God it is as plain as you and I. See that you never go near anything we forbid, neither before us nor behind our backs. If we do not see, God certainly sees.' Besides this she kept teaching me the manners and rules of everything: eating and drinking, getting up and sitting down, walking about, speaking and listening, respect for elders, regard for one's betters. And she would stop me from things like sitting with the servants, talking loudly, running about, staring people in the eye while speaking, laughing out loud, going about bareheaded at all hours, going up on the roof without need; and she kept such a check on everything that I shall never forget her warnings and admonitions as long as I live. Son! That is why I remember the things of my childhood to this day; otherwise what age is five years? How could I remember the things of that age until now? Many of her ways were such that I took lessons from them of my own accord. When I was ten or twelve, then I realised that Mother did these things to teach me. You have already heard about the toys; how was I to know at that age that those toys were for my training? Besides that, when a servant made some mistake and she found out it had been done deliberately, she would scold her in front of me, and say such hard things that my ears pricked up [2]. If she found anything untrue in someone's words, she would take her to task [3] in front of me and say: 'Listen, my good woman! I cannot bear a lie [4]. I can't stand a lie even from my own children. You have told your lie this time and that's that; if you are caught lying again, there is no place for you in my house.' And when someone made a mistake and came and told the plain truth, she was not so angry with her, and would say: 'Because you told the truth, I have forgiven your fault.' When someone came to the house and told of some child's mischief or naughtiness, she would say to my face: 'If a child of mine were like that, I would scorch the wretch's face.' Sometimes she would put herself to trouble merely to teach me, but would not give up her practice. For example, she was unwell [5] and some elderly lady came to ask after her. Now, as long as the lady sat with her, Mother would never dream of lying down on the bed; she would go on sitting up. If someone insisted very much, she would put a cushion behind her back. Seeing her, I too got into the same habit. Sometimes she would lay such stress on some small thing that it became a lesson to me for life, and I would understand that this thing was very important to do. For example, I got up leaving my little bundle of things open, and some maidservant got up to tie it, and Mother happened to see; now the woman would never dare tie it up and put it away. Mother would have it opened again in the same way and make me tie it."

Having said this she turned to Nurse Husaini: "Well, nurse! Do you remember these things too?" She said: "Well, I never! You were a child of seven and you remember; I was a grown woman of thirty, and I wouldn't remember?" "Very well, do you remember other things from that age too?" Mother said: "Nurse! I haven't written it all down in a book. Plenty I remember and plenty I have forgotten." The nurse said: "Well then, when you sat down to study with the schoolmistress, how old were you? And at what times did you do what? And at what time did you go and sit with the master? And what advice did they give you?" Mother said: "Nurse! I haven't the strength [6] in my head to lay the whole tale out before you now." I said: "No, Mother dear! We shall hear it. I put you on oath by my life, if you don't tell it." She said: "There, listen to that: one trouble has become two. Now the nurse has set this thorn-bush on my tail. Had I known, I would never have mentioned it. Very well, son, I will do as you say. Now go to the Maulvi Sahib and do your lesson [7]; I will tell it tonight when I have leisure."

So when we had finished our evening meal, Mother told the whole story. She said that I was five years old when Mother handed me over to the schoolmistress. This much I remember well: for seven years I studied only the Quran Sharif with her, and in that time I learned five or six parts of it by heart. As yet nobody pressed me much over reading and writing.

When my eighth year began, all my hours were filled. From six in the morning until nine the schoolmistress taught me the Quran Sharif; then for an hour the Mughlani taught me some sewing. After the midday meal at ten, Mother had given me leave to play, but of my own accord I often went to the Mughlani and sat down to some sewing or other. At eleven o'clock Father would come from his reception hall into the women's quarters to take his rest. He would seat me on the carpet beside his bed and have me practise the *naskh* and *nastaliq* scripts in front of him. As long as he stayed awake he kept looking at my letters, and wherever my hand strayed he would correct me at once. When his eyes closed I would sit on practising by myself. He got up towards one [8] o'clock, and I sat writing there until then. The moment he woke, I put away my board, inkpot and pen and got up for my prayers. Sometimes he would keep me back to look at my practice. If it was not good, or not enough, he would explain things to me gently, and also say a word or two that made me ashamed. After the noon prayer I had my lesson with the schoolmistress in a book of religious rules in Urdu, and stayed with her until three.

When I had finished the Quran Sharif and knew the rules of prayer, fasting and washing, Father started me on Shah Abdul Qadir's translation of the Quran, Persian and arithmetic, and said: "When you have done with your lessons, sit down for an hour or half an hour with *Chahar Darvesh* (The Four Dervishes) or the translation of *Akhlaq-e-Mohsini* and read them on your own. Ask the schoolmistress about any word you don't understand." I took great pleasure in these books. He would say an hour or half an hour; I would stay at them for hours on end. At three o'clock I had my leave. In that time I would sometimes go and sit with the Mughlani and help with her work, and sometimes, when girls of the family came over and pressed me very hard [9], I would play dolls with them. But Mother had set me to such work from the very beginning that I had come to dislike play even in childhood.

Then, when I had said the afternoon prayer, I would go into the kitchen and watch the maidservant grinding the spices, putting the pot on the fire and browning the meat, and I would keep an eye on the measure of salt and water and the heat of the fire. That is the hardest thing in cooking. When I had grasped this and properly understood how everyday meals were cooked, Mother said: "Child! Sometimes cook something with your own hands too. This work isn't learned by watching; the whole game lies in the heat of the fire. Until every dish has come from your hands ten or twenty times you will never learn it. Besides, who knows when the habit may come in useful? A person's circumstances do not always stay the same." It had always been in my nature to learn every task with my whole heart. I was keen anyway, and then she had told me to; little by little I began to do all the kitchen work with my own hands: washing lentils, grinding spices, lighting the stove, peeling vegetables, slicing onions, tempering ghee, making kababs, setting broth to stew, boiling and draining rice [10], steaming pulao. I would just seat the maidservant in front of me and do all this myself before her. At first the dishes kept going wrong in my hands: sometimes too much salt in the curry, sometimes the lentils undercooked, sometimes too much water in the *khichri*, sometimes the meat scorched in the browning, sometimes the rice left hard at the core. But Mother was never cross about it. If Father grew impatient on some day, she would stop him with a sign, and say to him behind my back: "The person you think cooked the meal did not cook it; Zubaida cooked it. She is still a child; at my bidding she cooks a pot now and then. Her hand will come right with cooking and cooking. If you start finding fault with her cooking already, she will never set foot near the stove again." In short, in a few days I had a fair knack for cooking. I had also learned the methods for the dishes that were made only once in a while [11]. But I am a truthful woman: to this day I cannot cook them as they ought to be cooked. The truth is that such things come only to those who have a craving for good food [12], or to those whose trade it is. I never acquired a taste for fine eating, and by God's grace I never had to work as a cook; so how would it come to me?

Now there remained baking bread, and that I did rather shy away from. When Mother saw that this made me nervous, she would come and sit at the stove herself and put one or two breads on the griddle with her own hands, then have me put them on. I said, she is only putting herself to this trouble to give me courage; that is no way. One day I said: "Mother dear! I swear by my head, you must just stay in your own place [13]. My heart did quail a little at first, but now, just tell me, I will cook and bake a whole maund of flour every day." In short, in a few days I had learned to bake bread too: *chapatis*, *phulkas*, rich bread, bread stuffed with lentils [14], *parathas*, I could make them all with my own hands. Well, it would be silly to say I cooked better than the maidservant; she was my teacher after all. But my father had grown so used to the taste of my cooking that he did not like anything from any cook's hands. Heaven knows whether he praised me sincerely or out of kindness; but the more he praised me the more heart I put into my cooking.

So when everyone had finished eating, it was the custom to sit with Mother until nine or ten at night. She kept giving me all sorts of advice in the course of talk, and above all she made me keen on reading and writing. First she had a little bookcase made for me, and had handsome bindings made for all the small books in Father's library: one green, one red, one yellow, one sky-blue, one almond-coloured, and all stamped with gold. Then she arranged them all in my bookcase and said: "Child! This bookcase is yours, and here, keep its key yourself as well. If I hear the least complaint about you from the schoolmistress, I shall take the bookcase away." From that day the love of books was born in my heart, and for fear of losing them I put my whole heart into my studies too.

Then sometimes she would say: "Child! Do you know what comes of reading these books? Look, no news has come from your uncle for so long; if you read these books and his whereabouts became even a little known, you could sit right here and talk to him every day." Sometimes she would say: "Do you know where this sun comes from every morning and where it goes in the evening? By reading these books you will learn that too." Sometimes she would say: "Child! Just tell me, what is the difference between you and these poor children who go about naked? Are they not human beings?" I would say: "Mother dear! Why should they not be human?" "Well then, my darling [15]! Then how is it that you have, by God's grace, clothes and jewellery and everything, and they have neither shoes on their feet, nor a cap on their head, nor clothes on their body? The whole thing is that among them there is no custom of reading and writing, and God has given us this honour through learning." Sometimes she would say: "Just tell me, why do the children of comfortable families become beggars? And why do the children of the hungry and naked become rich?" Then she would answer herself: "Child! When the children of the educated stay ignorant, poverty comes; and where the children of the uneducated get a little learning, that house's fortunes turn." Sometimes she would say: "Just tell me, what are the most precious things in our house?" I would say: "Mother dear! Most precious is the jewelled jewellery, after it the plain jewellery, then clothes, the copper vessels, the china, the carpets, the rugs, the couch, the four-poster, the bed, the stool; those are the things." She would say: "No, darling [16]! Never say such a thing again. The whole house on one side and one book from your bookcase on the other, and even so they could not be equal. In these books there are some letters worth more than the price of the whole world."

Sometimes she would say: "Just tell me, is the rank of men higher or that of women?" I would say: "Mother dear! It seems the rank of men is higher." She would say: "No, my girl! Never think so. Learning is a thing through which a single woman can make millions upon millions of men her subjects. Look, our princess Queen Victoria, sitting thousands of miles from here, is ruling over two realms by the power of her learning." Sometimes she would say: "Child! If you read these books, you could tour all the countries of the world while sitting at home, and whatever there is above the sky and beneath the earth, at the bottom of the river and in the caves of the mountains, would all become clear to you as in a mirror." Sometimes she would praise with a thousand tongues whichever girl of our family was educated, and in all sorts of ways mock those who were unlettered. In short, until I was nine or ten she kept urging me to read and write with this sort of coaxing and encouragement. Her words made such an impression on my heart that my regard for learning grew day by day.

Atu-ji: Mahmooda Begum! What time will it be?

Mahmooda Begum: Hazrat! It has just struck ten.

Atu-ji: That's enough, child! Go to bed; we have to get up early in the morning. You shall hear the rest tomorrow.
