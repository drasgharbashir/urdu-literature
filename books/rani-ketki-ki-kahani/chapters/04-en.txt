# The Yogi Mahendar Gir Comes from Mount Kailas, and Turns Kunwar Ude Bhan and His Mother and Father into Deer

Jagat Parkas writes to his guru, who lived on Mount Kailas: "Come to our help somehow; a very hard calamity has fallen on us unfortunates. Raja Suraj Bhan has grown so wild-headed that he has sought a marriage alliance with us, the great kings."

Mount Kailas is made of a single solid piece of silver. On it lived Raja Jagat Parkas's guru, whom all the people of Indra's heaven called Mahendar Gir, absorbed in meditation and knowledge, day and night at the worship of the Lord, with some nine hundred thousand ascetics. Making gold and silver out of copper and tin was the least of it; he would put a magic pill in his mouth and fly about wherever he liked; and he knew other arts of such a kind as are beyond all telling. To make it rain gold and silver, or to take whatever form he pleased, was all child's play to him. And in singing, all except Mahadev himself held their ears before him in humility. Even Saraswati, whom the Hindus call the Primal Power, had learned some of her humming from him. Before him the six ragas and thirty-six raginis (melodic modes) stand at his service with joined hands, all the eight watches of the day, in the shape of young wives. There the male ascetics were called by such names as these: Bhairo Gir, Bibhas Gir, Hindol Gir, Megh Nath, Kedar Nath, Dipak Das, Joti Sarup, Sarang Rup; and the female ascetics were called in this way: Gujri, Todi, Asavari, Gauri, Malsiri, Bilaval. [TN: all of these are the names of ragas and raginis.] Whenever he wished, he would go flying about in mid-air seated on his throne, and his nine hundred thousand ascetics went with him, each with a magic pill in his mouth, dressed in ochre robes, their matted locks flying.

The moment a holy man brings Raja Jagat Parkas's letter, the yogi Mahendar Gir gives a roar that shakes the banks of cloud. Seating himself on his tiger skin, rubbing sacred ash on his face and muttering spells, he mounted the back of the horse of the wind, and all the ascetics, seated on their deer skins with their magic pills in their mouths, cried out: "Gorakh is awake!" In the blink of an eye he arrives where the battle between the two kings was going on. First a black storm came, then hail fell, then a great tempest came. No one was left with their wits. The elephants and horses and all the men and the whole host of Raja Suraj Bhan: no one could make out where they went, or who carried them off. And on Raja Jagat Parkas's people and on Rani Ketki's people a fine, fine drizzle of drops of screwpine water began to fall.

When all this was over, the Guru told his ascetics: "Ude Bhan, Suraj Bhan, Lachhmi Bas: make these three into deer and does and turn them loose in some forest, and break and scatter all who were with them." Exactly as the Guru said, they did it in a flash. Kunwar Ude Bhan, struck by calamity, and his father Maharaja Suraj Bhan, and his mother Maharani Lachhmi Bas, as deer and does, went on grazing the green grass of forest after forest for several years. And of that great host no trace at all was found, where it went or where it was. Leave what happened there where it is. Listen to what comes next.

Now for Rani Ketki's part. Together with Maharaja Jagat Parkas, his whole household fell at the Guru's feet, and all bowed their heads and said: "Maharaj! You have done a great deed; you have saved us all. If you had not arrived today, what would have been left? Everyone had resolved to die fighting. We knew we could do nothing against those wicked ones. Now take all our kingdom, give it away to whomever you like, make us all ascetics and take us with you. We cannot hold the kingdom. You saved us from Suraj Bhan's hands, but now if some uncle of his, Chandar Bhan, comes against us, how shall we be saved? We have no strength of our own. Shame on such a raja! How long shall we keep troubling you?"

Hearing this, the yogi Mahendar Gir said: "You are all our sons and daughters. Be happy, go about boldly, live in peace and comfort. Who is there who can so much as look at you with a hard eye? We have given you this tiger skin and this sacred ash. If ever such trouble comes again, pull one hair from this tiger skin, put it in the fire and burn it. The hair will not have finished burning before we arrive. As for the ash, it is for this: whoever puts it on the eyes like collyrium sees everything and is seen by no one, and can do whatever they please."

Guru Mahendar Gir, whose feet we should worship and whom we should call blessed Maharaj, hides nothing. Maharaja Jagat Parkas, waving a peacock-feather fan over him, took him to the queens. All of them filled their laps again and again with flowers of gold and silver, diamonds and pearls, and scattered them over him, and rubbed their foreheads at his feet. He patted them all on the back. Rani Ketki also prostrated herself, but in her heart she called the Guru a great many bad names. The Guru stayed there seven days and seven nights, seated Raja Jagat Parkas on his throne, and then, on that tiger skin of his, in the same way, landed back on Mount Kailas. Raja Jagat Parkas began to rule in his old way.

# Rani Ketki Weeps before Madan Ban, Remembering What Has Passed, and Despairs of Life, in Her Own Kind of Speech

The Rani was in great distress;
when could she tell good from bad?

Softly, softly she would moan,
and had no wish to go on living.

Sometimes she would say, "Listen, Madan Ban,
all the eight watches I think of nothing else.

Who feels thirst here, who feels hunger?
I keep seeing those same green trees.

Now my heart lives in dread of every drop,
now it is the dwelling-place of love.

How he alighted in the mango groves,
and how the night sighed and whispered,

and how I got up quietly and went,
and how you told him of my love,

how I took his ring from his finger
and gave him my own ring,

it keeps passing before my eyes:
the shape my heart took then, it keeps still.

How can I forget him? What am I to do?
How long must I go on fearing my mother and father?

Now I have heard, O Madan Ban,
that Ude Bhan has become a deer of the forest.

He must be grazing the green, green grass.
Have some pity, sink into thought for me.

I have forgotten how to leap and play.
Do not make me smell these fresh flowers.

Pick up the flowers and take them away from here;
my heart has broken into a hundred pieces.

Do not gather up my scattered heart;
bring a bundle of grass and set it down.

Let me look at the green of that,
for what else can I say to you?

In these eyes is the startled look of a deer;
my eyelashes have become like forest grass.

Whenever you look, they are brimming;
dewdrops of tears are spread across them.

This thing that has sunk into my heart
has fallen on me like a dew."

In this way, whenever she was alone, she would string pearls like these with Madan Ban.
