# Lightning in the Church, and Purdah: Poems 16–27

## 16. Lightning in the Church (1907)

Last night I came face to face with that miss in the church:
ah, that beauty, that sauciness, that delicacy, that figure!

Such style in her twisting tresses that calamities themselves were her disciples;
such grace in her graceful form that Doomsday itself was her martyr.

Eyes that were the mischief of the age, that would make one a sinner;
cheeks like a shining dawn, that angels would love.

A fiery speech, that flames leapt to hear;
an enchanting voice, at which the nightingale blinked.

Such charm in her walk that the stars would stop;
such defiance in her pride that governors would bow.

One who burned up piety with the fire of beauty,
who brought down lightning with the grace of a smile;

beside her fine speech, drowned in the sauciness of her talk,
she was lightning on the affairs of Turkey, Egypt and Palestine.

I was crushed, I was overthrown, no strength was left in my heart;
the tune whose notes had been dignity, that tune was gone.

My resolve of self-control had no effect at that moment;
I recited "Ya Hafiz", "O Protector", but nothing came of it.

I said, "O springtime of Nature's garden,
wealth and honour and faith are all sacrificed at your feet.

If you would pledge faithfulness and be mine,
my heart would have its fill of the whole world."

When in the heat of longing I opened my mouth like this,
she knitted her brows with pride and coquetry and said,

"It is impossible for me to be fond of Muslims;
the smell of blood comes from the tales of this people.

They speak of 'You shall not see Me' when they turn to prayer, [TN: Quran 7:143, God's answer to Moses, lan tarani.]
they make attacks on the frontier when they turn *ghazi* (holy warrior).

If someone sets himself up as Mahdi, they go wild;
they leap into fire, they fight against cannon.

If someone wins a little in the field, they swagger;
if they get the means to settle, they work a doomsday.

How can anyone be easy that they are well-intentioned?
The effect of the command of *jihad* (holy war) is still in their veins."

In the glances of that enemy of patience I found a hint of fondness;
my wretched heart heard the footfall of success.

I said, "O delight of my life, O rest of my soul,
the age is no longer under the influence of Adam and Noah.

In this garden there is no sapling of the Tree of Sinai;
in this age there is no madness for a houri's tresses.

Where in our minds now are Buraq and Rafraf?
The nation's eyes are fixed on the engine.

The colour of the life-staking Khalid is no longer in us;
only the colour of Hafiz of Shiraz rules the heart.

Here there is no cry of 'God is great', no ardour of the army;
every one of us recites only 'Glory be to God'.

The temper of the warrior's sword is sacrificed to your eyebrow;
the light of faith is sacrificed to the mirror of your face.

The question of good and bad has left the page of the mind;
we are of two minds, while saying that God is one.

Where is the wave of Kausar round my garden now?
In civilisation I am the pupil of the Elder of the Magi.

You have no cause, my life, to be angry with me;
it is a name and only a name; otherwise I am no Muslim."

When I said this plainly, that, if you have any sense,
you should put this doubt, this suspicion, out of your delicate heart,

and take my Islam to be a tale of the past,
she laughed and said, "Then take me as willing too."

This is the Urdu that pours soul into meaning;
this is the turn at which the mind stirs. [TN: Buraq and Rafraf are the heavenly mounts of the Prophet's night journey; Khalid ibn al-Walid was the great general of early Islam; Kausar is the river of Paradise.]

## 17. A weak, broken, wretched old man

A weak, broken, wretched old man
was going to the bazaar on some errand.

His back was bent with the weakness of age;
the poor fellow walked bowed over.

Some boys found this funny,
and thought up a gibe about his figure being a bow.

One boy said to him, "Tell us,
how much did you pay for this bow?"

The kindly and wise old man
laughed and said, "My son,

the moment you reach my age,
you will get this bow for free."

## 18. I said to Akbar, "Come into my cell"

I said to Akbar, "Come into my cell;
let us say our prayers on this mat, as is the custom.

Give up this commotion of the new education;
the Forgiving Lord will somehow see us through."

He said irritably, "Hell is easier for me
than being known as a fool at the college."

## 19. When Akbar saw Anwar's English dress at the gathering yesterday

When Akbar saw Anwar's English dress at the gathering yesterday,
he said, "These are signs of ruin.

The meaning too will change in the end,
if the outward form keeps changing like this.

You will begin to feel shy of worshipping the Creator;
you will be ashamed to declare your Islam.

You will become strangers to your dear ones of the homeland;
you will hide away in some bungalow, leaving home and family.

[illegible] the longings of the pores will rise;
the life that was easy will become hard.

You people will not agree among yourselves;
each will look at the other with distaste and denial.

In the end you will be neither of this side nor of that;
the English too will stay aloof, and the nation will be disgusted."

Anwar said, "Bless the Prophet! Very good indeed!
There is no doubt these words deserve praise.

But the generalising in your honour's speech
this sinner will not accept.

In every religion and community there are good and bad;
what sect is there in which all are righteous?

As for your mention of clothes and houses,
I do not accept that it is right either.

Good character is a matter of the inner self;
one who is good by nature will never become bad.

The fashions of the age will keep changing;
the world's movement shows no sign of stopping.

It is the command of necessity; one is compelled by necessity;
why should one not do, unwillingly, what one must?

The real aim is the soundness of the heart,
whether hat and overcoat, or gown and turban.

If there is any doubt about the truth of what I say,
hear this pearl-scattering saying of Sa'di:

'You have no need to wear a felt cap of honour;
be like a dervish, and wear a Tatar cap.'" [TN: The closing couplet is Persian, attributed in the text to Sa'di.]

## 20. Spring has come, the flowers have bloomed, adorning the garden court (1899)

Spring has come, the flowers have bloomed, adorning the garden court;
the nightingales have raised a clamour, eager in lament.

The emerald carpet has been spread, arranged with the adornment of greenery;
the morning breeze has gone, as if drunk, scattering ambergris.

The branches have swayed with the height of the intoxication of growth;
the birds of the garden have sung their songs, rejoicing.

The morning breeze has taken the branch of roses in its arms;
the buds have opened, become the colourful faces of idols.

The young ones of the garden have each shown their colour,
one by becoming jasmine, another by becoming the Judas-tree.

The flowers have made their ablutions with the dew in the garden court;
the song of the nightingale has risen as the call to prayer.

In the air of longing the branches have bowed to prostrate before the Creator;
every leaf, become a tongue, is busy telling its beads.

The tongue of the rose-petal has prayed in colourful phrases:
"May God, being kind, keep this garden green."

Eyes do fall upon the perfect in this world;
can a flower stay hidden among the leaves, Akbar?

## 21. I said, "I know a great many languages" (qita)

I said, "I know a great many languages;
for a long time I sat examination after examination.

German, French, Latin and English I have mastered;
my excellence is proved before the whole world."

A saucy-natured miss stuck out her tongue at me;
it was lightning in the cloud, or the moon in the sky.

She said, "You will remain ignorant of the pleasure of life
if you have not gained command of this tongue."

## 22. When the coquettish idol demanded of me

When the coquettish idol demanded of me,
"You are very distinguished in the art of poetry today;

attach some fine and lovely line to this one:
'If the age does not suit you, suit yourself to the age'",

I said, "There is a condition of beauty and grace;
so hear this delightful, eye-pleasing couplet:

'Wear the shade, my life, take off your peshwaz;
if the age does not suit you, suit yourself to the age.'" [TN: The refrain is a Persian proverb. The peshwaz is the long Indian gown; "wear the shade" plays on saya, "shade", and the English-style skirt also called saya.]

## 23. When I was displeased at such lack of regard

When I was displeased at such lack of regard,
that miss smiled with an air and said,

"There is no quality of honour left in you at all;
alas, only the sense of contempt remains."

## 24. God protect the Muslims, Akbar

God protect the Muslims, Akbar;
I despair of their prosperity.

They are lovers of the beloved called the Goal,
but they will not go near effort.

Let me tell you an imaginary joke,
which I have set down on paper.

Laila's mother said to Majnun,
"Son, if you pass your M.A.,

I will marry Laila to you at once;
I will become your mother-in-law without any fuss."

Majnun said, "That's a fine thing to say!
What has a lover to do with the twaddle of college?

What has this natural ardour of temperament
to do with the feeling of things crammed in?

Good lady, what has come over you?
Does anyone load grass on a deer?

This is a fine appreciation you have shown;
you have taken me for some Harchand Das.

I am ready to make my heart's blood flow,
but I will not accept the swelling of the brain.

If this is the condition fixed for union with Laila,
then here is my resignation, with longing and despair."

## 25. Although they have joined in political debate

Although they have joined in political debate,
the honoured Pandit Chand and Babu Ashutosh, [TN: names uncertain]

we keep altogether silent on this head;
we have understood this point of the wise Sayyid:

"Kings know the secrets of their own kingdom;
you are a beggar in a corner, Hafiz; do not cry out." [TN: The last couplet is from Hafiz.]

## 26. I married a silver-bodied miss in London

I married a silver-bodied miss in London;
for this fault I hear heart-rending taunts.

One says that he has spoiled the breed of the nation;
another says that he is of bad character and a rogue.

None of the elders gives it any fair thought in his heart;
now I am compelled to reveal this secret myself.

I had been strictly told: go to London, study English,
mix with the English nation, learn their very cut and style;

go and look at the glittering hotels,
taste soup and curry, and leave *yakhni* (broth) and *ash* (pottage);

meet the ladies and see their ways and manners,
dance in the hall, go to the club and play cards with them;

drain jar after jar of the wine of European civilisation,
and smash the glass of Asian piety to pieces.

When I acted on this, I came under the spell of fairies,
which wholly revived the warmth of my heart.

Before me were ladies like Venus, with magic eyes;
here was the urge of youth, and they were looking for a lover.

Her glance was enchanting, her words captivating,
her walk stirred mischief, her eyes rained lightning.

That glow of the fire of her cheek, before which the sun
was like the corpse of a moth before a candle.

When things were thus, how could it be that a lightning-bolt
should hold out a silver hand, and I say, "Keep off"?

On both sides the stirring blood of mischief ran in the veins;
it was a heart after all, it was no slice of ice.

Again and again the thought comes into my heart, Akbar:
if only someone would go and say to the honoured Sayyid,

"You have tied me to a plank in the depths of the river,
and then you say, 'Take care, do not get your hem wet!'" [TN: The closing couplet is Persian, a well-known verse often attributed to Hafiz.]

## 27. How long will the women be kept in purdah? (Kanpur, 27 August 1891)

How long will the women be kept in *purdah* (seclusion)?
How long will you remain the "masters" in this country?

When no sword is left to guard the women's quarters,
how long will these slats of the chilman serve?

The wife exists through the husband, and purdah is her duty; but
when the husband's learning itself is gone, how long the husband?

Minds grow in the air of the West;
how long these jealousies, this warmth, these heats?

Let the common people pack the dohars in third and intermediate class;
how long will the windows of second and first stay shut?

When *Iblis* (Satan) insists on the custom of showing the face,
how long will the daughters of Eve hide?

The honourable Akbar is a supporter of purdah,
but how long will he last, and his quatrains, how long? [TN: Miyan means both "husband" and "master, gentleman"; the poem turns on the double sense. The chilman is the slatted bamboo blind of the zenana. A dohar is a light cotton wrap.]
