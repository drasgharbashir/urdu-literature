# The Cataract of Lodore, and Sir Sayyid's College: Poems 28–34

## 28. That Southey, sweet-tongued master of speech

That Southey, sweet-tongued master of speech,
who was an English poet without equal,

at the request of a well-mannered daughter
whom he held dear in his heart,

wrote a matchless poem
showing the form of running water.

The water that flows in the midst of Lodore:
it is its force that the poet has shown.

Finding suitable English verb-forms,
he rhymed all their sequences.

He made a fine gathering of verbs,
so that it is both a lesson and a delight.

Brother Hasan insists
that I too should surge in this metre,

and show the flowing of the river,
in such a way that the connoisseurs of pearls speak of it.

It is no wonder he has not considered
what am I, and what is the renowned Southey?

Besides this there are other difficulties too;
the stages of this road are not easy.

I have not enough capital;
not those verb-forms, not those rhymes;

neither breadth in the language, nor such a taste;
here the usage is something else again. [TN: reading uncertain]

If it is translated, the meaning becomes muddled;
no coherence or order arises in the sense.

These are the obstacles I fear,
but, well, I am giving it some thought.

The difficulties there were, I have stated openly;
in short, look, here the water comes:

leaping and boiling,
strutting and frolicking,

this one forming and that one tautening,
dripping and straining,

roaring as it runs,
forcing as it is checked,

the hollows of the mountains, the pores of the earth,
on every side it is doing its work;

breaking loose here and straying there,
turning this way and slipping there,

dashing its head against the mountains,
shaking its hem on the rocks,

pressing the shore with mounds,
spreading a sheet over the grass,

wandering and clamouring,
setting the whole land awash,

singing and playing,
dancing the waves without pause,

swaying and strutting here,
whirling and catching there,

raging and seething,
foaming at the mouth in its fury,

that song of turbulence in high notes,
and in its own fervour bringing up the foam;

mending and tidying itself,
quivering and dancing,

booming here and humming,
buzzing there all by itself,

clinging and cleaving,
this bursting and that gathering in,

sinking in and turning back,
sliding and stirring,

this ebbing and that rising,
descending and climbing,

this drawing aside and that escaping,
pressing and being drawn,

slipping and tottering,
swaying and stumbling,

hiding the face of the earth,
turning dust into silver,

taking flower and thorn as alike,
tangling with each one equally,

carrying away and flowing,
bearing the slaps of the wind,

trembling and writhing,
sobbing and gurgling,

falling and tumbling from the height,
wandering and winding in the hollows,

springing and flying,
catching and bending,

cutting paths through the fields,
making the lands green,

filling the laps of the basins,
laying its favour on the earth,

making garlands of flowers,
catching in the whirl of the stones,

darting and rumbling,
welling up and whistling,

gleaming and glinting,
steadying and spilling,

setting its waves to fight the winds,
leading on the armies of bubbles,

writhing and sparkling,
showing off the bloom of the sunbeams.

So, in short, let this water flow;
now let the poet who knows fine points look on.

That is the outpouring of Southey's Lodore,
this the force of the sea of Akbar's thoughts.[1] [TN: Robert Southey's "The Cataract of Lodore" (1820), written for his children, describes the waterfall in a long cascade of rhymed participles. Akbar answers it with Urdu participles in -ta hua; "That is how the water comes down at Lodore" is Southey's own closing line. The English renders each Urdu line literally rather than imitating Southey's verse.]

## 29. O philosopher, the force of electricity and steam

O philosopher, the force of electricity and steam:
when is it the straight path for the soul?

People of vision do not go by telegraph;
the sound heart is not drawn along by rail.

## 30. Everyone knows that learning is the soul of life

Everyone knows that learning is the soul of life;
a man without learning is incomplete.

Any nation in the world without learning and skill,
it is the demand of Nature, must remain a slave.

If education is not suited to the times,
what hope then of wealth, comfort and respect?

This thought was engraved on the Sayyid's heart;
taking God's name, he laid the foundation of a school.

He suffered shocks, bore grief, heard abuse,
but the servant of the nation did not leave his work.

He showed the age the strength of heart and mind;
he showed how those who do things do them.

Since his intention was good, God gave blessing;
the college was set up with great splendour and dignity.

There was a shortage of capital, no support at all;
the Sayyid's heart was set on completing the arrangements.

At last that man of auspicious step rose to travel,
with a few friends, men of learning and fine speech.

With the guidance of fortune he reached the goal of his wish;
he paid his respects to the ruler of the Deccan,

showed him the state of things and explained the need,
and presented the nation's message with skill.

His Highness was moved to pity at the nation's state;
what then? The river of general bounty surged:

he raised the monthly grant from one thousand to two thousand;
the gift beyond hope was, without question, this.

Akbar's prayer at the court of God is this:
may this ruler and this state endure until Doomsday.

How timely it came, that without need of thought
"The bounty of the Nizam" is its own date![2] [TN: The last half-line is a chronogram: the letters of fayyazi-e Nizam add up, by the abjad reckoning, to 1892.]

## 31. Someone said to the Sayyid, "Honoured sir, you

Someone said to the Sayyid, "Honoured sir, you
accept neither a *pir* (Sufi master) nor any spiritual leader.

You do not ask help from the world of the dead,
nor accept the ritual of reciting the *Fatiha* (opening chapter of the Quran) for them.

Just consider the Hindus:
with all sincerity they accept every god.

There are many who worship the elements with all their heart;
they worship fire or they accept the wind.

The Christians too are devoted to the name of Mary;
with their hearts they accept the Messiah, on whom be praise.

Among you yourselves, the dignified Shias
accept the Prophet's family, the People of the Cloak.

Those who bear the title of the noble Sufis
are devoted to tombs, and accept the saints.

People ask their wishes of pure souls;
they accept some elder or some leader.

Then what has got into you, that you
accept neither the Helper nor the Remover of Difficulties?"

He answered, "We are followers of the Quran;
we respect everyone, but it is God we accept.

Our authority, friend, is 'You alone we ask for help'; [TN: Quran 1:5.]
we accept that one and only Fulfiller of Needs.

His name is on our tongues, the Living, the Self-Subsisting;
we accept His boundless power.

This smell of associating others with God is itself the root of war and discord;
so when do the wise accept such a plague?"

The honoured Sayyid's answer is fine, Akbar;
we accept his sound and proper words.

But most of the elders of this new civilisation
accept neither God nor the way of prayer.

In words they say everything, but in truth
they accept only the power of the ruler. [TN: "The Helper" (dastgir) and "the Remover of Difficulties" (mushkil-kusha) are popular titles of the saint Abd al-Qadir Jilani and of Ali.]

## 32. Someone asked the moth, "O foolish one

Someone asked the moth, "O foolish one,
why do you throw away your life by falling into the fire?"

It burned and said, "O enemy of reason,
hear from me this bright meaning:

is it better to seek union with the flame,
or to be trampled in the dark?"

## 33. What doubt is there of the sun's splendour and glory?

What doubt is there of the sun's splendour and glory?
What thing in the imagination is brighter than it?

But after it has set it has no effect at all;
one should think this question over well.

However much you think about the sun,
not even a corner of night's veil will lift.

Even if you worship it, it will not be brought back;
the darkness will not go by calling on it.

The state of man too, in my view, is the same;
look at it with the eye of inquiry, and it is just so.

However lofty and accomplished someone may be,
however influential or high-minded,

when he has left the world for the land of non-being,
to think of any help from him is idle and empty.

Only God's being is self-subsisting and living;
only God's word lives for ever.

Understand that following and respect are one thing,
but beseeching and asking for one's needs is another.

Whether some shaikh is offended or a Brahmin displeased,
this is the truth, this is the right philosophy.

## 34. When I had finished mastering my subject at college

When I had finished mastering my subject at college,
Mister [illegible] said this to me:

"Though your fame has spread far and wide,
you do not have reason and understanding like mine."

I said, "O enlightened mind,
that is just what Bashir weeps over:[3]

you have learned from your father,
and what I studied, I studied from you."
