# The Example of a Poor Family's Contented Life

## The Example of a Poor Family's Contented Life, Showing That Luxuries Bring Trouble and Love of Ease Brings Hardship[1]

Mahmuda: "Wealth never, never brings contentment. The teacher keeps pointing out the life of this neighbour of ours to explain it to me: look, what a free and contented life she has. There is herself, there is her husband, and there are four or five children, who are very small, not old enough to do any work. The husband carries earth somewhere at the canal, and she grinds flour for hire. Go and look at her home: there is no wooden platform and no floor-cloth; perhaps there are three or four broken-down beds strung with coarse tape, and without any fuss they all sleep on bare[2] beds. For pots there are clay water-jars, clay cooking pots, clay bowls and plates, and a wooden ladle. And that is all there is to it[3]."

Husn Ara: "Is that a free and contented life? May God not show such pleasures even to an enemy! What greater misery could there be in the world? She is worn out with her own life, and the teacher envies her contentment!"

Mahmuda: "At first I too was surprised at what the teacher said, but for a long time I kept watching the neighbour and her children. At last I too understood that the teacher was right. When I thought about it I saw that bodily[4] comfort and bodily hardship all depend on habit. Someone who is used to hard work is so happy in it that we, lying about idle, can never have that happiness. This same neighbour: I have seen it when the sticky[5] heat of the rains is on and the air is so still that not a leaf stirs; I stand in the outer courtyard fanning myself without stopping, and the sweat pours off me in streams and I can hardly get my breath; and there is our good neighbour, God keep her, inside the hall grinding at the hand-mill all alone. And when I listened I found that she was so happy, bless her, that she was even singing something to herself. At first I thought, what on earth could make her think of singing in that state? But when I called to her from the window she answered very cheerfully[6], 'What is it, child? Tell the teacher there are only two or three handfuls[7] left; I'm bringing the flour right now[8].' She answered in such a crisp voice that nothing about it seemed like hardship. A little later what do I see but her coming along laughing with the flour. She took the weights and scales along with the flour, weighed the flour, sifted it and filled the jar.

The teacher said, "Neighbour, did you cover the flour jar properly?"

Neighbour: "Yes, my lady, I covered it with the big platter and put the five-seer weight on top." The teacher: "Very well, you may go." Neighbour: "Won't you give me some more grinding[9]?" The teacher looked at her book[10] and said, "No, there's no need yet; there's flour for four or five days. It's the rainy season, and if it is kept a little too long, weevils[11] get into the flour and it turns bitter."

Neighbour: "No, my lady, do give me some grinding." The teacher: "Wretched woman, take a rest for one day! It's dreadfully hot; don't you feel faint?"

Neighbour: "What can I say? It's become such a habit that on a day when I have no grinding my whole body aches, and my food feels as if it were sitting on my chest. Sitting idle I feel sort of sluggish and can't settle to anything."

Mahmuda: "I said to myself, look, what complaints of hardship there are! The teacher said, "I'll give you grinding, but neighbour, I give you the wheat well measured out; why is the flour so fine that it blows away?"

Neighbour: "The wheat was damp; with the very first handful it started coming out as cracked meal, so I warmed it a little over the fire. Otherwise I don't even grind outside in the air; I grind inside the hall where no air comes through."

The teacher: "What can I say? I have been waiting for days for some donkey-man to call in the lane so that I could buy two sacks of earth. Then the hall could be plastered, and the hearths are broken too, they could be plastered again. If I had earth I would get you to make me the hearths."

Neighbour: "What's hard about getting earth? Himmat will be taking his father his bread in a little while; he'll fill a basket with earth there and bring it back. Canal earth is sticky and lasts well too."

The teacher: "If the earth comes, do that work tomorrow instead of the grinding." The neighbour began to call down blessings on her. A little later what do I see but Himmat's little sister, about ten years old, walking in front with a big basket on her head, and our neighbour coming along behind. When I saw the poor wretched girl I felt very sorry for her. I didn't know what she had brought, but I ran quickly and helped her get the basket down at the door. When I looked, it was wet canal earth.

I said, "Oh, God bless you! What a dreadful thing you've done! You poor thing, such a load!" Just then the neighbour arrived too, and I began to quarrel with her: "Neighbour, haven't you any pity in your heart? Look at this girl's size, and look at her carrying such a load all the way from your house to here! If the girl is such a burden to you, then for goodness' sake give the poor thing poison one day and put her to sleep. Really, not even a stepmother would do such a thing!" I had helped to lift the basket down, and it was so heavy, like a stone, that it slipped from my hands halfway. Wet canal earth, heaven help us, iron is lighter. In that little time I was quite out of breath. I wanted to blame the neighbour with such vehemence[12], but the neighbour put it off carelessly[13], saying, "My lady, we are poor people and this is a poor family's daughter; we spend day and night carrying loads. What is a basket of earth? She carries beds by herself. The day before yesterday she took the millstone to the door of the mill-dresser[15] to have it roughened[14]. If our children were delicate darlings and Lady Fragile and Miss Slender like the daughters of the rich, we couldn't manage for a single day." When I heard what the neighbour said I was so ashamed[16] that I broke out in a sweat, and I thought to myself, O God, what is it? These people never get enough to eat, so why are they so strong and sturdy? One day I asked the teacher and she said, "All this strength, all this vigour, all this power comes from hard work. People like us lie about idle day and night like the *ahadis*[17]; the food we eat just sits in our bellies as it went in; our digestion is out of order and we never feel properly hungry. Ever invalids, always ailing: sometimes constipation, sometimes indigestion; someone or other is at the doctor's every other day; the habit of treatment, the routine of medicine. Our constitutions are like the touch-me-not plant: one knock and it wilts. Whatever the season, we always have something wrong. If it is hot, somewhere a head is splitting with pain, eyes are burning, fire comes out of palms and soles; and so we spend our whole lives longing for an appetite. In the hot weather what little there was goes from bad to worse: there is no relief from water cooled with ice and saltpetre, and no comfort from sherbets of pomegranate, *phalsa* berries, jujube and water-lily. When the rains come, such preparations are made against flies and mosquitoes, you would think an enemy[18] had invaded some king's country. Because of the east wind the digestion[19] stops working[20] entirely, and the wind-pain[21] that was in the shoulders[22] in the morning is in the back at noon and in the ribs in the evening. Winter comes and brings colds and coughs and catarrh. Now the head will not obey us[23]. Love of ease alone has robbed us of the taste of every blessing and the pleasure of every comfort. We take a hundred thousand pains over our food, but never find the flavour that poor people get every day in a dry loaf with a chutney of salt and chilli. Our sleep is always broken; day and night we try to sleep soundly for an hour or so, but at the slightest sound sleep is miles away. I am always amazed when I see that neighbour's life. One day, I remember, I was wandering about restlessly on the roof at night because of the heat; and what do I see but all five of the neighbour's children, one on top of another, with no bedding, no pillow and no fan, lying happily on a bare bed, snoring. I have been married six years, and, dust in my mouth, I have never heard the neighbour complain of any pain or illness. When the season is about to change, it is the rule that even perfectly healthy people get a fever; but, God be praised, the neighbour and her children do not. It is such poverty that the hearth is never lit twice a day, but look at the children, lively and strong. Tell me, how old would you say this little girl is?"

I: "About ten."

The teacher: "I was in my fourth month after coming here when she was born; she has completed six years, bless her, and is in her seventh. God be praised, what fine growth! Look, Mahmuda, she is taller than you."

Husn Ara: "That is very true. It is the same with the maids and servants at our house; they have eaten and eaten till they have grown so fat you would not know them."

Mahmuda: "Well, what is the reason that you people, the masters and owners of the house, with everything God has given you there and everything to hand, have no flesh on your bodies, while the maids, however much they steal, cannot equal the ladies of the house?" [TN: Literal rendering; the point of the comparison is that the servants are sturdier, but the exact sense of this last clause is unclear.]

Husn Ara: "Yes, it does seem that hard work has something to do with it. But tell me this: how could we start doing the work that is the maids' to do? In the first place we couldn't; and if we did force ourselves to do one or two jobs, our own inferiors would start looking down on us."

Mahmuda: "Don't ask about can and can't; nothing is as hard as a human being, and nothing is as soft. It is women just like us who grind at the hand-mill and do work that some men of the city could not do; and it is women just like us who find their own lives a burden. Never mind work, never mind labour: whatever habit you form, that is what you will have. And there is no reason for inferiors to look down on you; to my mind, if a person does work with their own hands while having servants, they should be all the more respected in people's eyes. How fine a thing it is to have servants to wait on you and maids to serve you, and yet not think it a disgrace[24] to work with your own hands. Look at the teacher: there is a cook, and there is a woman servant for the work upstairs as well; so many girls sit in the school that if she only said a word, all of them would run to do the job; but she gets up and fetches even her own water. How pleasing this must be to God: 'Look, I raised this servant of Mine so high and favoured her so much that I gave her fellow creatures to serve and obey her, yet what a good servant she is, that pride has not touched her; she thinks herself as nothing, just as before.'"

Husn Ara: "But what is a person to do if they simply cannot do the work themselves?"

Mahmuda: "I have already answered that: work that other people do, anyone can do. But never mind; even if the one to whom God gives wealth and riches[25] in this world does not do very hard work, there are still thousands of little jobs that they can do without any trouble. Not to stir oneself for such jobs, and always to depend on servants and attendants, is a very bad thing. In the first place one grows lazy, and the habit of ease quietly keeps growing; in the second, however small the job, a servant, however skilful[26] and however well they know one's ways[27], can never do it the way one can do it oneself with one's own hands to one's own liking. I have made it my rule that whatever time is left over from reading and writing, I spend doing some work or other. For two years I have been cutting out and sewing my own clothes with my own hands. I had become very practised[28] at cooking; for the last three or four months it has fallen off a little, but even so I am the one who seasons the meat, and whenever anything new is cooked in the house, I cook it."

Husn Ara: "Elder sister, can you cook too?"

Mahmuda: "Can I? Well, I manage to roast and fry a little after a fashion. By the teacher's kindness I have learned to cook one or two things fairly well; and it is not only me, all the girls of the school know how. All the girls have clubbed together; tomorrow the frying pan goes on the fire, and the things have come and are ready. We would have fried them today, but the teacher said it is very hot in the daytime, so get up early at dawn and finish the frying as the sun comes up. So tomorrow you can watch the fun too."

Husn Ara: "Can you fry *samosas* too?"

Mahmuda: "God willing, I will fry you *samosas*, soft and crisp, with thin layers, that even you will like. But tell me, sweet ones, savoury ones, plain ones, or filled with mince?"

Husn Ara: "Sweet ones." Mahmuda: "Shahr Banu makes sweet *samosas* that are simply marvellous!"
