# The Geography of Arabia and the Ways of the Bedouin

## The Geography of Arabia and the Ways of the Bedouin

Kulsum: "Arabia is a desolate country; looking at its map one sees that it has very few people; there are deserts hundreds of *kos* across, with no water, no trees, no village, no settlement. If Mecca and Medina were not in Arabia, nobody would even turn their face towards it. When people go to other countries, they go for some purpose after all: in one place there is plenty of grain, in another an abundance of fruit, in another precious stones are found. In short, there is something rare or abundant in that country whose need draws people there. In Arabia there is only the name of God; no grain, no fruit, no jewels, nothing, nothing at all."

Mahmuda: "Why, the camels of Arabia and the horses of Arabia are famous all over the world. Camels, of course, are found in Hindustan too, towards Bikaner, and in the country of Africa as well; but horses as fine and costly as those of Arabia are found in no other country."

Kulsum: "You are right; the horses of Arabia are very fine, but in the first place a horse is not a thing of such common need; and in the second, the people of Arabia do not even let their fine horses go to other countries, they are so fond of horses themselves. Certainly every year hundreds of thousands of people gather in Mecca from every side for the Hajj, and some religious people also emigrate and settle in Arabia. Its native people are the Bedouin, who have neither town nor house; these people are wanderers like the Kanjars of this country; instead of reed huts they live in leather tents, and they go about with their wives and children and cattle; wherever there is water near and fodder for the cattle, they settle; when water and grass grow scarce, they go and live somewhere else. Plunder and pillage are their hereditary trade; every year in the days of the Hajj they rob two or three weak caravans."

## A Short General Geography

Husn Ara: "Well, Kulsum dear, in what book did you read all this?"

Kulsum: "Books in which the cities and countries are described are called books of the science of geography. There are many books in this science, but lately Babu Shiv Prasad Sahib has written a book called *Jam-e Jahan-numa*; it is a very good book."

Husn Ara: "Is a short account of the whole face of the earth also well [illegible] by reading this book, but the account of Asia and especially of Hindustan is written in very great detail [illegible]" [TN: The text gives this to Husn Ara, but it reads as Kulsum's answer about the book; the line is damaged in the scan.]

Husn Ara: "Asia, Africa: I hear new words; I don't quite understand what they mean."

Mahmuda: "Let me explain to you. Just as in a house we give a name to each part, the bathroom[1], the water room[2], the kitchen, the wardrobe room[3], the upper room, the courtyard, the servants' passage[4], the awning, the stable, the house garden[5], the lower garden[6], the alcove[7], the hall, the closet and so on, in the same way names have been given to the parts of the earth. The part that is sunk in the water of the sea is called the sea or the great ocean[8]; and the part that is open of the water is called land or the great continent[9]. The great ocean too has been divided into pieces: the Red Sea, the Black Sea, the Indian Ocean, the Northern Ocean, the Southern Ocean are the names of these pieces. There are two great parts of the land: the bigger, the Old World; and the smaller, the New World."

Husn Ara: "What do you mean, new and old world?"

Mahmuda: "Nobody knew about the New World before; it is only about four hundred years since it was found that there are people there too. The New World is called America; it has two pieces: North America and South America. We live in the Old World; it has three pieces: Asia, Europe and Africa. In Asia there are Hindustan, China, Afghanistan, Arabia, Iran, Turan and so on; Europe is the country of the English; and Africa of the Abyssinians. That is the short account; books are full of the long one; if you look at a map you will understand it well."

Hajira: "Just give me that book with the maps." Mahmuda took the book from Hajira and first spread the map of the globe before Husn Ara and said, "Look, this is the picture of the whole earth."

## The Map of the Globe, with General Information

Husn Ara: "You said the earth was round; why are there these two separate circles like the stones of a hand-mill?"

Mahmuda: "Join the two together and fill the middle with earth, and it will make exactly the shape of the earth. They make a ball of clay and mark on it in their places the countries, seas, mountains and rivers; that is very good; it is called a globe. The teacher of the school at the Farrash Khana has borrowed our globe; if it were here you would understand well from it; but never mind, look in this map: the places enclosed by blue, yellow, red and green lines are land, and all the rest of the space that you see empty is sea."

Husn Ara: "Oh! There's sea spread out on all four sides."

Mahmuda: "Of course; I told you that about three parts are sea and about one part land."

Husn Ara: "Well, what is this thing made like a centipede?" Mahmuda: "Those are mountains."

Husn Ara: "There seem to be a great many mountains in America." Mahmuda: "There are indeed."

Husn Ara: "And what are these wavy lines?" Mahmuda: "Rivers."

Husn Ara: "Where is our Delhi in this map?" Mahmuda: "You won't find Delhi in it. The whole earth is in a span; there can't be room in it to write the names of all the cities, or the map would become such a muddle that it couldn't even be read; but look, here is Hindustan."

Husn Ara: "A big centipede is crawling here too."

Mahmuda: "Yes, that is the Himalaya mountains, in which lie Kashmir, Sabathu, Simla, Mussoorie, Landour, Naini Tal and other places, where the English go to live in the hot weather."

Husn Ara: "Well, what is this thing hanging down to the south like an ear-drop?"

Mahmuda: "The Lanka of the Hindus, whose story they act in the Ram Lila at Dasehra."

Husn Ara: "What are these coloured dots put in the empty space?"

Mahmuda: "Little islands." Husn Ara: "What's an island?"

Mahmuda: "Sea on every side, and high ground in the middle where people live."

Husn Ara: "At both ends there is no sign of people, nor [illegible: rest of line cut off at the foot of the scan]"

Mahmuda: "The two ends of the earth are called the poles: one north and the other south. Up to now nobody has been able to reach them; it is terribly cold; the sea is frozen with the cold."

Husn Ara: "Is the heat and cold not the same all over the earth?"

Mahmuda: "Not at all. This line drawn across the middle is called the equator; the sun's rays fall straight on it, and the heat is so dreadful that the sea, where there is sea, is boiling, and the land, where there is land, is baking like a hot griddle. The further you go from this line, north or south, the less the heat and the more the cold; until at the poles there is cold to the utmost degree."

Husn Ara: "That's a very fine thing you've told me; so the country of the English must be much colder than our countries, and Africa hot."

Mahmuda: "You have understood quite rightly; that is really so."

Husn Ara: "The Abyssinians are probably so black because of the heat."

Mahmuda: "People are of three colours: black, white and copper-coloured. The people of cold countries are white, of hot countries black, and the colour of the people of America is like copper."

Husn Ara: "As for heat and cold, our country is in the middle; oh, why don't the people of other countries come and live here too!"

Mahmuda: "Everyone likes the country they were born in; God has made their natures to suit it, and the things they need are easily to be had in that country."

## The Maps of Asia, Europe and Africa

Husn Ara: "Well, what other maps are there in this book?" Mahmuda: "This map was of the whole earth; after it there are maps of Asia alone, Africa alone, Europe alone, North America alone and South America alone. Then there are separate maps of all the countries in Asia, Hindustan, Arabia, China, Afghanistan and so on. In the same way there are maps of districts and *parganas* and villages and houses."

Husn Ara: "How is this? The map of the whole earth is small, and that of Hindustan big!"

Mahmuda: "That is a difference of scale. The map of a *pargana* is on a large scale, say one inch to a mile; if the map of a district were made on the same scale it would not fit in a house; so they make the scale smaller, one inch to four miles; and in this map of Hindustan there is one inch to fifty miles, and in the map of the globe one inch to five hundred miles. A map is a picture; making it smaller or bigger is in our own hands."

Husn Ara: "If they kept the scale of a *pargana*, heaven knows how big the map of the whole earth would be; it would stretch as far as the Qutb Sahib."

## The Benefits of the Sea

Husn Ara: "God made the sea for nothing; if the whole earth were dry, people could go about happily from here to there and settle wherever they pleased."

The teacher: "That is a very irreligious thing to say; repent. Nothing in the world is without use and without purpose, and all the works of God are full of wisdom and good sense. People have thought so much, but have not understood even a particle[10] of His wisdom."

Husn Ara (slapping her cheeks softly[11]): "Oh dear, I repent! Teacher, please tell me the benefits of the sea."

The teacher: "I will tell you two or three benefits that I know; but a human being is of such weak understanding[12] that they are unable[13] to understand the use of many things; that does not mean that a person should go and find fault with God's ordering because of the shortness of their own understanding. God's ordering is a touchstone for human reason; when reason thinks something contrary to God's ordering, that is a proof that reason is mistaken. If you doubt the benefits of the sea, listen: one benefit is that precious[14] pearls worth hundreds of thousands of rupees come out of the sea, which serve to adorn us women. In the sea there are hundreds of thousands of kinds of fish, which people eat with such relish; the fat of fish is used for burning, and indeed the oil of some fish is a medicine for many illnesses. In the sea there are fish so huge that you would be astonished to hear it; there is a kind of fish called the whale, hundreds of yards long and broad, weighing thousands of maunds, in itself a whole ship. Then great ships laden with goods sail on the sea; if so much merchandise went by land it would need great labour, great delay and heavy expense. Although the steamship[15] goes on the sea and the great rivers just as the railway goes on land, hundreds of ships sail only with the help of the wind, and if the wind is favourable they go hundreds of *kos* in a day. Are these small benefits? And never mind benefits, if there were no sea, nobody would be alive at all."

Husn Ara: "Ma'am, there are hundreds of thousands of people who have never even seen the sea, and perhaps never even heard its name."

The teacher: "When did I say that life depends on seeing it or hearing its name? I said that if there were no sea, nobody would be alive at all."

Husn Ara: "Please be kind enough to explain the reason to me."

The teacher: "The reason is plain: all the various kinds of grain for food grow by rain, and the rain comes from the sea."

Husn Ara: "Oh! That's why people say the clouds go to the sea to drink water."

The teacher: "That saying is wrong, but the rain certainly comes from the sea. Rabi'a, a few days ago you read about rain, dew, mist[16], the rainbow[17], lightning and hail; explain it in front of Husn Ara Begam."

## Rain, Lightning, Clouds and So On, and the Speed of Light and of the Air

Rabi'a: "Because of the heat, vapour rises from the sea and the rivers and from every wet and damp thing; and because the water of the sea is spread over thousands of *kos*, the most vapour rises from the sea. This vapour is called cloud; being light, it goes up into the air, and with the reflection of the sun the clouds look many-coloured to us. When this vapour reaches a height it meets coolness[18] and falls as rain, and sometimes because of the cold it freezes and becomes hail."

Husn Ara: "Rain is the vapour that meets the cold and turns to water; then lightning must be vapour that turns to fire. The coolness up there turns vapour into water; can't it put out this fire?"

Rabi'a hesitated. Mahmuda: "Nothing is without heat; even frozen ice has heat in it; and heat is produced anyway by rubbing and grinding two things together. When a star falls, the heat of the air is kindled[19] and blazes up; and from not knowing this rule people have been badly deceived: when the air sometimes blazes up like this in graveyards and burning-grounds and old buildings and gardens and forests, people think it is a ghost. Lightning is the heat that is in the clouds; and this too is a property of heat, that when two things are put side by side, one with more heat in it and the other with less, the heat will go out of the thing with more heat into the thing with less, until both things have equal heat. For example, put your hand into cold water and the heat of the hand will go into the water until both have the same heat; and that is why, after a little while, the hand no longer feels the cold of the water. In the same way, when one cloud has more heat, the heat rushes with force into a cloud nearby that has less; that is called the thunderclap, whose sound we hear."

Husn Ara: "In the example you gave of the cold water and the hand, we don't see any fire coming out of the hand; but in lightning there is such fire that it dazzles the eyes."

Mahmuda: "What wonder is it that heat turns to fire? Strike one stone on another, and you will see sparks flying plainly. Khair-un-Nisa told me that once there was a storm in her home country; the bamboos rubbing together set the forest on fire so dreadfully that the whole bamboo-brake[20] was burnt black."

Husn Ara: "Lightning falls on the earth too; what is the reason?"

The teacher: "When a cloud is near the earth, because it is near, the earth draws that heat to itself."

Husn Ara: "Aren't these clouds up in the sky?"

The teacher: "Mostly they are not more than a mile or two high; and on the mountains the clouds come right into the houses; you are sitting there when suddenly a kind of smoke rises up like mist; a little later, when it clears, the smoke is gone, and you are soaking wet."

Husn Ara: "Lightning is a terrible thing; is there any way of guarding against it? Whenever I hear the sound of thunder, I run indoors."

The teacher: "Running after you hear the sound is foolish; when lightning strikes, it has struck before the sound reaches you. The speed of light is far greater than that of the air; how could you have seen it, but in the days of the Mutiny we used to watch the guns of the Bara from the roof, and the flash of the priming was seen first, and a few moments later the sound of the gun was heard. It is exactly the same with lightning and thunder; pay close attention and try it whenever you like: first the flash is seen, and a little later the sound of thunder is heard. And as for guarding against lightning, which you asked about: yes, wise men have found a way for that too. Lightning was a harmful thing, but by the power of reason they have made even that useful. Have you heard of the electric telegraph?"

Husn Ara: "Yes, two or three times I heard my grandfather say that news had come by wire."

The teacher: "Look, the land of the English is five thousand *kos* away, but by the telegraph news comes in four or five hours; all that is the play of electricity."

Husn Ara: "You didn't say anything about guarding against it."

The teacher: "Metal things, iron, copper, brass and so on, draw lightning. In magazines they have to guard against lightning to protect the gunpowder; they fix iron rods into the sides of the roofs so that if lightning strikes it goes into the ground by way of the rods. And this too has been found to be a property of lightning, that it shies away from pointed things; that is the real reason for the finials you see on domes and minarets, and even on pavilions. I have a little book in which everything about the telegraph is written, and it describes wonderful properties of electricity; when you have read more, look at it."

## About the English

Husn Ara: "The English are sheer bundles of brains!" The teacher: "The whole nation is like that; if they were not bundles of brains, how could they have come from the ends of the earth and made themselves kings? Just read the history of England and you will see what these people were at the beginning: utter savages; they killed animals and ate their flesh and wore their skins; they lived in caves in the mountains; they had not even the sense to farm or build houses. The Romans ruled over them; it was from them that the English learned skill and good management, until they drove the Romans out of their country. Now these are the same English, and on the face of the earth there is no nation so wise and so civilised."

Husn Ara: "Until now I thought that God had given all people equal sense; but from what you say it seems that there is a special property in the air and water of the English country, so that the people there are wiser. In my book too 'the wise men of Europe' comes in several places; then what fault[21] is it of the people of other countries?"

The teacher: "Sense is indeed God-given[22], but it does not grow without knowledge; in the same way the body too is God-given, but its strength and growth[23] depend on food; the food of sense is knowledge. And it is a pity that knowledge has gone altogether from Hindustan, and what there is, is worse than ignorance: is there anything in Hindustan besides pointless hair-splitting and false poetry?"

Husn Ara: "Are the English great Maulvis?"

The teacher: "You should not use the word Maulvi here; Muslim scholars are called Maulvis, and Hindu scholars Pandits; but there is no doubt that the English know more than anyone of the sciences that are of use. By the power of that knowledge they have invented such wonderful machines, and more come every day, that the mind is amazed[24] to hear of them. All the work of the whole world is done by machines: machines spin yarn, machines weave cloth, machines grind flour, machines print books, machines play music, machines do the work of the blacksmith and the carpenter; indeed machines do things that people could never do."

Husn Ara: "Are their *mems* (ladies) as wise too?"

The teacher: "Certainly; the women too are all educated and skilled; and it is not possible that the men should be so capable and . . . the women unlearned and unskilled like wretched us. A *mem* lives next door to Halima; just hear about her. Halima dear, tell us."
