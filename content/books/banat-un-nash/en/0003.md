# Husn Ara's Bad Temper and Mischief

## Husn Ara's Bad Temper and Mischief

Husn Ara's temper had taken[1] such a bad turn that she was at odds with everyone in her own home: no respect for her mother, no regard[2] for her elder sister, no fear of her father, no consideration for her brothers. The servants complained[3] on one side, and the maids begged for mercy on the other. In short, Husn Ara kept the whole house in an uproar[4].

When Shah Zamani Begam came, you would have expected Husn Ara to sit quietly for an hour or two out of respect for her eldest aunt. Not a bit of it! Shah Zamani Begam had hardly got out of her palanquin when two or three complaints came one after another. Nargis came crying: "Begam Sahib, look, the little Sahibzadi threw a stone so hard that my eye was nearly knocked out." Sausan came and complained: "Begam Sahib, the little lady[5] said to me, 'Let me see your tongue, Sausan.' The moment I put out my tongue to show her, she punched me under the chin so hard that all my teeth went into my tongue." Gulab cried out: "Oh, my ear is all bloody!" The nurse shrieked: "Look, she hit my wretched girl so hard with a stick that there is a weal across her arm." From the kitchen the cook-woman called for help: "For pity's sake, somebody make her see sense. She is throwing fistfuls of ashes into the curry pots."

Shah Zamani Begam called out: "Husna! Come here." Husn Ara knew her aunt's voice and did come at last, but with no greeting and no salaam, ashes on her hands and mud on her feet; she ran up just as she was, all dirty and smeared, and threw her arms round her aunt. Her aunt said, "Husna, you have become very naughty."

Husn Ara said, "That witch Sumbul must have complained." With that she slipped out of her aunt's lap, darted off and pulled the hair of Sumbul, who had done nothing wrong at all. Her aunt kept saying "Now, now!", but she would not listen to anything.

## The Plan to Send Husn Ara to School, and a Short Account of the Teacher Asghari

Then Shah Zamani Begam turned to her sister and said, "Sultana dear! For God's sake get a teacher for this girl." Sultana Begam said, "Elder sister, what can I do? I have been looking for a teacher for months and cannot find one anywhere." Shah Zamani Begam said, "Oh, my dear, it is the old saying with you too: you beat the drum through the city for the child who is sitting on your hip. In your own quarter the younger daughter-in-law of Maulvi Muhammad Fazil is a teacher in a hundred thousand." Sultana said, "I never heard of it till today. Look, I will send someone at once."

With that she called the housekeeper of her household: "Mani-ji[6], there is some Maulvi Sahib living in this quarter, and elder sister says his younger daughter-in-law is very well educated. See whether she will take a post as a teacher, and if so, bring her here. We are ready to give her food and clothes and ten rupees for her betel and tobacco. And when the girl has finished the first part of the Qur'an and learned manners and good behaviour, then, God willing, we will make the teacher happy over and above her pay."

Mani-ji went to the Maulvi Sahib's house. She exchanged greetings with Muhammad Kamil's[7] mother and asked, "My good lady, are you the Maulvi Sahib's wife?" The maid Diyanat said, "Yes, this is she. Come and sit down; where have you come from?" Mani-ji: "Where is your younger daughter-in-law?" Muhammad Kamil's mother: "She is upstairs." Mani-ji: "May I go up to her?" Diyanat: "Tell us who you are and where you are from; the young mistress will come down here." Mani-ji: "I have come from Hakim Sahib's house."

Hearing this, Muhammad Kamil's mother asked after the health of everyone, young and old, one by one by name, and said to Mani, "What do you want with Tamizdar Bahu?" Mani-ji: "I will tell her when she comes." It was nearly time for Tamizdar to come down anyway, because Asghari came downstairs after saying the afternoon prayer and said both the sunset and the night prayers downstairs. When Mani-ji saw Asghari she hesitated to speak of a post as a teacher. In the course of the conversation she said only this much: "The Begam Sahib wishes to have her younger daughter educated. The elder Begam Sahib mentioned you, so the Begam Sahib has sent me."

Asghari said, "Give both the Begam Sahibs my very warm salaams, and tell them too that whatever little I know, good or bad, I grudge it to no one. That is why a person reads and writes: to be of use to others. And the elder Begam Sahib must know how many girls I used to teach at my parents' house. I should very, very much like to teach the Begam Sahib's daughter, but what can I do? The Begam Sahib will not send her daughter here, and I cannot go there."

Mani-ji did not mention pay openly, but she said under her breath[8] that "the Begam Sahib is ready to take on all the expenses as well." Asghari said, "That is all her kindness, and it befits her rank; but we poor people also live in her shadow, and God does not leave us naked or hungry. I am ready to serve her as a maid without wages; if she needs a paid teacher, she will find plenty in the city." After this Mani-ji asked about Asghari's family, and when she heard that she was the daughter of a *tahsildar* (a revenue officer) and that Maulvi Muhammad Fazil too was in service at fifty rupees a month, Mani was ashamed that she had hinted at employment for nothing. Although Mani had seen the households of Nawabs, she was astonished[10] to hear Asghari's polished[9] speech, and she apologised: "My lady, forgive me."

Asghari said, "Why do you put me to shame[11]? In the first place, service is no sin and no disgrace; and the service here is teaching Hakim Sahib's daughter. And then, if you asked because you did not know, what harm is there in that?"

So Mani-ji took her leave, and when she got back she said, "Begam Sahib, the teacher really is a teacher in a thousand. Seeing her face would make a person human, sitting beside her would teach humanity, if her shadow fell on you you would learn good sense, and if her breeze touched you you would learn manners. But she is not one to take service. She is a *tahsildar*'s daughter and the daughter-in-law of the agent of a Lahore nobleman. They keep a maid in the house; in the hall a white floor-cloth[12] is spread, a quilted mat[13] on top of it, and a large bolster[14] to lean on. They live very comfortably; what would she care for a post!"

Shah Zamani: "True, Sultana dear. You did send Mani-ji, but I never believed she would take a post."

Mani-ji: "But she is such a good person that she is glad to teach for nothing." Sultana: "By coming here?" Mani-ji: "Why, Begam Sahib, would someone who does not care for service come here?"

Sultana: "So will the girl go there?" Shah Zamani Begam: "What harm is there in that? The teacher's house is not so far that one has to go in a covered litter. Not five, God bless you, but six houses in between, and in the seventh you are at the teacher's. And there is a connecting door from one house to the next all the way from here to there. And what did you take the Maulvi Sahib's family to be[15]? He is the son of the first cousin, the father's sister's daughter, of brother Ali Naqi Khan."

Sultana: "Oh, so in a way they are of our own clan." Shah Zamani: "Well, God forbid they should be people of no account! They used to be very well off; since their nobleman was ruined the poor things have become poor, but even so they have always kept a maid in the house, and there are always one or two men at the gate." Sultana: "Very well then, Husn Ara will go there."

The next day the two sisters, Shah Zamani Begam and Sultana Begam, brought Husn Ara to Asghari's house. Although Asghari's things were those of poor people[16], thanks to her management and good sense the Begams were entertained[17] as befitted their rank. Two or three kinds of perfume, large four-celled[18] cardamoms, betel nut boiled and dried, everything was produced on the spot; nothing had to be sent for from outside in a hurry. At last the two sisters said to Asghari, "Please be kind enough to teach this girl with all your heart." Asghari said, "In the first place, what do I know? But the few letters I have learned through the kindness of my elders, God willing, I will not grudge teaching her as far as I am able." As they were leaving, Sultana Begam began to give Asghari a gold mohur. Asghari said, "There is no need for this. How could I take a fee for teaching from you?"

Sultana Begam said, "God forgive me[19]! How could we presume to pay you a fee? It is for the sweets for the *Bismillah* (the ceremony of beginning lessons)."

Asghari said, "At the beginning people hand out sweets for a blessing; what would a gold coin be for? Half a seer or a seer of sweets is enough to sweeten the children's mouths."

With that she made a sign to Diyanat, who brought out from the small room a whole dish[20] of sweet cakes. Asghari herself said the *Fatiha* (the opening prayer of the Qur'an) over them[21] and gave the first to Husn Ara, and handed the full dish to Diyanat to share among the children. Sultana said, "Well, you have put me to shame!" Asghari said, "What are we poor people worth? Whatever is here is yours too. My part is to teach Husn Ara; may God grant the day when I can face you with honour." So, after polite conversation, Shah Zamani Begam and Sultana Begam went away and left Husn Ara in Asghari's charge.

## Husn Ara Joins the School, and the Senseless Flattery of the Maids

To look at and to speak of, Husn Ara came to school alone, but a good dozen[22] maids came with her and a whole score[23] of companions. The maids' habit was to surround Husn Ara on every side every moment, even when there was no need, and when there was nothing else to do, to flatter her at every word and praise her at every turn. If she shifted her seat, they all cried "*Bismillah*, *Bismillah*!" If she sneezed, they shouted "Thanks be to God, praise be to God!" Mani-ji kept quietly reciting the *Qul huwa Allah* (the short chapter "Say, He is God") on her rosary and blowing it over her; the wet-nurse kept breathing the verse *Wa in yakadu*[24] over her again and again.

And if Husn Ara so much as raised her eyes, one began fanning her in a hurry, another stood up to wave the fly-whisk or a handkerchief, another said "May I be your sacrifice, take a betel roll", or "put a couple of grains of molasses-sweet in your mouth; it has been a long time and your mouth must taste bad." Another began: "I am your sacrifice, take a sip of sherbet. Her poor lips are getting drier and drier, they have cracked. To the oven with such reading and fire on such a school! Look at the girl's face, how thin it has grown." And she would rush up, crack her knuckles over Husn Ara's head to take her troubles on herself, and hug her.

That anyone on whom, like Husn Ara, such maids are set like a curse from God and such servants like a plague[25] should keep a good temper would be a wonder. Even an angel in such company, heaven help us, would become worse than a demon.

## Husn Ara's Habits

Poor Husn Ara too was caught in this calamity. There was no fault that was not in her temper and no vice that was not in her habits. When she went to school she took all her old companions with her: mischief, bad temper, a foul tongue, conceit[26], boldness[27], quarrelsomeness[28], envy[29], lying[30], backbiting[31], rudeness, greed, impatience, laziness, lack of skill and lack of good sense. Since the teacher herself, praise be to God, was the daughter of a well-to-do house and knew the ways and customs of the rich very well, she was not at all surprised to see Husn Ara's airs and the pampering of her servants; but the girls of the school got a fine show. Never mind reading or learning lessons; every one of them sat staring at Husn Ara and her attendants. Asghari saw that it was this company that had spoiled Husn Ara thoroughly[32]. If this company stayed with her now, it was doubtful whether teaching and training would have any effect. She said to Mani-ji, "Now let these people go and see to the work of the house. The girls of the school are lost[33] in watching them, and Husn Ara Begam's mind is getting restless too." Mani-ji was sensible, and at once she signalled to them all to leave; but when the maids heard they were to go they made a terrible fuss. One said, "Well! How could I have a moment's peace without the Sahibzadi? Could I sit at home?" Another said, "Mani-ji, I say goodbye to such service. I did not take service for greed of bread and clothes, but for love of this child; that is my pay and that is my reward."

What these servants wanted was to use Husn Ara as an excuse to get out of the housework. Hearing this, Asghari said, "My dear, to claim more love than the Begam Sahib is a claim and nothing more. It is the old saying: she who loves more than the mother is called a sham. And, God forbid, this is no parting and no farewell; the house is four steps away. You can see how short of room the school is. Your sitting and getting up among the girls will certainly spoil their reading and writing. You had better go now and see to your own work." Even then two or three objected that "one or two people must stay to fan the Sahibzadi and give her water." Asghari answered, "After all, we all do our own work with our own hands. If we do this much for Husn Ara Begam, our hands will not wear out." In short, Asghari pushed them all out by force. Mani-ji had brought the Baghdadi primer and the *'Amma* part of the Qur'an, wrapped in a brocade cover and tucked under her arm; as she was leaving she began to give the wrapper to Husn Ara. Asghari asked, "What is this?"

Mani-ji: "It is the Baghdadi primer and the *'Amma* part. Just look what beautiful writing it is." Asghari: "But there is no need for it at present." Mani-ji: "Well, what will you start the Sahibzadi on?" The teacher: "Nothing at all just now." Mani-ji: "If nothing at all, then what is the use of sitting in school?"

Asghari: "I do not know how to grow mustard on the palm of my hand[34]. Whatever is gained will show itself in a few days. It is not my way to teach against a child's wish. Reading and teaching are of use only when the reader wants them; otherwise, what good is something taught by force? In the first place, what is taught like that does not stay; and in the second, when the heart is unwilling, forcing it only dulls the mind."

Mani-ji: "True; but if teachers put things off until children want them, all reading and writing would come to nothing."

Asghari: "I do not say that all children read out of eagerness; but I have made it my rule first to create a love of learning in the heart, and only then to begin the reading."

Mani-ji: "Praise be to God! With eagerness, what is reading? What the unwilling do in years the eager do in months. But the Sahibzadi runs a mile at the name of reading. Only if God gives her the desire will it happen." Asghari: "Mani-ji, God willing, this same Husn Ara Begam will fold her hands, fall at my feet and beg me to teach her; only then."

So all her attendants took their leave, and Husn Ara was left alone with Asghari Khanam. In the first place Asghari was very shrewd[35] herself and understood Husn Ara from her looks[36] and from her ways in a short time; in the second, they lived in the same quarter, and she had heard about her long before. So Asghari knew all the difficulties[37] that lay ahead in reforming[38] Husn Ara. The one good fortune[39] was that, while Husn Ara's temper had all the faults in the world, it had one good point too: besides being clever and intelligent she was good at heart. Her heart at once took the impression of anything good; and if she did something wrong and was gently made aware[40] of it, she was convinced and ashamed, regretted[41] what she had done and tried to make up for it[42]. It was on this one support that Asghari Khanam took on[43] her education. In fact Husn Ara's nature was very good; only the faults that can come from being pampered[44] and from wealth had affected her temper to the utmost degree[45]. When Husn Ara came to school she was, bless her, in her eleventh year. And although there was not yet any great crowd of girls in the school, there were ten girls there: Asghari's sister-in-law Mahmuda, Zubaida, Amina, Rabi'a, Kulsum, Kaniz Fatima, Khair-un-Nisa, Hajira and Shahr Banu.
