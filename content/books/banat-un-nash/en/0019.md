# An English Family and Its Good Life

## An English Family and Its Good Life

Halima: "Ma'am, five or six months ago a *mem* wanted to rent the house adjoining ours (that one is ours too). The water-carrier's wife of our quarter is in service with the Mem Sahib as an *ayah*[1]; she brought the message. At the word '*mem*' Mother refused flatly: 'We don't let houses to *mems*.'"

The water-carrier's wife: "Take half as much again or twice the rent, month by month; you won't find such a good tenant."

Mother: "What am I to do with the rent, throw it in the fire? The house adjoins ours with only a wall between; the voices of the girls carry right through. Mirza'i Sahib kept begging for it for his workshop, and I wouldn't let him have it; if I let it at all, it will be to some decent family; otherwise it can stay empty for all I care."

The water-carrier's wife: "Lady, the Mem Sahib is a very decent person too; she is of another nation and another religion, but I swear by my Natthu[2], she is a very good woman; and by living near her you'll find it out for yourself; if you find anything different from what I say, cut off my nose and my plait."

Mother: "Well, there must be English people coming and going at her house all the time."

The water-carrier's wife: "Lady, the Sahib is in service at the telegraph office; he comes at nine at night and goes off to work at four in the morning; his daytime meal[3] goes to him there; and nobody from outside comes or goes. There are little children; the eldest daughter, the Missy Baba[4], is younger than your girl, bless her, but, dust in my eyes, she's about two fists taller."

Mother: "Even if nobody from outside comes and goes, the Mem Sahib herself goes out and about."

The water-carrier's wife: "Morning and evening she certainly goes outside the Kashmiri Gate on foot with the children to take the air."

Mother: "The Mem Sahib or her children will come into our house, I suppose."

The water-carrier's wife: "Never without your leave."

Mother: "Look, let there be no trouble; I'm frightened, I tell you."

The water-carrier's wife: "Lady, have no doubt; I'll answer for it."

So the Mem Sahib came to live there. For a few days Mother kept telling all of us children to speak softly, and had forbidden us to go up on the roof; and in all that time we didn't hear so much as a sound from the Mem Sahib's side. Onions were spread out upstairs; about four *gharis* before sunset I asked Mother and crept up quietly to fetch them; and what do I see but a table set out in the outer courtyard and the Mem Sahib and her children sitting round it on chairs, all of them reading something. Hearing my footsteps thumping on the roof, the little girl saw me, and the moment she saw me she said *salaam* of her own accord. As soon as she said *salaam* they all looked up; then I said *salaam* to the Mem Sahib too. The Mem Sahib returned my greeting very kindly, got up quickly, came and stood below the roof and said: "We have made the first move in getting to know you; you aren't displeased at that, are you?" When I saw the Mem Sahib coming, I felt like running away; but hearing what she said, I took a little courage and said: "Ma'am, what is there to be displeased about? It is an honour for us to know you."

The Mem Sahib: "I have something to ask; if your mother would kindly come up on her roof for a moment, she would do me a great favour. Give your mother my very best greetings and this message."

I said: "Very well, I'll go and tell her now." I came down and told Mother all about it; at first Mother too hesitated a little; but in the end she went.

The Mem Sahib: "After greetings: I have troubled you only to ask whether, apart from this coming up, our living here has caused you any other trouble; if so, please be kind enough to let me know."

Mother: "To say it to your face would be flattery; but to this day I didn't even know whether anyone was living in this house or not. Not even an Indian would have lived like that. Among us there's this wretched strictness about seclusion; that was all I was thinking of."

The Mem Sahib: "I am English, of course, but I was born in this country and grew up in this country. I am the daughter of a great man; both my mother and father were killed in the Mutiny; I was left alone and married; by God's grace I have four children, and I am bringing them up. And I know your customs well; God willing, I shall do nothing to cause you annoyance. In our Book[5] the rights of a neighbour are set very high; so even if I cannot fulfil those rights, I hope at least that you will not be troubled in any way because of me."

Mother: "Your living here is nothing but a comfort; but it would be no wonder if you were annoyed because of us. I have four children too, God keep them, but they keep up a row among themselves all day; I scold them over and over, I curse them, and when I'm at my wits' end I even give one of them a slap; but they keep me harassed all day. Real brothers and sisters, and they can't get on together for a single moment. Since you came here there's a little peace; I keep checking them at every word, but what difference does it make? It's impossible that their noise and din[6] doesn't trouble you."

The Mem Sahib: "What of it? They're only children; it's the age for play; they're bound to get up to mischief; it's their noise and din that makes a house feel lived in."

Mother: "I'm amazed that your children don't make a din."

The Mem Sahib: "They do, but not all the time."

Mother: "For God's sake, tell me some way too; I'm at my wits' end with these children. May God give no one such sorrow; all they do is fight. Because of them I've cut down going to weddings; people say, and they're right, what an unruly lot of children she has brought up; I'm put to shame for nothing."

The Mem Sahib: "It isn't such a great matter that you need worry so much about it; when they're older they'll come right of themselves."

Mother: "Is some other time coming for them to grow older? Here's my Halima, God keep her, in her thirteenth year; I keep telling her, you're the eldest, you're sensible, don't bandy words with the little ones; she teases them and quarrels with them. Somehow in these times blood has turned so cold; the young have no respect for their elders, and the elders no love for the young."

The Mem Sahib: "Do you give the children any work to do?" Mother: "What work? By God's grace there are servants and attendants in the house; their only work is to eat and play."

The Mem Sahib: "That is just the trouble. I have given each child so much work, according to their ability, that they have no time left over. All of us, young and old, whatever the season, get up at five in the morning; each one bathes, changes and has a little breakfast; by six o'clock I take them all out of the city to take the air, and come back at about half past seven or eight. As soon as we are back I say prayers with them all, and then give them all their lessons. At eleven, after hearing their lessons, I eat; after that some write and some sew; we never sleep in the daytime. At three we first have something to eat, and then the second lesson is given. At five we bathe again and change; I go out for an airing; I come back at seven; by then the Sahib has come; he hears all the children's lessons and looks at each one's work, and we all say prayers together; after prayers we have supper and go to sleep. Tell me, where is there time for them to fight? And if I saw them fighting among themselves, what could I expect: if they cannot live in harmony with one another, how will they get on with other people in the world?"

Mother: "Glory be! You have made a very fine arrangement; and that is just why you people are ruling. Among us Indians these are a woman's only tasks: she learns how to make two or three dishes, and sews her own clothes with her own hands; there's no custom of reading and writing at all; and if she can afford to keep servants, she sits idle like an *ahadi*."

The Mem Sahib: "Among us people do not learn skills out of need; rather, they regard skill as a source of honour. I remember something my father said: when he sent me home to England to study, he wrote to my uncle to enter me in some good school. My uncle wrote that the education in such-and-such a school was very fine and of the highest class, but that the fees were very high there. My father wrote that he had set aside two hundred rupees a month for this girl; whatever was saved from it would be put by for her; but if the whole of it were spent on her education, that would be better than saving it, because the gathering of skill is far more useful than the gathering of money. So my uncle entered me in that same great school, where the fees and my necessary expenses together came to two hundred rupees a month. When my father was killed in the Mutiny, there was no support anywhere now; I had no choice but to leave the school. One year was still lacking; otherwise I would have studied another year. The grief of my mother and father being killed and the shock of having to leave school in such helplessness, I tell you truly, both affected my heart badly. Although I left school unfinished, my abilities were talked of far and wide; and as soon as I left school, when people knew that I was ready to marry, hundreds of men asked me to marry them. Among us there is the very good custom that a marriage takes place with the consent of the young man and the young woman. I have read in books that consent is a condition in your religion too, but I see that it is not practised at all; you mostly marry off your children when they are too young to know their own minds; and that is why among you there is so often unhappiness[7] and discord between husband and wife. When so many people sought my hand, I had great difficulty in choosing: sometimes my heart was charmed by good looks, sometimes the lure of wealth tempted me, sometimes the pride of birth . . . was desired. I consulted the teacher of the school, who was as kind to me as my own mother. She gave me this good advice: that knowledge, ability and goodness are the great jewels of a human being; choose the one in whom you find these qualities. So after thorough inquiry and investigation I chose this Sahib. The Sahib is a great scholar; he took a degree of honour from the college, and he is so good that all the English here respect him as much as the clergy. And I am so happy with the Sahib's good nature that even the happiness of a kingdom seems nothing beside it. The Sahib's pay is not very much, only four hundred rupees a month; but I have not the words to thank him for the love and kindness with which he keeps me and the children. I have been married fifteen years; there has never once been a dispute between us over anything. He treats the children in such a way that every child is devoted to him heart and soul; when he comes home from the office the children are as happy as at 'Id; but none of them would dare do anything against his wishes; he does not beat them, he does not scold them, he is never sour-faced; but he has such a way that there is awe in his love and fear in his fondness. And it is through his arrangements that the children are improving as well as I could wish. Thank God, my sons and daughters all do as I tell them. My eldest girl's name is Miss Rose; you have put a lock on the connecting door, otherwise my girls are so friendly that they come and stand by that door a hundred times a day, longing to meet your children. If there is no harm in opening the lock, come one day and just see our house; you may rest assured that no stranger will be allowed inside except me and my children."

Mother: "God Almighty willing, I will certainly come one day."

The next day Mother took all of us with her and went into the Mem Sahib's house by the connecting door; and how clean and neat it was: not so much as a straw in the courtyard. The household things were set each in its place with such good order that among us there is no such arrangement even at weddings. Not that everything was rare and costly at all; most of the things were such as we had in our house too, but the things there had a quite different look. The wash-basin was scoured so bright that your eyes could not rest on it; a cane stool is nothing much, but its canes were shining, and over it a fine cover of hand-made netting: elegance in simplicity. In short, whatever thing there was, was a model of cleanliness; going into the house you felt you could spread your food on the courtyard floor and eat it. Seeing the things there I became convinced that cleanliness is a great adornment. The Mem Sahib's children were in their own rooms, one writing, one sewing; they all saw us come, but how could they dare come out without their mother's leave? The Mem Sahib sat us all down in the drawing room; we were looking about here and there, and Mother too kept glancing at the things out of the corner of her eye.

The Mem Sahib: "What can I offer you? I don't take betel; you may not like our scent; but there is no objection to dry sweets." She sent for a tray and set them before us on saucers. We hesitated.

The Mem Sahib (laughing): "Why, eat without hesitating; there's no harm in it; and in any case it is written in your religion that it is lawful to eat with us; and no Muslim in Turkey or Egypt keeps any such scruple; the Muslims of Hindustan have invented this new ruling."

Mother: "No, it isn't a question of scruples; but everyone has just had a meal."

The Mem Sahib: "What of it? Don't be afraid it will do any harm; there is medicine in our sweets too." So we all ate some very fine and delicate sweets. After that the Mem Sahib called her children; they all came; the Mem Sahib signed to them to sit down. Look at the children's good sense: each one came and sat by the one of their own age. Miss Rose sat by me, and the first question she asked me was, "What are you studying?" The moment she asked, I felt as if pitchers of water had been poured over me, and I said in shame, "Nothing." Miss Rose heard my answer in great surprise and fell silent. Then she showed me pictures she had drawn with her own hand, the costly and beautiful cushion and chair covers she had made herself, table-cloths, cloth flowers, stockings, bookmarks, mufflers, hair-braids, handkerchiefs, fringes and thread-work; never mind me, Mother was left amazed. Then the Mem Sahib took us into all the rooms; she took a book out of the bookcase and showed us pictures of her relations and of the fine buildings of the world and of famous and well-known people. We had gone meaning to sit for just a little while and come back, but only about four *gharis* of the day were left; then Mother said, "I have wasted a lot of your time today."

The Mem Sahib: "I have had great pleasure in your visit, and you have not wasted any of my time."

Mother: "But forgive my rudeness; I leave you feeling sad."

The Mem Sahib: "Is everything all right? Do tell me."

Mother: "When I look at my own state now, I feel very sorry; is this any way to live! Well, my life is spent; my regret is that I have brought up my children to be just like me."

The Mem Sahib: "What is there to regret? Every country has its customs."

Mother: "Fire take the country where there is no name of skill. In the city we are called very capable housekeepers, but the truth is that skill and good management end with you people."

So when we had taken leave of the Mem Sahib and come home, wherever our eyes fell everything looked mean and clumsy. As for me, I was so upset that night that I couldn't even eat my supper. The next day I said to Mother, "If you allow it, I'll learn something from Miss Rose."

Mother: "Well, child, Miss Rose isn't one of our own people, that we could rely on being acquainted; God forbid, she isn't in need, that she'd be tempted by money; how can I ask her? Look, I'll find out from her mother somehow." I went and opened the connecting door, and saw Miss Rose walking up and down in the courtyard; the moment she saw me she asked, "If you like, may I come to your house?"

Mother: "By all means." Mother did ask Miss Rose to come, but I was saying to myself, "I hope she doesn't come; if she comes, where shall we seat her?"

Husn Ara: "Why? Was there no place to sit in your house? I hear . . . that your house is a very grand house, and that you have plenty[8] of everything, carpets and furnishings, chairs, stools."

Halima: "We have everything God has given, but I had been to the Mem Sahib's and seen it; there wasn't one thing fit for her; where in our house was that cleanliness and brightness?"

Husn Ara: "You've just got the Mem Sahib's importance fixed in your head; otherwise, God bless you, you keep yourself quite clean and neat."

Halima: "Yes, you may think so; but if you had seen the Mem Sahib's house as I did, you'd know what cleanliness means."

Husn Ara: "Never mind; you could have had a white quilted mat spread for Miss Rose."

Halima: "That didn't need you to tell me; I did whatever I could in a hurry; but how many things could I hide? When Miss Rose came, I set a chair with its back to the kitchen; a little later the sun came round in front; Miss Rose turned the chair round and sat facing right towards the kitchen; and I was in such a state that I kept her busy talking all the time, so that her eyes wouldn't fall here and there. After a few words Miss Rose said, 'I'd like you to make me your sister.' I said, 'I'm not fit to be your sister; make me your pupil and teach me something; that would be very kind.'

Miss Rose: 'You should learn reading and writing in your own country's way. Miss Greaves, who is the inspector of girls' schools, used to praise (she named the teacher's) school very highly to me; but I'll teach you every kind of sewing; and nothing could make me happier than for you to learn something from me.'

I: 'Won't your mother object to it?'

Miss Rose: 'Object? You don't know her nature yet. She is my mother, of course, but in all fairness I have never seen so good a woman; you'll find out for yourself by living near her longer. For others, I know, she would perhaps not grudge even her life; and with you she has neighbourliness and acquaintance; whoever it was, she would be bound to be kind.'

I: 'Doesn't your mother ever scold you?'

Miss Rose: 'She has every kind of authority over me; but may God not make me such a disobedient daughter that my mother should ever need to scold me. Could there be anything more foolish in the world than that I should do anything against the wishes of my dear, kind, well-wishing and tender-hearted mother?'

I: 'You must have disputes with your younger brothers and sisters over something.'

Miss Rose: 'If I disputed with those younger than me, shame on my being the eldest! I am grateful to all my younger brothers and sisters that they respect me in every way; and I too hold them all as dear as my life and would die for any of them; and why shouldn't I? Is anything dearer than one's brothers and sisters?'

I: 'Do you brothers and sisters really never quarrel?'

Miss Rose: 'Never mind brothers and sisters; by God's grace we never even happen to quarrel with strangers.'

I: 'I'm very surprised to hear you; it isn't possible for brothers and sisters close in age not to quarrel.'

Miss Rose: 'And I was surprised to hear from you that brothers and sisters must quarrel.'

I: 'Why, quarrelling isn't, God forbid, enmity; just arguing and bickering.'

Miss Rose: 'Yes, I understood; but I'm amazed at what sort of brothers and sisters they are who bicker with one another.'

I: 'If the little ones, not knowing any better, are stubborn about something, what can be done?'

Miss Rose: 'Explain things to them gently and lovingly, not quarrel with them.'

I: 'And if they don't understand?' Miss Rose: 'They don't understand, or the elder one can't make them understand?'

I: 'That's the same thing.' Miss Rose: 'Then it is the elder one's fault.'

I: 'Well, when you want something to eat or wear, doesn't your mother ever forbid you anything?'

Miss Rose: 'A hundred thousand thanks to God that I have no need to think at all about what I eat and wear; Mother thinks of my needs more than I do; and I find that whatever I need and is suitable for my position, Mother provides it herself without my asking; so what is the use of my interfering?'

I: 'Well, have you ever happened to get angry with a servant?'

Miss Rose: 'My mother has taught me this: if a person, who is a sinner and wrongdoer to every hair of their head, wants their wrongs to be forgiven, they should forgive the wrongs of those under them; so what occasion is there to be angry with servants?'

I: 'That's why, in all the time you've lived in this house, we never heard a sound.'

Miss Rose: 'Thank God, ever since I can remember I have always found our house free of noise and uproar.'

I: 'Well, then, aren't the little children in your house ever beaten for anything?'

Miss Rose: 'If, God forbid, children needed to be beaten, we would think their faults were past mending; beating is the last punishment for children, as hanging is the last punishment for criminals.'

I: 'Did you learn reading, writing, sewing and stitching from your mother or from someone else?'

Miss Rose: 'A great deal from my mother, and a little from studying at school too.'

I: 'Has your mother never beaten you?' Miss Rose: 'Never.'

I (laughing): 'Will you beat me?'

Miss Rose (laughing): 'Certainly, but only the kind of beating I've had.'

I: 'When will you begin?' Miss Rose: 'Now.'

I: 'Do ask your mother first.'

Miss Rose: 'I've told you there's no need at all to ask her about such things.'

I: 'Even so, ask her leave to be on the safe side.' Miss Rose took a paper and pencil from her pocket, wrote a note to her mother sitting right there and sent it; on the back of it came this answer: 'If you can teach something to our neighbour's daughter, who is as dear to me as you are, there is no better reward for all the trouble you took in learning these things; and certainly, if we do not try to teach our neighbour's girls, our living here is utterly useless, and when we leave here we shall carry this debt on our necks. If by any means you can make them willing to learn, I shall be very happy, and in your efforts from now on I will share in every way.'"

So from that very day I began coming to this school, and Miss Rose very kindly teaches me sewing. The whole family is so good that I have never seen people like them. In only two months they have won the hearts of the whole quarter; the poor quietly get a great deal from them; if anyone falls ill, the Mem Sahib gives medicine free from her own store, and such care as not even one's own family would give. One day my younger sister 'Alima was unwell; the Mem Sahib sat with her from a watch before sunset till midnight; she gave her this medicine and that; Mother kept saying, 'Go and rest, it's very late,' but she didn't even stir; only when 'Alima had fallen asleep and was easy did she go. This I have seen only in her: when trouble comes on herself, she is very steadfast, very strong, very patient, and never lets it pass her lips even by mistake; but if she so much as hears that someone else's eyes are sore, she starts up and becomes restless."

Husn Ara: "You praise the Mem Sahib beyond all bounds; people generally think the English bad."

Halima: "They can't have had dealings with the English; it was the same with us; we met the Mem Sahib in fear and trembling, and for a long time we were uneasy in our hearts; when we had dealings with her, we knew."

Husn Ara: "Well, if she's so good, why does she go out of doors?"
