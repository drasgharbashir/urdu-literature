# Husn Ara Takes Leave of the School

## Husn Ara Takes Leave of the School

We wrote at the beginning of the book that Husn Ara was in her eleventh year when she joined the school; when, happily, she entered her fourteenth year, the family at Jhajjar began to press for the wedding. In this time Husn Ara had read the whole Holy Qur'an; and since it was her habit to recite[1] two parts a day, she knew it as if by heart[2]. She read and wrote Urdu fluently and without effort; her handwriting[3] too was not at all bad. The translation of the Holy Qur'an and the books *Qiyamat-nama*, *Rah-e Najat*, *Wafat-nama*, *Qissa-e Shah-e Rum*, *Qissa-e Sipahi-zada*, *Mu'jiza-e Shah-e Yaman*, *Risala-e Maulud Sharif* and *Mashariq-ul-Anwar*: so many religious books passed under her eyes; and besides these she had learned and finished arithmetic as far as the necessary rule of fractions, the geography of India, a few leaves of the history of India, *Muntakhab-ul-Hikayat* and *Mirat-ul-Uroos*; she would read Urdu newspapers without hesitation and understand them; and besides reading and writing she had acquired all the household skills that women need; and she had gathered such a store of useful knowledge that it was enough for the comfort and happiness of her whole life. What she learned through books, she gained a thousand times over from the teacher Asghari Khanam and the girls of the school in the course of talk. When the date of her wedding drew near, however much the family kept her from going to the school, she had grown so attached to it that to be parted from it for a moment was hard[4] for her. She kept coming to the school as usual, until only three days were left before she was to sit in *mayun*; then at last Sultana Begam herself came to the teacher Asghari Khanam. After greetings and blessings and asking after her health[5], Sultana Begam said: "Teacher, my heart was so set on you that every day I said I'd go today, I'd go tomorrow; but in the cares of this slave-girl of yours' wedding and procession I don't get a moment's leave. I'm not sewing, I'm not threading, but the work is something that never gets gathered up. At last today I set out by force, let the work suffer, and said, let me go and see the teacher, if only standing."

The teacher: "That's right, this is just the busy time; you troubled yourself for nothing; you should have sent for me; I too am busy day and night with your work. The suits I had sent from your house to be sewn and have their trimmings stitched on are all ready. At first I was afraid, since the suits, God bless them, are rich[6], and, by God's grace, are going to a wealthy house; what if these girls should spoil them somewhere? But no, out of love for Husn Ara the girls sewed with all their hearts, and stitched on the trimmings very neatly too. In the trousers of that *gulbadan* suit, which I had sewn and sent the day before yesterday, the *gokhru* edging on the gores has gone on a little too much; Shahr Bano kept saying, 'Teacher, let me unpick it and stitch it again'; I said, 'Never mind, leave it; unpicking will spoil the *gokhru*; be careful of it in future.'"

Sultana Begam: "I showed that suit to my *Mughlanis*[7] at home; they were delighted and said, 'Well, where is a man's fingerwork and where is a woman's?' I said, 'Why, what has a man to do with this?'"

The *Mughlanis*: "Oh, your ladyship, this suit looks to have been stitched in Miyan Ali Jan's[8] workshop; that's why the stitching sits so true; so what your slave-girls meant to say was that women's work, however fine[9] it may be, can't match men's work."

I: "What Ali Jan, and what men! This suit was sewn by the girls of my teacher's school, and they themselves stitched the trimmings on it." Hearing this the *Mughlanis* kept opening the suit again and again and looking at it closely, saying, "Since your ladyship says so, we believe it, but we have never seen such neatness and finish in women's hands." [TN: Sultana Begam is quoting her own words to the Mughlanis, hence "I".]

The teacher: "Well, I like the sewing of the other suits too. Then you should have sent all Husn Ara Begam's suits here; the girls would have sewn them gladly."

Sultana Begam: "And who sewed and stitched all the rest of the dowry? From the *Mughlanis* I took only the coarse work: there were floor-sheets, bundles, dining cloths, quilted covers, hair-ribbons, ornaments, cases, pillows, mattresses, quilts; things of that kind the *Mughlanis* did sew; or, yes, the night[10] clothes; the rest of the clothes for wearing came mostly from the school, and a few from Baji Amman's."

The teacher: "O God, may Husn Ara Begam have a happy fortune, a thousand more like these, and may she wear them till they're old and worn."

Sultana Begam (with a deep sigh): "Yes, teacher, pray; may God give her a good fortune. Daughters are a strange and delicate matter: rear them through who knows what troubles, bring them up, and then they are someone else's wealth[11]. What can I do, there's no help for it; otherwise I would never let Husna[12] out of my sight. Having made one[13] marriage alliance[14] in the city, I went through such trials that I swore off it for the future and pulled my ears; otherwise it's no fault of poor Hakim Sahib[15]; what offers he brought for Husna, each better than the last. I said, never[16]! Even if the world turns upside down, I will not now give a daughter in the city. Black be the face of a city where there's all this disgrace and scandal. So, teacher, now we have dealt with country people; our honour is in God's hands."

The teacher: "As for Husn Ara Begam, you may rest easy; first, the Jhajjar family are themselves great gentry; second, I say it licking the dust[17], you'll see, God willing: if within the second or third month after the wedding Husn Ara Begam hasn't become mistress of all the black and white[18] of the estate, hang me upside down. Don't you see any difference in Husn Ara Begam's nature?"

Sultana Begam: "The difference, thanks to your kindness, is that of earth and sky. The blessing of your teaching has made dust into elixir, copper into pure gold, a mote into the sun[19], a glass bead into a white ruby[20], a beast into a human being, and Husna, God bless her, into Husn Ara Begam. One great sign of her good fortune is that she should be the pupil and you her teacher. You have done all of us at home such a kindness that as long as we live we shall remain in your debt[21]. But ever since Husna saw the preparations for the wedding, she has grown somewhat frightened[22]. Even before, she didn't feel at home in the house; now her heart has turned still more against it; she neither eats nor drinks, nor speaks or talks with anyone. I meant to have her sit in *mayun* for a whole month; seeing her state I said, she's becoming worse than *mayun* of herself; her colour has gone yellow; there are rings under her eyes; look at her face, sad; look at her looks, grieving. I say, why should she worry so? At this age girls are so happy to be a bride."

The teacher: "Husn Ara Begam isn't foolish like other girls; God bless her, she is a very understanding and shrewd[23] girl; it must be some thought of leaving home."

Sultana Begam: "She doesn't care in the least about home; but the school is her life; I wonder how the child's heart will be consoled."

The teacher: "I'll explain things to her; and anyway, when a person is parted from those they love, there's bound to be grief."

Sultana Begam: "The day after tomorrow is, happily, the twenty-fifth and a Friday; if you permit, Husna could be made to sit in *mayun*; the relations keep sending to ask, 'Haven't you put the girl in *mayun* yet?'"

The teacher: "God bless it! A good date and a good day too; Husn Ara Begam had no need to sit in *mayun*, but never mind, it's the custom of the world."

Sultana Begam: "Then, if you would say so, Husna shouldn't leave the house; I've been telling her for days; she says nothing aloud, but the moment no one's looking she's off to the school."

The teacher: "Excuse her tomorrow too; the day after, God willing, I will send Husn Ara Begam off from the school. The girls want the school to give Husn Ara Begam a feast at both meals tomorrow, and to keep a night vigil; the day after, early, do come yourself and bring Jamal Ara Begam with you, and the girls' mothers and sisters will come too."

After this Sultana Begam took her leave; the next day the feast for Husn Ara Begam was given with great ceremony and pomp; the girls of the school cooked such dishes with their own hands as no master cook[24] could; at night there was a vigil; songs of Husn Ara's *suhag* and *mayun* were sung. And the girls also agreed that besides the suit the school would of course give for the *charhawa*, the *manjha*[25] suit too should be from the school, and that Husn Ara Begam should take leave of the school wearing that very suit. Early in the morning they rose, finished their prayers and recitation, had the school swept, and had clean, fresh carpets spread neatly in the halls; meanwhile the guests' palanquins began to arrive; by the time the day was about four *gharis* old the whole house was full of guests. Of the girls' mothers and sisters there was not one who did not come; all the ladies of the quarter came unasked to see the show; and it turned into a regular wedding[26]. In the middle hall, where a quilted cover and a bolster were spread, the teacher sat, and all the guests came and filled that hall. When everyone had settled down, the girls dressed Husn Ara Begam in the *manjha* suit and brought her out from the inner room and seated her right in front of the teacher. Then the teacher addressed Husn Ara in this speech: "Dear Husn Ara Begam, today I bid you farewell on behalf of myself and the girls of my school; today teacher and pupil and schoolfellows have all come to an end." Hearing this, tears fell unbidden from the eyes of all the guests, and the teacher's heart was so full that though she restrained herself, her voice showed her emotion. "But love and sincerity, God willing, will remain as long as there is breath in the body. Husn Ara Begam! I loved and cherished you like my own Batul[27] and Mahmuda, and do, and as long as I am in the world, if God wills, I shall. But the bond of teacher and pupil is such that I had to show that love with restraint. Sometimes I will have warned[28] you about your mistakes, and perhaps even reproached you for something unseemly; all that warning and reproach was for your good, your correction[29] and your betterment. When two people in the world have any kind of tie, even if it is only that of neighbours, fellow-citizens or fellow human beings, each has many rights over the other. The tie I had with you, I have said, was close to that of mother and child. Although I have tried as far as I could to fulfil your rights, it is possible that I fell short[30] in fulfilling some right of yours; so today, in this full gathering, I humbly ask your pardon for it; because I too am human, and a human being should never be proud that they have fulfilled their human duties in full." (On every side there were cries of "Bravo!" and "Glory be to God!", but with them there was weeping too.)

"Dear Husn Ara Begam, man is kneaded from fellowship; with two or four greetings people come to love one another; and with you there has been such intimacy[31] for three full years that we were together day and night. So today I bid you farewell with the same shock, the same pain, the same grief with which I shall bid farewell to Batul and Mahmuda, if God so wills." (All who were present cried aloud.)

The teacher (after restraining herself for a little): "Dear Husn Ara Begam! I don't want to speak again and again of parting and farewell, because it pains you and me and all who hear it; but consider, your leaving is nothing unusual; it is the way of all the daughters of the world that they are married and parted from their parents. I too once had such a bond with my mother as you now have with the Begam Sahib or with me; like you, I had an elder sister; I had friends like yours too; but in the end I came and settled in the new world of my in-laws, and did I settle alone? Thousands and hundreds of thousands like me. Perhaps you are thinking of being married outside the city; there is no reason for that; outside, the city, every place is not for you, who, God bless you, have every kind of conveyance; if you wish to come, then it is not. [TN: The sense of these two clauses is unclear in the text; they are rendered literally.] So, dear Husn Ara Begam, remember that the ties of one's parents' home grow weak[32] by themselves little by little. Well, is it so great a thing to persuade your heart to take those ties as weakened from the start? Husn Ara Begam, I hope you are not unaware of the great change[33] that is to come in your life; and you should be thankful that for the test you are called to you have had ample time and leisure. All that you have read and learned and heard will now be your guide and helper in that test. If one who, like you, keeps a store[34] of books by her should think herself alone, or think herself cut off from those she loves, that is her mistake. These very books are the companions of your solitude; and what companions! Kind as a mother, affectionate as a teacher, comforting[35], sympathetic[36], companionable[37], consoling[38], counselling[39], friendly, well-wishing[40] and faithful[41]. Dear Husn Ara Begam! Until now, whatever you have read must have seemed a story and a tale; but till now that tale was the world's story, and now it will be your own. The books you have, though they are few, are plenty to think about and to act upon; and with only your good in view I give you this last advice, that you go on reading and studying them as regularly as you used to read and study them when you were at school. From the day you entered the school I began to write down your doings; and all the debates[42] and affairs that have taken place between you and the girls of the school, I went on writing down in order. Now I see that they have made quite a good book. I have named it *Banat-un-Nash*[43]; this is the very book that I give you as a keepsake of me."

Saying this, the teacher Asghari Khanam took the book out of an embroidered wrapper of red satin: its binding thread of gold wire, its cover like a nugget of gold, written in the teacher's own hand in the finest *nasta'liq* script, so that the eyes lit up to see it; the frontispiece[44], the spaces between the lines[45], the ruled borders[46], the headings[47], everywhere work in lapis blue and gold[48]. First the book passed from hand to hand among those present; then the teacher put it back in its wrapper as before and gave it to Husn Ara Begam. Husn Ara, keeping her veil drawn, stood up straight as a cypress, greeted the teacher very respectfully and sat down. For two or four moments, while the book was being looked at, the talk was broken off[49], and then the teacher began her speech again:

"Dear Husn Ara Begam! In this book you will find exact pictures of yourself, and indeed of all the girls of the school." Hearing this, all those present who had turned the book over and looked at it well were surprised.

The teacher: "By pictures I mean that it describes your temper, your habits and your ways so fully that anyone who knows your story will understand, as soon as they read the book, that it is about you. This book will remind you of the habits whose correction cost me great pains. In reading this book you will feel that it is the school again, the same obstinacy at every word and the same surprise at every word. In reading this book you will find out how far the school's teaching has affected you; which habits there were that it broke; which misunderstandings there were that it corrected; and which good things there are whose merit it first made you admit and then made you take them up. Although outwardly you are parted from this school from today, in my heart and in the hearts of the girls of the school you will always be near, and from time to time whatever benefit you get from this school will continue; whatever new book we find, or whatever fine piece we hear or see, we will certainly make you share in reading it. Dear Husn Ara Begam, you know that I am a poor woman, but I thank God that I am content with my state and happy in my position; because, in the words of a holy man, I look at the sky and understand that some day or other the bird of the soul[50] must leave the cage of the elements[51] and, to the height of heaven[52], take its flight[53]; then I look at the earth and find that when I die only a few spans of earth will be needed for my bones; then I consider that I brought nothing into the world and will take nothing away with me; and there are thousands and hundreds of thousands of God's servants beside whom my state is in every way and every respect better by degrees. These thoughts have so affected my heart that, apart from a little lentils and porridge to fill the hell of the belly and some coarse, plain cloth to cover the body, there is nothing in the world that I think I must have and must take trouble to get. And yet God in His grace and kindness has given me much more than my need and beyond my want. With a little of it, out of love, and some money[54] from the school, I have had a suit made for you costing two hundred rupees. The school's money, you know, is not mine; it belongs to the girls, and it is gathered from the price of their work. So this suit is a school robe of honour[55], which I give you with the greatest joy; may God bless you in wearing it. In your dowry there will be suits of far greater price; but when you see with what eagerness, what delight and what love a few of us poor people together have made this suit, we all hope that its being included in your precious, fine and choice dowry will not look out of place[56]." Hearing this, Husn Ara again rose as before and gave her greeting.

The teacher: "Dear Husn Ara Begam! The day is well advanced now, and it is time for people to cook their meals; I don't want to keep you all talking much longer; but let me say just one thing more, for if I do not say it, it will be as if my duty of farewell to you were left undone. When girls, after they are married, are parted from parents, brothers and sisters, kinsfolk and relations and go to their in-laws, in that change God Almighty in His grace shows us women a model of the change that is destined[57] for every human being. The world is our parents' home, and the hereafter is in place of the in-laws' home. No girl stays for ever in her parents' home; sooner or later, one day or another, she must go to her in-laws; in the same way no one will stay in the world for ever; only the name of God will remain for ever. The girl who in her parents' home has learned skills and gained sense and discernment will be the darling of her mother-in-law and father-in-law, the favourite of her sisters-in-law and her brother's wives, and dear to her husband. In the same way, whoever in the world has done good works and good deeds will have honour and esteem in the hereafter, and such people will be masters of Paradise. But the girl who has wasted her time in being petted by her parents and has taken no thought for correcting her temper, mending her habits and acquiring skills, when she goes to her in-laws will be despised in her husband's eyes and of no account with her mother-in-law and sisters-in-law; exactly the same will be the state of those who waste the days of their life in heedlessness and carelessness; on the Day of Judgement they will be disgraced and put to shame. In the same way girls take a dowry with them from their parents' home; the dowry from the parents' home of faith is each person's own deeds, which go with them.

"Husn Ara Begam! I know that in these days all sorts of strange thoughts must be passing through your heart, about what is happening and what will happen; but raise your thoughts a little, and take your gaze a little further forward. If anything is worth thinking about and understanding, it is this: what the world is, why we have come here, what we are doing, and what the end is to be. Just as your days of living in your parents' home are complete, for every person there will be a day when their span of life[58] is over; and let us all together now pray at God's court that He give us all the grace to do good." (On every side there were cries of "Amen, amen.") "The parents' homes and in-laws' homes of this world last only a few days; O God, in that world we must stay for ever and ever; keep our secrets covered and do not put us to shame." (All cried aloud: "Amen, amen!") "O God, this Your handmaid, whom we call Husn Ara Begam, is about to begin the stage of the world's journey which we are all travelling by Your command; may Your grace and kindness be her guardian, Your help her escort[59], and Your favour and kindness her provision for the road[60]." (All were moved, and all said: "Amen!") After this the teacher rose, embraced Husn Ara and kissed her, softly recited some prayer and breathed it over her, went with her to the door and saw her into the palanquin, and the gathering came to an end. The End.
