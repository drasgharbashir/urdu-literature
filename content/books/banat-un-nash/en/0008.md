# The Beginning of the Tale of Masih-ul-Mulk, a Merciless Nobleman

## The Beginning of the Tale of Masih-ul-Mulk, a Merciless Nobleman

Among the forebears of Nawab Badal Beg Khan, a well-known Nawab who lives at Lal Kuan[1], there was once a certain Nawab Masih-ul-Mulk[2]. In name he was one of the royal physicians, but he had gained such influence[3] over the King's mind that all the affairs of the state were in his hands. Having gained such power, Masih-ul-Mulk ought to have comforted the King's dependants[4], looked after the poor and seen justice done[5] to the oppressed; but instead he behaved in such a way that in a short time he set a whole world complaining and a whole people crying out. Whoever you listened to had a complaint, whoever you asked had a grievance. Hundreds of men who, being servants for ten generations[6] and hereditary eaters of the King's salt, wished the King well with all their hearts and souls, were dismissed without any fault or offence. Except for Masih-ul-Mulk's own people[7], not one person was left whose pay had not been cut more or less, or whose pay, if it came at all, came once in six months. In the Hakim's time[8] it came to arrears of years, and even then such deductions were made that the man owed ten would barely get[9] six, and the man owed six four. He confiscated without hesitation[11] the rent-free lands[10] of widows, orphans and cripples. Complaints about all this reached the King; whenever he asked, Masih-ul-Mulk would explain to him: "Your Majesty, there is not a penny left in the treasury, and there are debts of crores. I say I will pay off the debt however I can; in two or three years everything will be in order. All their lives they have eaten Your Majesty's salt and enjoyed thousands of comforts thanks to this court; if they all bear a little hardship together for a few days, Your Majesty will be relieved of the burden[12] of debt."

Even so the King would only say: "Do not dishearten the people. Never mind if my expenses[13] have to be cut, but the servants have small means[14]; do not harass them. If the debt is not paid in four years, it will be paid in ten; but these people of small means will be finished by too much hardship. God forbid, if even one of them were to go, it would be hard to find another like him even by spending thousands of rupees. Every one of them is known, tried and tested. And look, do whatever you like, but beware of cutting the sums for charity. In the first place, what does that charity amount to? If you reckon it up, it is a mustard seed beside a mountain. But still, such as it is, it is very necessary." Not even a shadow[15] of goodness had fallen on Masih-ul-Mulk's heart; he was wholly without generosity, without care for the good of God's creatures, and without pity. The King's words had no effect on him at all. In the end the tyrant's life is short; the poor man's doom came. . . . . . .

Zubaida had read the story up to here when the teacher raised her hand and said, "Wait a moment," and asked the girls, "Now tell me, what do you think of the King and Masih-ul-Mulk?"

Rabi'a: "Both bad. Masih-ul-Mulk was merciless, of course; the King was bad because he gave such power to such a merciless man."

Husn Ara said angrily, "Heaven forbid! What a bad tongue the girls of this school have! They don't care whether it's a king or a minister; they just blurt out whatever they like." And turning to Rabi'a she said, "Look at your own face, and then look at you calling kings and ministers bad! At the very least thousands of maids like you must have stood before them with folded hands every moment of the day."

Rabi'a: "And what does that prove? Does being a king or a minister, or keeping lots of maids, give a person licence for violence and oppression?"

Husn Ara: "What violence and oppression? They gave orders to their own servants and their own subjects however they pleased; who would have dared to say a word before them? It's only now that they're dead that you speak up; while they were alive, if any of your elders were around, their mouths must have gone dry saying 'Your Honour, Your Honour'."

Rabi'a: "So in your opinion, kings and ministers may oppress their servants and subjects as much as they like, even kill them, and it is lawful for them?" Husn Ara: "Certainly; and what kind of king is it who has no awe about him?"

Mahmuda Begam: "Madam, don't be offended. If the King, for no reason at all, sitting idle, were to confiscate[16] your house and home, and seize every man and woman and imprison them, would you still say that the King had done right?"

Husn Ara: "Why should he confiscate our property and imprison us?"

Mahmuda: "Why, aren't you a subject?"

Husn Ara: "Oh, but there are subjects and subjects."

Mahmuda: "So what you seem to mean is that there's no harm in violence and oppression against the poor."

Husn Ara: "What else?" Mahmuda: "What wrong have the poor done? Haven't the poor got lives?"

Husn Ara: "Of course they have lives, but the poor can bear hardship."

The teacher: "Well, my dear Husn Ara Begam, if, God forbid, you became poor, would it then be right to harass you?"

Husn Ara: "No." The teacher: "That is a dreadful injustice, that other poor people may be harassed, but if, God forbid, Husn Ara Begam becomes poor, she is to be spared."

Husn Ara: "Even if a rich person becomes poor, the scent of wealth doesn't go for several generations."

The teacher: "How is it proved that all the people who are poor in the world at present[17] have always been poor? Wealth is a passing shadow; the rich keep becoming poor and the poor become rich. Not only in this city, but in the whole world, there can be no family that has always been rich or always poor. If there have been two or four generations of rich, there will have been two or four of poor as well."

## The King Is the Servant[18] of His Subjects, and His Powers Are Limited[19]

Husn Ara: "Well, Masih-ul-Mulk was certainly at fault, but what had the poor King done?"

Rabi'a: "I have already said that the King's fault was keeping Masih-ul-Mulk in such power."

Husn Ara: "When I hear the word 'fault' from your mouth together with the King, I can't help laughing."

Rabi'a: "You may well laugh, but not as much as I laugh to hear of Masih-ul-Mulk's power while there was a King."

Husn Ara: "He was king of the whole wide world; one thing did not reach his ears."

Mahmuda: "That was just the King's fault. He should have kept his ears so open that he heard the faintest murmur[21] of complaint and appeal from days' journeys[20] away. That is why people had made him king."

Husn Ara: "Listen to that! People had made him king!"

The teacher: "Husn Ara Begam, it is a pity you have not read anything. Until you can read, you will be surprised like this at thousands of things. All the kings there are were made by people. When there came to be many people in the world, quarrels and fights began among them too. Some wretches were so wicked that whenever they got the chance they would kill a man, steal his goods, or dishonour respectable people. Then they took counsel together and settled on this plan: come, let us make someone our chief among ourselves; let everyone obey his orders and submit[22] to him, and let it be this chief's work to settle people's quarrels and to guard the lives, property and honour of the subjects. That is what came to be called a king. The people's work is to obey him, and the king's work is to give the subjects peace, so that no one commits oppression or wrong. Well now, let the story go on."

Hajira: "Ma'am, I have a very long way to go, and the six-*ghari* gun is about to be fired[23] any moment; then the road will be closed. Please let me go."

The teacher: "Very well, put it off now; God willing, we will see to it another time."

Husn Ara was so fond of hearing stories that she did not like the story being put off. She said to Hajira, "Oh, do wait a bit, let the story finish; it hasn't even got back to where it left off."

Hajira: "No, my dear, it's very late; I can't stay."

Husn Ara: "Oh, then stay here tonight, or else come home with me."

Hajira: "Is this any time for me to stay behind for the sake of a story? My mother will be watching for me."

Husn Ara: "Oh, doesn't it upset you that the story's left unfinished?"

Hajira: "What is there to be upset about? If I want to hear the story so much, can't I read it myself?" So all the girls took their leave.

## Husn Ara Begins to Read

When Husn Ara was about to leave, she took Mahmuda aside and said, "Mahmuda Begam, how long will it take to learn enough reading to read a storybook myself?"

Mahmuda: "If you study with all your heart, four months, perhaps even a little less."

Husn Ara: "Oh, then start me from tomorrow."

Mahmuda: "Ask the teacher." Husn Ara: "I did ask."

Mahmuda: "And then?" Husn Ara: "The teacher said, 'What's the hurry yet?'"

Mahmuda: "The teacher can't be satisfied about your eagerness yet."

Husn Ara: "It's something like that."

Mahmuda: "Then be patient a few days." Husn Ara: "No, what I say is, I want to be able to read the storybook today."

Mahmuda: "Then I'll tell the teacher." Husn Ara: "Is there any harm in your teaching me on the quiet?"

Mahmuda: "What harm could there be?" Husn Ara: "That the teacher might be cross."

Mahmuda: "Not at all. And if that's what you think, why don't you start with the teacher herself?"

Husn Ara: "Girls smaller than me read books fluently; I'm ashamed to be reading A, B at my age."

Mahmuda: "Very well, I'll take you up on the roof and teach you there so quietly that nobody will even know."

Husn Ara: "Promise?" Mahmuda: "Promise."

Husn Ara: "Oh, and don't tell the teacher either." Mahmuda: "I won't." So, after all this talk, when Husn Ara was about to go, the teacher sent two women with her. The house was close by anyway, and she was there in no time.

Sultana Begam: "Oh, Husna! I thought you were staying there today."

Husn Ara: "If I had been sleepy, what would there have been to stop me staying?"

Sultana Begam: "It's surprising you haven't fallen asleep yet; otherwise it is your regular habit that the moment the sun goes down, you go to sleep."

Husn Ara: "I found out today that it is because I have nothing to do that my sleep keeps growing. Look, today I didn't sleep, and I didn't feel at all sluggish[24]."

Sultana: "What sort of work were you busy with today?"

Husn Ara: "There was no work at all, but the talk there is so interesting that you could sit and listen to it day and night."

Sultana: "Tell us something too, then."

Husn Ara: "Now it's very late at night, and I have to get up early. If I don't go to sleep soon, it'll be hard to wake at dawn[25]."

Sultana: "Oh yes, you, getting up early!"

Husn Ara: "God willing[26], I'll get up so early that you'll see. Nurse, you always say that when I get up the stars are out[27]; so you must, you absolutely must, wake me and sit me up at that time. Look, mind you don't forget."

The nurse: "I'll wake you all right; whether you get up or not is up to you."

Husn Ara: "If I don't get up, splash cold water on me."

The nurse: "I couldn't bring myself to torment you when you're fast asleep."

Husn Ara: "I'm telling you to wake me, so there's no need for you to worry about tormenting me."

The nurse: "You say so, child, but what bad luck would I have to bring on myself, teasing you first thing in the morning and getting myself a scolding[28]?"

Husn Ara: "No, nurse, I swear by God I won't mind a bit; do wake me, please."

Sultana: "But why do you need to get up so early? You'd do well just to get up a little earlier than usual."

Husn Ara: "Oh, but I've made a bet. Even if I don't get up, take me to school very early before it's light, asleep as I am." Nurse, look, I'm telling you again: you must, you absolutely must wake me, or there'll be nobody worse than me."

## Notes

[1] "The name of a well-known quarter of Delhi."

[2] "Kings used to give titles to noblemen, that is, the king would bestow some name of his own choosing; Masih-ul-Mulk is a name of this kind. *Masih* (the Messiah) is what Hazrat 'Isa (Jesus) is called; God gave him the miracle that any sick person he touched was cured. So the King must have given Masih-ul-Mulk this title meaning 'you are the Messiah of the whole realm'."

[3] *Darkhwur*: "access, influence".

[4] *Mutawassilan*: "those who had a connection with the King's court, that is, his servants and attendants".

[5] *Dad-rasi*: "to do justice and get the wronged person their right".

[6] "People who had eaten the King's salt from the time of their fathers and grandfathers."

[7] "Dependants, those with recommendations."

[8] *Hakim-gardi*: "the turn of the times when the Hakim held sway".

[9] "Were received."

[10] *Mu'afiyan*: "land from which the King takes no revenue".

[11] *Be-darigh*: "without hesitation".

[12] *Subukdosh*: "with the shoulder lightened of the burden of debt".

[13] *Masarif*: "expenses; plural of *masraf*".

[14] *Auqat*: "small pay, small income".

[15] *Partau*: "shadow".

[16] *Ta'liqa*: "to confiscate".

[17] *Bil-fe'l*: "now, at this time".

[18] *Khidmatguzar*: "one who serves, that is, a servant".

[19] "That is, there is a limit to his power; it is not that he may do whatever he likes."

[20] *Manzilon*: "that is, from a road of many days, as from a great distance".

[21] *Bhanak*: "a faint, thin sound like a buzzing".

[22] *Ita'at*: "obedience".

[23] *Chali ki chali*: "this is an idiom: it must go now, there is no time".

[24] [illegible: the note is not legible in the scan; *kasal* means sluggishness]

[25] *Tarke*: "early in the morning".

[26] *In sha Allah*: "if God wills".

[27] *Chhitke*: "scattered".

[28] *Bura dhara*: "a bad state, a bad plight".
