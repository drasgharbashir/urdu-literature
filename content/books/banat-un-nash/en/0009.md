# Husn Ara Begins to Get Up Early

## Husn Ara Begins to Get Up Early

The nurse got up at her usual time. She finished her prayer[1] and said her supplications, then went fearfully to Husn Ara's bed and called her. Husn Ara was the kind who, if you called her a score of times, would not so much as grunt[2]; and if she did begin to come out of her sleep, whenever she was called she would sometimes just stretch, and sometimes roll over from one side to the other. But this time, at the sound of the nurse's voice, she actually sat up at once. She tried hard to open her eyes and pulled her eyelids apart, but they felt as if someone had sewn them shut or stuck them with gum; and when she blinked them open a little, it hurt as if someone had filled her eyelids with chilli. But yesterday's promise and the treat of the frying were before her mind, and she held out her arms. The nurse lovingly lifted her into her lap and said, "Child, it is still very early; bless you, take another little sleep."

Husn Ara: "No, nurse, no, take me to school now."

The nurse: "Child, wash your face, have some breakfast, and then go."

Husn Ara whimpered[3] and said, "Oh Lord! Look, the wretched woman keeps on dawdling and won't take me; all the girls will be there by now." So the nurse brought her to school. Husn Ara had come along half opening her eyes; arriving here, she saw that in fact all the girls, big and small, were there, but one was opening her book, one had begun reading over her old lesson[4], and another was just sitting down with her preparation. At the sight of this whatever was still shut of Husn Ara's eyes opened wide.

Mahmuda: "Oh, the Begam Sahib so early! God bless you, you are certainly true to your word and firm in your resolve."

Husn Ara: "Word and resolve, my foot! In the end I came after everyone else."

Mahmuda: "Even if you came after everyone, getting up so early on the very first day shows great firmness. In that sense you are the first of all."

Husn Ara: "What about the frying?" Mahmuda: "Everything is ready. As soon as you have washed your hands and face, we will start."

Mahmuda brought the water-pot, the basin, tooth-powder, a mirror, a comb and oil, everything, and set them before her.

Husn Ara: "Well, really! Why do you make me feel guilty for nothing?"

Mahmuda: "We are poor people; we don't stand on ceremony, and we don't know how to. You may think it clumsiness if you like. We do every kind of work with our own hands, and you will see that we never quarrel among ourselves. Whatever the job, and whoever's job it is, we all do it together; one helps the other. And this is not for show or pretence; there is very true love among all of us girls, to each other's faces and behind each other's backs[5]; each is dearer to the other than a real sister. God gave us hands for work, and is bringing a water-pot and setting it down any sort of work at all? Do whatever of these things you can."

Husn Ara: "I can't grind spices; at the very first rub my shoulders will give out[6]. If you say so, I'll knead the flour."

Mahmuda: "Kneading flour is hard work too; in fact it is more labour than grinding spices."

Husn Ara: "Never mind, I'll do it." Mahmuda: "But why?"

Husn Ara: "There's a reason." Mahmuda: "Is it something secret?"

Husn Ara (abashed): "Well, after kneading the flour I can wash my hands and be done; but if I grind spices, the turmeric colour, that black mark[7] of disgrace, won't come off for days, and I'll be put to shame for nothing."

Mahmuda: "What is there to be ashamed of in that?" Husn Ara: "Not you, but if people see my hands covered with turmeric, what will they say?"

Mahmuda: "It's all a matter of how one sees it. Some think work a disgrace; others think it a disgrace to eat what others have cooked, like cripples. So, for all my explaining, the scent of riches has not left your head and will not."

Husn Ara: "The thoroughbred cock stands on one leg[8]: let life go, but not honour[9]."

Mahmuda: "Well, there's no compulsion. You sit and rest; whatever we are able to do, we, sitting here..." [TN: The sentence breaks off here in the printed text.]

## The Schoolgirls Fry Savouries Together, and Husn Ara Joins in the Work, but, Not Being Used to Work, Has Great Trouble over Small Jobs

So while Husn Ara washed her hands and face, Mahmuda first asked the teacher, "If you allow it, the things for the frying have been here for several days; it is cool now and early in the morning; let us all fry the things together."

The teacher: "Very well, but let Husn Ara Begam join in too." Mahmuda: "With all my heart."

Then they opened the store-room, took out all the things and carried them to the kitchen. One began mixing gram flour, one began shaping patties, one sat down to chop onions; in short, they all set to work.

Husn Ara: "Mahmuda Begam, give me some job too. It isn't right for everyone to work while I stand looking on."

Amina: "Why should you trouble yourself? We'll do all the work; you just watch the fun."

Mahmuda: "No, there's nothing wrong in it. Whatever the work, you only learn it by doing it. But what job shall I give you? Grinding spices, kneading flour, frying: there are plenty of jobs, and they'll get the better of you."

Husn Ara: "Elder sister, you're stubborn too; you only care about your own work. Somebody has to knead the flour anyway; will maggots get into it if I touch it?"

Mahmuda: "Not maggots, but you'll break its stretch[10] and ruin it. Can you do anything besides showing off about being rich?"

Husn Ara: "All right, give me some other job." Mahmuda: "What job shall I give you? You don't want to grind spices, you don't know how to knead flour; what other job is there? Well, there's some ginger fallen under the spice-grinding stone, two little knobs; take it out and slice it."

Husn Ara: "Yes, now you've given me a job I can do; you'll see what fine shreds I'll cut." Mahmuda: "May God prosper it."

Husn Ara ran and began lifting the grinding stone. The stone was heavy; Husn Ara made an effort and raised it about a span, but in the end she couldn't hold it; it slipped, and when it slipped it fell on her hand. Husn Ara screamed. All the girls ran; when they got there they found Husn Ara sitting with her hand under the stone, her face pale and trembling all over. Quickly they lifted the stone off; when they looked at her hand it had been crushed, but the ground was damp and soft[11], so she was not hurt.

Halima: "Well, Begam Sahib, you're very faint-hearted; you screamed so that we were all frightened out of our wits." Husn Ara: "Have you eyes in your face or not? If such a big stone had fallen on your hand, you'd know."

Halima: "Why should it fall at all?" Husn Ara: "Well I never! One wasn't enough, now there are two[12]. At least I lifted it a span; if you can so much as budge it, I'll bow to you a hundred times."

Halima: "Really?" Husn Ara: "Really."

## A Brief Mention of the Science of Mechanics

Halima picked up a pointed stick from beside the hearth, wedged the thin end under the stone, and the moment she raised the other end, the stone toppled over to the other side.

Husn Ara: "Sister, that was marvellous!"

Mahmuda: "What's marvellous about it? There are thousands of things of this kind in the science of mechanics. Wisdom[13] is a great thing; by the power of wisdom one person alone can lift a load of thousands of maunds and throw it away like a straw; what is a grinding stone?"

Husn Ara: "Now you all get on with your own work; I'll slice the ginger."

Mahmuda: "Leave it; some other girl will slice it; your hand must be hurting too."

Husn Ara: "No, now I'm going to slice it whatever happens." Husn Ara peeled one knob with the kitchen knife and found the knife blunt. What did she do but take out Mahmuda's Rodgers[14] knife from her pen-case and start peeling the ginger. In the first place the juice of the ginger ruined the knife's temper; in the second, the knife was sharp and the ginger soft; three or four times the knife went chk, chk into her hand, and on top of that the ginger juice got in, and it stung like chilli. But Husn Ara hid it out of shame, and the ginger did not get sliced well either.

When she brought the sliced ginger, a redness glinted[15] in it. Mahmuda looked and said, "Oh, why is this ginger so red? It hasn't gone rotten, has it?" When she washed it, it came out nice and white. Then she suspected that Husn Ara might have cut her hand somewhere. In alarm she said, "Let me see your hand." Husn Ara hesitated a little and then showed it, and it turned out there was not a finger without two or three scratches.

Mahmuda: "Oh dear, what have you done? What knife did you slice the ginger with?"

Husn Ara: "The knife you make your pens with."

Mahmuda: "Does anyone cut vegetables with a penknife? That is why I was afraid to give you work. Look, you've gone and wounded your hand."

Husn Ara: "Never mind; what does a hand matter, it'll get better; but how ugly the knife has become; how will it be put right?"

Mahmuda: "Bless you, never mind the wretched knife; if it's gone, it's gone. Quickly soak a cloth in water and wrap it round your fingers, and for God's sake go and sit in the schoolroom."

Husn Ara: "Oh, but I'm going to work." Mahmuda: "Do you want to make the teacher cross? Certainly not[16]; I'm not going to let you touch anything now." Husn Ara: "Now I'll be very careful and ask about everything before I do it. Please, tell me something." Husn Ara insisted so much that Mahmuda could do nothing, and in the end she said, "All right, light the fire and heat the ghee till it crackles." Husn Ara thought she had got a very easy job. Quickly she filled the hearth with sticks and cow-dung cakes, struck a match and began to blow. She blew and blew, but would the fire catch? Her face went red[17], water streamed from her nose and eyes, the whole house was filled with billows of smoke, but the sticks took no notice at all. When the girls had got everything ready, Mahmuda asked, "Well, Begam Sahib, what does the ghee say?"

Husn Ara: "The wretched sticks are so wet that there's no heat at all."

Mahmuda: "Well, Diyanat[18], I told you that it's the rainy season, so look and bring dry wood; and you've gone and brought wet wood after all."

Diyanat: "Ma'am, the wood is so dry that not even the damp air of the rains has touched it; I had it taken out from the middle of the pile in front of me. For two days the food has been cooked with this same wood; it burns so fiercely that you don't even need to blow."

## Husn Ara Spoils the Work Herself and Is Angry with the Maid for Nothing

In the first place it was the first time Husn Ara had ever had to blow on a hearth; and on top of that[19], she had struggled for a whole hour and the fire would not catch; so she was sitting there abashed and burning with vexation[20]. When the maid Diyanat spoke against her, she flew into an even worse rage[21] and said, "You lying wretch, just come and use your blasted eyes and see whether they're wet or not! I've been wearing my brains out for ages; are these what you call dry sticks? If you weren't the maid of this house just now, I'd beat you flat[23] with these very logs[22], you carcass."

The maid Diyanat trembled with anger at Husn Ara's foul words and wanted to give her a crushing[24] answer, but the teacher stopped her with a sign. The girls too were deeply offended by what Husn Ara said, and they were on the point of leaving the frying and going off. Mahmuda had understood the meaning of the teacher's sign; she held the girls back with a hint[25], and herself went up to the hearth and said, "Let me just see what the wood is like." She saw that Husn Ara had crammed the sticks right inside and the hearth was stuffed full of ash[26]. Mahmuda pulled out all the sticks, first cleared out the ash, then laid four sticks crosswise one over another, put the kindling at the outer side of the hearth, and she had hardly blown on it once, just a little, when the fire blazed up.

Husn Ara: "That was marvellous!" Mahmuda laughed and said, "Is there anything marvellous about lighting a wretched fire? But that is why I said that no job comes to you without doing it. You crammed the sticks in so tight that no air could get through; how could the fire burn? It is well known that the lighter the kindling, the quicker it catches."

Husn Ara: "I didn't know that trick."

Mahmuda: "Of course; a person is bound to be helpless at work they have never done. But you did one very wrong thing."

Husn Ara: "What? I haven't burned any of my clothes, have I?" With that she began looking at her clothes.

Mahmuda: "It's all right; you didn't burn your own clothes, you burned someone else's heart."

Husn Ara: "You must mean my scolding the maid a little."

Mahmuda: "For God's sake, forgive me! First tell me, was your anger wrong or not? The fault was your own; you couldn't even light the fire yourself, and the poor maid was disgraced for nothing."

Husn Ara: "That much was certainly my fault, but what need was there for the maid to speak up in between?"

Mahmuda: "The maid didn't speak to you; I asked her and she answered."

Husn Ara: "Even so, it wasn't right for her to contradict[27] me."

Mahmuda: "She certainly didn't know that she ought to support[28] you even when you are in the wrong."

Husn Ara: "Why, doesn't she know I'm the daughter of a rich family?" Mahmuda: "Perhaps she does."

[5] *Hazir-o-gha'ib*: "to one's face and behind one's back".

[6] "Her shoulders will tire and be useless."

[7] "A mark of black."

[8] "A thoroughbred cock usually stands on one leg; that is, a person of good birth is firm in their ways and their word."

[9] *An*: "one's ways, one's honour".

[10] *Loch*: "stickiness, elasticity".

[11] *Poli*: "soft".

[12] "This is a Persian proverb (*yak na shud du shud*); literally, 'not one but two'; it is said where one is surprised."

[13] *Hikmat*: "wisdom".

[14] "The name of a well-known cutler in the English homeland, who makes matchless steel tools." [TN: Rodgers of Sheffield.]

[15] *Jhilmilati*: "showed through".

[16] *Hasha*: "certainly not".

[17] *Tamtama*: "went red".

[18] "The name of the maid of Mahmuda's household, who used to bring in the shopping from outside."

[19] *Turra*: "the sense is 'more than that'; a *turra* is the end of a turban".

[20] *Jali bhuni*: "meaning angry".

[21] *Ag-bagula*: "in idiom it is used for 'furious'".

[22] *Chailon*: "split logs of wood".

[23] "That is, I'd lay you flat on the ground."

[24] *Dandan-shikan*: "tooth-breaking, that is, sharp".

[25] *Kinaya*: "a hint".
