# Mahmuda Explains to Husn Ara the Saying "The Richest Are the Neediest"

## Mahmuda Explains to Husn Ara the Saying "The Richest Are the Neediest"

Mahmuda: "Does a dependent person have horns on their head? What greater dependence could there be than that you cannot get through a single day without servants? Let me ask you: if there were no cook, who would cook your food? If there were no maids, who would give you water? Who would wash your face? Who would fan you? Who would pick things up and hand them to you? Who would put out your bed? Who would make up the bedding? Who would sweep the house? Those are the everyday jobs. Food, clothes, pots and pans, jewellery, and all the things you need, small or large, down to the clay cup you drink water from, your comb, your needle, your kohl-stick: did you make them with your own hands, or did other people make them for you? And still you say, 'God forbid, why should we ever depend on anyone!'" [TN: The heading quotes a Persian half-line, "ananki ghani-tarand muhtaj-tarand", "those who are richest are the most in need".]

Husn Ara: "Of course other people make all the things we need, and other people wait on us too; but does anyone give us anything for nothing, and does anyone wait on us without being paid? We spend money on everything and on every job. For greed of money people come running of their own accord with their goods, and turn up to serve us without being called. If you have money you can get everything in the world sitting at home, and as for servants, you can hire one in the morning and another in the evening. What I know is that wealth is a great thing; the one who has wealth depends on no one, and the whole world depends on them."

Mahmuda: "Oh, Begam Sahib, you are making a big mistake. Tell me, if people did not value your wealth and nobody wanted[1] money, what would you do then?"

At this Husn Ara fell silent, and when she had thought, what she said was this: "In that case what could one do except lie down and die? We cannot do any work. And suppose I forced myself, got up and fetched my own water, and made my bed with my own hands; even then cooking is impossible. And granted that by struggling hard I cooked some simple food, since I have heard that Mother knows how to boil vermicelli and plain rice; still, there are a thousand other necessary things. Who will weave cloth? Who will make jewellery? But can it really happen that wealth is not valued and money not wanted?"

Mahmuda: "Of course it can. A long time ago the teacher taught me a book, and it said that at the beginning of the world, for a very long time, there was no such thing at all as gold coins, rupees or pice. In those days people knew nothing of farming, nor of how, as now, people grow every kind of grain and all kinds of vegetables, fruits and flowers from the earth by their labour. They killed fish from the sea and animals from the forest and filled their bellies with their flesh; or they ate, like animals, the greens and leaves that spring up of themselves in the forest. These fine, showy clothes that are so cheap nowadays that even every poor person can have them, nobody had even heard the name of before. They covered their bodies with the skins of animals and the leaves of the *dhak* tree and the like, and instead of grand palaces they took shelter from rain, cold and heat in the shade of trees and in caves in the mountains. As the world grew older, people went on inventing new trades and new things for their comfort. It was not possible for one person to do every kind of work alone and make every kind of thing himself; so one took up one trade and another took up another. One began to farm, one became a blacksmith, one a carpenter, one a goldsmith, one a weaver, one a shoemaker. The idea was that the farmer should grow grain for everyone to eat; the blacksmith should make iron things such as knives and scissors; the carpenter wooden things such as the plough, the bed, the stool and the chair; the goldsmith should make jewellery; the weaver should weave every kind of cloth; and they should exchange[2] their needs and their goods among themselves. For a time[3] the world went on like this without money or coin, but in the end difficulties began, which the writer of the book described like this. Suppose, for example, that the shoemaker needed cloth, and he made a very stylish[4] pair of shoes and took them to the weaver: grained leather at the instep, a turned-down toe, a stiff heel, the uppers of shagreen, high sides, a well-cured sole, back-stitched seams[5]. And he said, 'Just look, Shaikh-ji, what shoes I have made and brought you! Walk in the mud, run on the paved road; the sole will not wear out and the shape will not spoil. Not a scrap of stuffing[6] in them. If they last less than a year, throw them back at my head. But I need a bolt of coarse cloth, eight-hundred[7] if not six-hundred, three-quarters of a yard wide[8].' The weaver said, 'For your pair of shoes[9], here is a bolt just as you want it. The yarn is round and the reed[10] is close; it is woven tight, beaten well, without a trace of starch[11]. But the first pair of shoes you made me is still going[12].' Shoemaker: 'Why, Shaikh-ji, a three-year-old pair of shoes still going!' Weaver: 'Why not? I sit at my loom-pit[13] all day, and once a week perhaps I happen to go to the market[14]; what wear[15] do the shoes get? And besides, brother, I am a poor man; I tread lightly.' The poor shoemaker came away disappointed and went to the goldsmith: 'Well, Lala, do you need shoes?'

Goldsmith: "Yes, brother, you have come at the right moment. I have been going about barefoot for ten days; and in exchange I will make you jewellery such as nobody in the whole community has."

Shoemaker: "Oh, Shah-ji, what have the likes of us to do with jewellery! Look at me, going about in rags. At home the children haven't even got caps. My wife is worn out with patching and darning. It's cloth I need."

Goldsmith: "If you need cloth, go to Shaikh Namazi[16]."

Shoemaker: "I went; he has shoes already."

Goldsmith: "Come along, let's see whether Shaikh Namazi wants some jewellery made. I heard he was about to marry off his daughter. Then I'll make him jewellery, you give me the shoes, and I'll take the bolt from him and give it to you."

Now the goldsmith and the shoemaker both went back to the weaver.

Goldsmith: "Shaikh-ji, tell us, when are you marrying off your daughter?"

Weaver: "Chaudhri, that match has fallen through." Goldsmith: "Why?" Weaver: "The boy turned out very bad: a thief, a gambler, and he drinks *bhang*[17]." Goldsmith: "Do you want any jewellery made?"

Weaver: "Not just now; when another match is settled, we'll see."

So once again the poor shoemaker's shoes stayed on his hands[18]. When everyone began to meet with such difficulties, they all agreed together that exchanging goods for goods was no good; let a thing be fixed that everyone would take in exchange for anything, which the shoemaker would get for the shoes he made, the goldsmith for the jewellery he wrought, and the weaver for the bolt he wove. Then coin came into use. At first the coin was of iron, and so heavy that a hundred rupees' worth was perhaps a cartload. Then coins of copper and silver and gold came into use. They say that at one time a leather rupee was current, and even that had a nail of gold in it. Now the English have set up such a system that they issue paper money: one sheet of paper is worth ten, a hundred, a thousand, a hundred thousand rupees, and you can change it wherever you like for as many rupees as are written on it, with no discount and no commission. So money is of no use in itself: you do not eat it like *nan-khatai*[19], and you do not make a necklace of it to wear. But you can certainly get whatever thing you want in exchange for money. So in reality it is the thing that is wanted, and money becomes a means of getting it and bringing it to hand. That is the truth about the money that the rich and wealthy are so proud of."

Husn Ara: "What a good thing you have told me! But tell me this: when money can be exchanged for anything, then the one who has money is in a way the owner of everything, and everything is in their power; so money must be a thing of great value and worth[20], and however proud and conceited the people with money are, it is all right and proper."

Mahmuda: "I see no reason for conceit. Money is certainly the equivalent of a thing, but it cannot itself do the work of that thing. Suppose, for example, that we need a pair of shoes; there are two cases. In one, shoes were needed and the shoes are here; in the other, the shoes are not here, but there is money with which we can buy shoes. Think about it: these two are not the same at all. The person with money still has the need to take the money to the bazaar and buy the shoes. Suppose the shoes were not to be had, or were to be had but the price could not be agreed; would the person with money be helpless or not? And this too is worth thinking about: when the person with money goes to buy shoes, they need the shoes; but the person with the shoes does not really need the money; rather, they need the thing on which they will spend the price of the shoes. So the person with money is more in need, and if not more, then at least equal[21] to the person with the shoes. Then what is there for them to be proud of? One wants one thing, the shoes, and the other wants another thing, the money."

Husn Ara: "But for money you can get things at any time."

Mahmuda: "That is a mistake. It often happens that people are ready to give two pice in place of one and still the thing cannot be had. My mother sometimes tells of the days of the Mutiny[22], when everyone was fleeing to Sultan-ji[23]. They looked for a seer of flour for a rupee and could not find it. The men went about all day with rupees in their hands and in the evening gave up and came back empty-handed. Because of the Mutiny no supplies at all were coming in from outside. The villagers who had supplies said, 'What shall we do with money? If there is a little grain in the house, at least it will keep our children alive.'"

Husn Ara: "If that sort of thing happened, then of course money would be quite useless; but is there a Mutiny every day? Heaven knows what that was all about. Now, whoever has wealth is comfortable[24]."

## Notes

[1] *Khwahan*: "wanting; that is, if they had no need of money".

[2] *Mubadala*: "exchange".

[3] *Chande*: "for a few days".

[4] *Tarahdar*: "well-shaped".

[5] *Dokht*: "seam, stitching".

[6] "Some people stuff bits of rug or sacking into the soles of shoes; by *bhara'o* these bits are meant here."

[7] "Cloth with eight hundred threads in the warp is called eight-hundred, and with six hundred, six-hundred."

[8] *Panha*: "broad, wide".

[9] *Sar*: "in exchange for".

[10] "The *rachh* is a weaver's tool through which the warp threads are run."

[11] *Mandi*: "starch".

[12] *Bani*: "is still there".

[13] "The pit in which the weaver sits with his feet hanging down while he weaves is called the *kargah* or *kargah*."

[14] *Painth*: "market".

[15] *Zad*: "knocks, rubbing".

[16] "Weavers, poor fellows, are usually regular at prayer (*namazi*), so this name for the weaver is very [illegible: the rest of the note is cut off at the foot of the scanned page]".

[17] "*Bhang*, correctly *bang*" (hemp).

[18] *Ainḍ*: "that is, they did not sell".

[19] "A kind of sweet biscuit."

[20] "*Qadr* and *manzilat* both have the same meaning."

[21] "In the course of her talk Mahmuda made the wealthy equal to the shoe-seller."

[22] "By the *ghadar* is meant the upheaval of 1857, when some of the Government's troops, faithless to their salt, turned against the Government; in the end, after some months of disturbance, the Government punished the rebels well, and the upheaval died down."

[23] "There is a village five miles to the south of Delhi; because the tomb of Hazrat Sultan-ul-Auliya Nizam-ud-Din is there, the place has come to be called Sultan-ji. On the seventeenth of Rabi' al-Thani a very large *'urs* (death-anniversary festival) is held, and this *'urs* is one of the famous fairs of Delhi."

[24] *Asuda*: "at ease".
