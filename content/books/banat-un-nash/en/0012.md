# The Pull of the Earth

## The Pull of the Earth[1]

Mahmuda: "The earth pulls all things towards itself. When a thing is thrown upwards, it goes up for some distance by the force and effort of the thrower, and then in the end the pull of the earth draws it down again. Throw a stone upwards and keep watching, and you will see that the higher it goes, the slower and gentler its movement becomes; and then when it turns, it rushes towards the earth like an arrow. This plainly proves that, because of the pull of the earth, things do not want to go upwards, and if they do go, it is with great force and difficulty. In the same way, when a person starts to climb the column, the weight of their body holds them back, and they have to heave their whole weight upwards by force at every step; that is why climbing takes so much effort and a person tires quickly. Then when they start to come down from the top, see how quickly they come thumping down, because in the first place there is their own strength, and in the second the pull of the body's weight; the force is doubled. For example, this upper room of ours is not very high at all, only eighteen steps, and still how hard it is for someone not used to it to climb. Whenever Mother has to go up, she sits down to get her breath the moment she reaches the roof, and says, 'Well, is this a roof or a calamity? It breaks one's legs.' But coming down is no trouble at all. So getting out of breath climbing the column cannot be a proof that it is thousands of *kos* high, as you said."

Husn Ara: "Well, madam, well! Today I have heard something quite new, that the earth pulls things. But tell me, why do the kites that boys fly go further and further from the earth by themselves? Once Arjumand[2] let out his kite so far that he made it touch the sky."

Mahmuda: "Whether it is a paper kite or a big kite, the pull of the earth works on all of them; and if a kite is let out and left there, it may take long to fall because of the gusts of wind, but fall it certainly will."

## Specific Gravity

Mahmuda: "The secret of it is that some things are light and some heavy. All light things rise to the top by themselves. For example, put oil into a glass first and water on top of it; since oil is lighter than water, it will come to the top by itself. Or put the straws of a broom in a basin and fill it with water; the straws will come to the top by themselves. And it is on this principle that boats and ships sail on rivers, because wood is lighter than water and cannot sink below it; in fact it so dislikes staying under water that even if there is some load on it, it still holds it up."

Husn Ara: "Boats sink too."

Mahmuda: "When they are loaded beyond measure, a boat is bound to sink. But wood by its own nature can never stay under water; what if it sinks to the bottom under a load? Once the load is taken off, the wood floats up to the top. So a kite is made of such thin paper that if you rolled that paper into a ball and weighed it, it would not be more than one or two *mashas*; but it is spread out so wide. The post paper on which letters are written, half a quire, that is twelve sheets, hardly weighs one *tola*. Now take one sheet of paper: it occupies a certain space, and if you divide the weight of the sheet over the air that fills that space, the load on a whole seer of air is less than a poppy seed. But roll the sheet into a ball, and the whole weight of the paper is gathered together; and what is a ball, how much air is there in that little space? That is why the kite goes up, and a ball of the same weight falls down. When wise men thought a great deal about the pull of the earth, they found that it depends on distance and on bulk; that is, the more solid a thing is, the greater the earth's pull on it; and the nearer it is to the earth, the greater the effect of the pull on it. If you roll a stone off the roof, the nearer it gets to the earth the faster it will go. In the same way, tie up a ball of paper weighing a pice and drop it together with a pice from up high; to look at, the paper ball will be bigger than the pice, but because it is not solid, the pice will always land first. Smoke too always goes upwards by this same rule; for smoke is the name of the fine particles of wood and the like that come out because of the heat of the fire, and because they are lighter than air, they rise."

Husn Ara: "What a fine thing you have told me! But from what you say it seems you think air has weight too."

Mahmuda: "Certainly, and air has weight without any doubt."

## The Pressure of the Air

Husn Ara: "The wretched air is as light as a flower; where would weight come into it?"

Mahmuda: "It has so much weight that you would be amazed to hear it. On a space the size of a rupee the weight of the air is not less than five seers; at that rate there must be a weight of several thousand maunds on your body."

Husn Ara: "Oh, heaven forbid! God forbid there should be such a weight; I'd be crushed to pulp."

Mahmuda: "This is not wrong; wise people have weighed the air and found it out by weighing."

Husn Ara: "Everything you say is the sort of thing nobody's mind can take in."

Mahmuda: "Certainly these things cannot get into the minds of uneducated people, but that is the fault of their minds."

Husn Ara: "Well, can anyone weigh the air?"

Mahmuda: "Listen to how it is done. You take an empty bottle and weigh it. The bottle is empty, but even so there is air in it. In the weight that this weighing gives, most is the bottle's, and a little bit the air's. Then you take the air out of the bottle and weigh it, and you see that the weight has gone down; now what is the reason for that?"

Husn Ara: "How can the air be taken out of a bottle?"

Mahmuda: "There is a machine for taking out air called a pump; with it, if you want, you can empty a whole house of air in a pinch . . . . of time. And in a way the air in a bottle can be sucked out with your mouth too, provided you do not take your mouth away from the mouth of the bottle." Husn Ara: "Then how is the bottle to be weighed, and how are we to be sure that there's no air left in it?"

Mahmuda: "Making you sure is not hard at all. Well, fill a bottle to the brim with water; now is there air in the bottle or not?"

Husn Ara: "I think there's no air." Mahmuda: "Why not now?" Husn Ara: "I don't know."

Mahmuda: "I'll tell you the reason: air is lighter than water; as the water filled the bottle, the air went out. Well, fill the bottle to the brim with water, press the cork in firmly at the top; then fill a pan with water, stand the bottle upside down in it, and take out the cork under the water. The water will come down from the bottom of the bottle and leave some space empty. Now there is certainly no air in that space, because we have not let air in from anywhere. Or why take so much trouble? Take the rubber balloon that boys play with; first squeeze it flat and weigh it; now there is no air in it. Then blow air into it; when it is tight, tie the end and weigh it again. There is bound to be some difference in the weight; and if the scale is a good one, it will show plainly. Try it whenever you like."

Husn Ara: "But the weight you say the air has is quite beyond belief."

Mahmuda: "There is a reason too why we do not feel the weight of the air: there is air in every place and in everything; the air inside holds back the air outside; if there were no air outside, the body would burst. And people who have sometimes gone very high in balloons have had good experience of this, because the air near the earth is very heavy, and the higher you go the lighter the air becomes. Let me explain this to you with a very simple example: if a big heap of cotton is piled up, the cotton at the top will certainly be light, and the cotton at the bottom will be pressed down and packed tight. The air is exactly like that. We live on the earth; just as dense as the air above and around us is the air that fills our bodies, and the pressure of the air outside and the force of the air inside are equal. When you go very high, inside there is the same dense air, but outside the air is light, and its pressure is much less than the force of the air inside; that is why the body begins to burst. So that you understand this well, I will give two more examples. You agree that water is heavy, or have you some doubt about that too?" Husn Ara: "Who doubts that water is heavy? I can't even lift a ha'penny water-pot." Mahmuda: "Well, have you ever bathed in a tank?"

Husn Ara: "Hundreds of times. At our house there's a big long tank right in the women's quarters, with an iron grating over it and fish of all colours kept in it."

Mahmuda: "Under the water, do you feel any weight of water on your body?"

Husn Ara: "No." Mahmuda: "Why?" Husn Ara: "I can't make it out at all."

Mahmuda: "The reason is just that the water above presses down, and the water below pushes up, because a person's body is lighter than water; the pressure from above and the push from below are exactly equal, so nothing is felt. Judge the air in the same way. And the second example is this: a mirror is a very delicate thing; have you seen the mirror in the teacher's dressing case?"

Husn Ara: "The one with a fine crack right down the middle?"

Mahmuda: "Yes, I'm the one who cracked it. One day I was combing my hair; a lock got tangled, and I gave a jerk to untangle it; the comb slipped out of my hand and hit the mirror with a crack; when I looked, there was a hairline crack in the mirror. And yet you could stand on that same mirror and nothing would happen."

Husn Ara: "How strange!" Mahmuda opened the cupboard, took out the mirror, laid it on a level place, and said to Husn Ara, "There, say *Bismillah* and stand on it with both feet."

Husn Ara: "No, my dear; if it breaks, the mirror will be ruined, and the teacher will be cross [illegible: the last line of the page is cut off in the scan]" Mahmuda: "Being careful is right, of course, but just now a question of science is being settled. Here, I'll cut off my horns and join the calves myself[3]." With that she stepped onto the mirror without ceremony, and the mirror came to no harm at all. Husn Ara was amazed at the sight, and kept picking the mirror up in her hands and looking at it closely.

Mahmuda: "Look at it well; never mind breaking, there isn't even a hairline crack. And why should there have been? As much as my weight pressed from above, so much the earth held it up from below; what harm[4] could come to the mirror?"

Husn Ara: "Now I too believe that it's true that the earth pulls things towards itself."

## The Attraction of Cohesion

Mahmuda: "It isn't only the earth; all things pull one another."

Husn Ara: "The earth's pulling was shown by the fact that whatever you throw falls to the earth; but how was it found out that all things pull one another?"

Mahmuda: "It is recognised from several things. First, if you dip your finger in water, a drop of water stays hanging at the tip of your finger; if there is no pull from the finger, why doesn't the drop fall? Besides that, put a hailstone in a little water, and you will see that the water climbs up from the bottom to the top of the hailstone; if the hailstone doesn't pull the water, why does the water climb upwards? Let me tell you one more thing: come up on the roof, and I'll hang a fine thread of raw yarn and hold it taut; it ought to stay straight, but because of the pull of the wall it will certainly look bent in the middle. In short, God Almighty has created the power of attraction in everything, and by thinking about this property the wise men of Europe have discovered thousands of such wonderful things that reading them sharpens the mind and" [TN: The sentence breaks off here in the printed text.]

Husn Ara: "But if all things are pulling one another, why don't they all come together into one heap?"

Mahmuda: "They are pulling, but that pull is not as strong as the pull of a magnet."

## The Magnet

Husn Ara: "What's a magnet?" Mahmuda: "Don't you even know what a magnet is? A magnet is a kind of iron; some people wrongly think it a stone and call it lodestone. God Almighty has given this iron the property of pulling other iron towards itself. Even better than that, it has another fine and useful property: if a needle is made of magnetic iron, one end of the needle will always point north and the other south."

Husn Ara: "Are you telling me all this from hearsay, or have you seen it?"

Mahmuda: "Seen with my own eyes and tried with my own hands. Amina dear, where is that iron fish of yours that swims in water and keeps running after its baby?" Amina: "I've got it, it's in my book-bag; shall I fetch it?" Amina ran and brought the fish and the baby, and Mahmuda gave the fish into Husn Ara's hand: "Look at it carefully; there's no wire anywhere, and no mechanism fitted." Husn Ara looked the fish over thoroughly, top and bottom. Then Amina said, "Now put the fish aside, and look at its baby."

Husn Ara: "Why should I put the fish aside?"

Amina: "My dear, if the baby sees its mother it will cling to her out of love, and then if you try to separate them, it will start crying[5]."

Mahmuda: "Well, Amina, show the lady the fun of its swimming." Amina brought a china bowl full of water and let the fish go in the water; it began to swim about merrily. Whenever she showed it its baby, it ran towards it. Husn Ara was bewildered at what was going on, and kept asking, "Oh, what's in it?" Mahmuda: "Nothing at all; the fish is iron and the baby is a magnet; whenever the baby was brought near, it came running. Put the two together now and they'll stick to each other."

Husn Ara: "That's a very wonderful thing!"

Mahmuda: "Now see another wonder. Hajira, look, my dear, the teacher's rosary[6] is hanging on the peg opposite; you've got long arms, please take it down." Hajira took down the rosary and brought it. Along with the *imam*[7] bead there was a little perfume box shaped like a green mango[8], and a *qibla*-finder[9] was set in it. Mahmuda opened the little box and showed Husn Ara: "Look, the red cock that you see has this property: it keeps its face to the west, its tail to the east, its right wing to the north and its left to the south; turn it whichever way you like." Husn Ara turned the box round and round and turned it upside down and right side up; but the thoroughbred cock stood on one leg: the moment the box was level, the cock turned its face to the west and stood still.

Husn Ara: "Oh, what an obstinate wretch of a cock! It just won't do what I want; cut the beast's throat! Well, Mahmuda Begam, after all, these are all just toys, aren't they?"

Mahmuda: "The fish is a toy, but the *qibla*-finder is not a toy; it is a very useful thing. In the wilderness, in a strange place, at night, in the rains, it shows you the direction. When ships sail on the sea, there is nothing to be seen on every side but water; no road, no path, no tree, no mountain; there is no knowing where they are going or where they are. In former times sailors managed by knowing the stars; but whenever it was cloudy at night and the stars could not be seen, there was great difficulty; ships would wander hundreds of *kos* off course and in the end be wrecked. Since the property of the magnet was discovered, there has been great security. It is only a little needle, but it does the work of hundreds of thousands of rupees; the merchandise[10] worth crores of rupees that comes and goes by sea from the English homeland is saved from sinking thanks to this needle, and hundreds of thousands of people who travel by sea come and go without fear or danger. Yes, so the earth's pulling of things, or things pulling one another, is not as strong as the way a magnet pulls iron."

## The Earth Is Round and Goes Round the Sun

Mahmuda: "But what a wonder of God's power it is that, because of this same pull, the earth is going round the sun, rolling like a ball."

Husn Ara: "The earth is rolling like a ball and going round the sun?"

Mahmuda: "Yes indeed, rolling like a ball and going round the sun."

Husn Ara: "Now you're really going too far and trying to make fools of the whole world!"

Mahmuda: "Why, am I saying anything wrong?" Husn Ara: "If I say it, you'll take offence; God has handed you the cudgel of a tongue; make the earth a ball if you like, roll it, spin it, do whatever you please. And if the earth ever really became a ball and started rolling, then in a single turn the lady's lies and truths would all come tumbling out."

Mahmuda: "Well, if it is proved that the earth is round, and rolls, and goes round the sun, will you believe it then?"

Husn Ara: "I haven't gone mad; even if the whole world came to believe it, yours truly won't. I can't put potsherds over my eyes; I can plainly see that the earth looks perfectly broad and flat; so why should I think it round for no reason?"

Mahmuda: "So that is the only reason you don't think the earth is round, isn't it, that to the eye it looks broad and flat?"

Husn Ara: "In this world what you see with your own eyes is the most trustworthy thing of all, but go ahead and deny that too. Because you've convinced me about two or three things, you think you've made me such a fool now that I can't understand even so plain a thing."

Mahmuda: "Well, what if the eye makes mistakes?" Husn Ara: "Mine, or everybody's?" Mahmuda: "Everybody's."

Husn Ara: "Oh yes, then I don't know the recipe for the kohl that makes the earth look round the moment you put it on."

Mahmuda: "I'll tell you that recipe. Zubaida dear, please go and ask the teacher for that microscope[11] glass in my name; mind you carry it carefully."

## The Microscope

Zubaida brought the microscope. Mahmuda: "Here you are, just look through this glass."

Husn Ara: "Is this the glass that shows the earth round?"

Mahmuda: "No, it doesn't show the earth round, but you can see lots of other sights." Husn Ara looked and said, "Oh my, these hairs of my head as thick as ropes! And look, it seems as if the hair is hollow in the middle like a tube[12]."

Mahmuda: "Yes, I've seen it; a hair is hollow inside."

Husn Ara: "Look at this sight too! A hole at every hair of the body. Look at a fly: thousands and thousands of eyes, and so many colours in its wings! Oh my, so many gnats in the air! God is great[13]! What dreadful creatures in the water! This glass is a glass of wonderful magic[14]."

Mahmuda: "It is this very glass that proves the shortcoming of the eye."

Husn Ara: "What shortcoming of the eye does it prove? Heaven knows what devilry is packed into it; it seems to be some magic glass. I have a three-sided piece of white glass too, and it has other properties: whatever thing you look at is white, but when you look through it you see blue, green and red stripes like a border."

Mahmuda: "That three-sided glass of yours is true too. In a book I read a little account of colours . . . . . . ."

## Colours

Mahmuda: "It says that there are many colours in the world, but the original colours are three: yellow, black and red; and all the other colours are made from these. And people think that when there is no colour, it is called white; but when wise men looked into it, they found that all the colours together make white. And although there are many other proofs of this, you saw it with your own eyes in the three-sided glass. The real truth of the coloured bow that appears in the sky in the rains is this: the sun comes out opposite the tiny drops of water left in the air, and its rays[15] in the drops begin to look coloured. Once I got up after washing my hair; my hair was damp; I shook it with my hand, and as the drops flew off, wonderful colours appeared. I was so absorbed in this sight that as long as there was a little damp in my hair, I kept on shaking it. Somehow the teacher noticed and said, 'Oh, Mahmuda, what's the matter today that you keep shaking your wretched hair? It's dry hair; the ends will break.' Then I told the teacher that I was watching this new sight, and couldn't make out the reason for it at all. The teacher took a book out of the cupboard and showed me the account of colours: 'Read this, and where you don't understand, ask.'"

Husn Ara: "What can I say? Coming here, I seem to have lost my wits. Everything I hear astonishes me, and I say to myself, what have I seen and what have I learned since I came into the world? Well, prove that the earth is round; that's the one thing still left."

Mahmuda: "Yes, the microscope shows us the shortcoming of our sight. I'll tell you two more things. One is this: you think yourself a great Jahaniyan Jahangasht[16]; Palta-ji, the Qutb Sahib, Meerut, Panipat, who knows where else, you said you had been." Husn Ara: "Yes, God keep me, I am a Jahaniyan Jahangasht; have I seen little of the country? Out in the open it could be seen plainly that a little way off the earth met the edge of the sky; not only I, everyone saw it so, that the sky is covered over the earth like a lid. I believe there isn't a city I haven't seen."

Mahmuda: "Why do you tell lies? Well then, have you been to your in-laws' at Jhajjar?" At the name of Jhajjar Husn Ara lowered her eyes and said, "Why mention that wretched village, no sun in winter, no shade in summer? Heaven forbid, why should I go there?"

Mahmuda: "Well, how did you travel to Panipat and Meerut?"

Husn Ara: "By relays of palanquin carriage." Mahmuda: "On the way you must certainly have looked about you."

Husn Ara: "I did look, but once when I put my head out to spit out a mouthful of betel juice[17], what do I see but the ground slipping away[18] under my feet; seeing this I began to feel dizzy, and quickly drew my head in."

## The Eye's Mistakes about Moving Things

Mahmuda: "Remember that this is the eye's second mistake: it is the carriage that moves, and it looks as if the earth is moving. Let me ask another thing: have you ever seen the moon running through broken clouds?" Husn Ara: "Hundreds of times; we always play 'Uncle Moon'[19] on moonlit nights."

Mahmuda: "Do you think the moon runs so fast?" Husn Ara: "What else?"

Mahmuda: "Well, when there are no clouds, why doesn't the moon look as if it's running like that? If the moon were really moving, its movement would show still more plainly on clear nights." Husn Ara: "I can't make out the reason."

Mahmuda: "I'll tell you; this too is a mistake of the eye. The wind carries the clouds along, and the clouds are moving; it looks to us as if the moon were running."
