# The Girls of the School

## The Girls of the School

These girls were not, like Husn Ara, all of them the daughters of rich families. Most were the daughters of tradesmen, and some of settled[1] people in service. Though compared with Husn Ara they were all poor, among themselves one was better off, one of middling means[2], and one very poor; and just as their circumstances differed[3], their looks and characters were certainly different from one another too. But the teaching of the school had wiped out the differences of character altogether. Although these girls came from several households, they were so close to one another that you would have thought they were all real sisters. There was never a quarrel among them, nor any kind of ill feeling. To remove[4] the differences in their looks was not in Asghari's power; but she had done this much, that differences of looks no longer counted for anything with anyone. The girl who was fair and light of skin never looked down on a dark-complexioned[5], very dark[6] girl, nor was she vain of her fairness[7]; and the one with good features did not despise the plain one, nor was she pleased with herself at the sight of her own face. As for riches and poverty, they were never discussed here at all; no one knew what a curse riches are, or that being poor is anything to be ashamed of.

No sooner had Husn Ara joined the school than the topics of looks and of riches and poverty came alive again. The moment she arrived, Husn Ara began frowning and pulling faces at the sight of the poor girls. Far from sitting beside them, she disliked the very fact that poor girls were in the school at all. And she was so dreadfully proud of her looks that at the sight of some of the girls she would burst out laughing, and say without a thought, "No face, no shape, straight out of the oven." Mahmuda and Husn Ara already had a kind of acquaintance; they had happened to see each other, and even to talk, two or three times at weddings. It is the rule that when a person goes somewhere new, they ask some acquaintance about the people there. Husn Ara was sitting next to Mahmuda anyway, and she began quietly asking her about the girls of the school.

## Husn Ara Looks Down on the Schoolgirls, and Mahmuda Puts Her in the Wrong

Pointing at Zubaida, she said, "I say, Mahmuda Begam, who is that pockmarked girl opposite with a face like a *Tabarak* loaf[8]?" With that Husn Ara laughed at her own joke and looked at Mahmuda's face, hoping that she too would be tickled by such a jibe. Here it had exactly the opposite effect on Mahmuda. She said nothing, but she heard Husn Ara's remark with such contempt that it showed on her face, and she turned away and answered, "She lives in Amir Khan's mansion; her name is Zubaida; her father is a darner."

Husn Ara: "Really, what sort of darner is he? Why doesn't he take a quarter-seer of mince and darn his daughter's face?"

Mahmuda: "My dear, she is pockmarked, not torn-mouthed[9]; if she were torn-mouthed, he would darn it."

Husn Ara: "And who is the other one beside her, the black, black one, like a black copper weight set on the corner of a carpet?"

Mahmuda: "The poor thing is the daughter of a poor tinner."

Husn Ara: "Why doesn't she get her face tinned at home, then?"

Mahmuda: "He must have no time left over from tinning in the houses of the rich[10]."

Husn Ara: "Well, who is that girl sitting in the corner? Oh dear, how dull her face looks when she cries."

Mahmuda: "Everyone's face is spoiled by crying."

Husn Ara: "Mine isn't."

Mahmuda: "How do you know?" Husn Ara: "I looked at my face in the mirror while I was crying, and it was quite a pretty, pretty face; in fact, going red made it look even livelier."

Mahmuda: "You are the first person I have heard praising a crying face. Well, may you enjoy your whimpering beauty; nobody here wants it."

Husn Ara made two or three more jibes in the same way, but she saw that Mahmuda gave her no applause[11]. In the end Husn Ara was abashed and left looking foolish. But from the very first day, in the conceit[12] of her wealth, Husn Ara began to set up such a domination[13] in the school, as if all the girls were her maids, and she began ordering everyone about without ceremony. At first Asghari Khanam did take care[14] that Husn Ara should not take a dislike to the school, because she knew very well that if her heart once turned against it, then though the world turned upside down[15], this child of God would never turn her face towards the school again. The girls of the school had taken offence at Husn Ara's manner[16] of treating them, and all of them had come to dislike her. The more Husn Ara held herself aloof, the more the girls kept away from her; and the more she gave herself airs, the more the girls thought her contemptible. Asghari checked them all with a sign, and said to Mahmuda, "Husn Ara is a very good girl, and you have found a very fine friend. Be patient for a few days and do not let her lose heart. The poor thing is like a wild bird[17] newly caught in a cage[18]. If you startle her, she will flutter and fly away and never let herself be caught again. But if you tame[19] her, you will see what songs[20] she will sing to you, and how she will charm you."

So on one side the girls set themselves to win her over, and on the other the teacher never once mentioned reading and writing; so what reason did Husn Ara have to be wild? In a few days she became so free with the girls that she might have played with them for ages, and of her own accord she asked and pressed Mahmuda to bring out her dolls to play with.

## Husn Ara Is Amazed at the Sight of Mahmuda's Dolls' House

Although Husn Ara had a great store of dolls at home, she was utterly amazed[21] at the sight of Mahmuda's dolls here. Husn Ara's dolls were bought in the bazaar: if you looked at the faces they were hideous[22], if you looked at the clothes they were clumsy; cheap trimmings, shoddy work, the sewing not right and the stitching not neat. But Mahmuda's dolls, from head to foot, were embroidered and made by her own hand. What a difference between that drudge-work of the bazaar and this home-made[23] work! Husn Ara had bought a ready-made two-storey wooden house for her dolls for fifteen rupees and was proud of it; Mahmuda had made herself a very pretty, well-shaped house of reeds and tinsel. At the sight of Mahmuda's dolls the thought came to Husn Ara for the first time that wealth is nothing beside skill and good management, and that with the skill of our own hands we can get done what money cannot. Again and again she asked Mahmuda in amazement, "Oh my, did you sew this tiny gold-embroidered[24] purse yourself? Really, tell the truth, did you make these bed pillows yourself? The trimming on this pale-green suit doesn't look as if you stitched it. Surely the teacher cut out the shirt for this baby doll[25]. Well, all right; but where did you get these striped silk curtains? Who gave you this scarf covered with gold-and-silver thread? What marvellous hair-braids, what wonderful drawstrings! And look, listen to this: chandeliers of mica, paper fans, rugs of Eri silk! And just look at this: screens of broom-straws, pillars of reed!"

In short, Husn Ara was so astonished[26] at seeing Mahmuda's dolls that she kept showing them in amazement to Mahmuda herself. To all Husn Ara's wonder Mahmuda gave only this answer: "All of this is my own doing and my own sewing and stitching, and it is nothing much. If you set your mind on sewing for just two months, you could make far better things than these. I am not even keen on playing with dolls; whenever the teacher teaches me some new kind of work, I practise on the dolls first. So what you have seen is my first, beginner's practice."

Husn Ara: "I could make better things than these in two months?" Mahmuda: "Certainly, and in even less."

Husn Ara: "Is it just sewing, nothing but sewing?" Mahmuda: "What else? And plain running stitch[27] at that."

Husn Ara: "But how could I learn so much sewing in two months?" Mahmuda: "If you set your mind to it, I promise you will learn it comfortably and quite well in two months."

Husn Ara: "Why, I can't even thread a needle yet. Look, only yesterday evening my nurse was trying for ages to thread a needle; and she wears spectacles all the time, bless her, and still can't see a thing. I happened to wander by while I was playing, and she begged me humbly, 'Dear child, won't you do one little thing for your nurse? Just thread this needle. My fingers shake so that they won't do what I tell them.' Hurmat is going about with her neck bare; she has somehow tacked the edging on, and the neck opening is still left. I tried and tried; the thread came right up to the mouth of the eye, but it wouldn't go through. Then I lost my temper and threw the needle far away."

Mahmuda: "However easy a job is, it always needs a little effort; and sewing in particular is very painstaking work. But threading a needle is not difficult at all; only, the fibres that stick out at the end of the thread when it frays should be twisted and pressed together with your fingertips, and then perhaps threading it won't take long."

Husn Ara: "Yes, that must have been it; my nurse didn't tell me this trick. Just give me a needle and thread and let me see whether I can thread it or not." Mahmuda gave her a needle with a very fine eye and a very fine reel thread. Husn Ara twisted the end of the thread with her fingertips, and the moment she put it to the eye, it went straight through. Then Husn Ara jumped for joy and said, "Hurrah! I've threaded the needle! Hurrah! I've threaded the needle! I can sew!" Mahmuda: "No, you can't sew yet, but there is only a little left to learn."

## Mahmuda Teaches Husn Ara to Sew

So Mahmuda tacked a straight running seam and had Husn Ara sew about half a span of it. In the course of this Husn Ara pricked herself with the needle three or four times. That made her lose heart[28] a little; and though she had jumped and danced over threading the needle, there was only a little of the seam done when she quickly handed it back to Mahmuda and said, "Oh, this is very difficult work."

Mahmuda: "I told you before that sewing needs a lot of eye-strain[29] and hard work. But in this world most women have to do hard work. Look how hard it is to grind at the hand-mill; and yet hundreds and thousands of women like us do it. Compared with that, sewing is no labour at all. Besides, it is the way of things that however easy a job is, it always seems hard to the beginner[30] and the learner[31]. It was only your lack of practice that made you prick your hand a few times. Look, I have had so much practice at sewing that, if you like, I could go on talking with my eyes looking into yours, and the stitches would still sit right and the seam would not be out by a hair; and as for pricking myself, there is no question of it!" With that Mahmuda laid the two pieces of cloth together for the rest of the seam, and put in the needle; it was here, and in no time at all it had come out at the other end.

Husn Ara: "Let me see, didn't the needle prick you somewhere?" Mahmuda: "No, it didn't." With that she showed her hand.

Husn Ara: "Why is your middle finger rough?" Mahmuda laughed and said, "These are not the marks of needle pricks; but I cannot deny that they come from sewing. I am not used to a thimble. Some cloth is starched[32] or thick[33], so that the needle doesn't come through easily; then you have to pull the needle through with your fingertips on one side and push the eye with the middle finger. These are the marks of that."

Husn Ara: "So it isn't only beginners; in sewing everyone's fingers are always bloody."

Mahmuda: "I am very surprised that you think such a tiny, hardly noticeable pain a great misfortune. Who knows how many little pains like this come to us between morning and evening. You get knocks and bruises at play; boils and pimples keep coming; your eyes get sore; heat and cold give you a cold in the head, and you get a fever."

Husn Ara: "Yes, but one is a pain you can't help and have no control over, and the other is buying trouble with your own hands. Why should I sit at home and wound my fingers for nothing, strain my eyes and make my neck ache? Put a coin on someone's nose and have it sewn however you like."

Mahmuda: "Is it not a hardship to live dependent on someone else?" Husn Ara: "Living dependent? What do you mean? God forbid, why should we ever depend on anyone?"

## Notes

[1] *Khush-bash*: "people who are not natives of a place, but who settle wherever their employment takes them".

[2] *Mutawassit-ul-hal*: "of middling means".

[3] *Mutafawit*: "more or less, differing".

[4] *Rafa'*: "to remove".

[5] *Fam*: "colour".

[6] *Kali bhat*: "very dark".

[7] *Sabahat*: "pure fairness of complexion".

[8] "A loaf is baked with equal amounts of sugar and milk put into the dough; the *Tabarak* chapter of the Qur'an is recited over it to finish a reading, and it is offered for the souls of the dead. That is called the *Tabarak* loaf. In the first place the loaf itself is rough on the surface, and on top of that poppy seeds are sprinkled. Husn Ara made a good jibe in comparing a pockmarked face with the *Tabarak* loaf."

[9] *Munh-phat*: "someone whose tongue knows no check and who blurts out whatever comes to mind. Mahmuda turned the remark back on Husn Ara: you are the one who is torn-mouthed, so you have more need of darning."

[10] "Here too Mahmuda put Husn Ara in the wrong: you are rich, so how could the tinner find time from tinning in your house?"

[11] "That is, she did not say 'well done'."

[12] *Za'm*: "pride".

[13] *Tasallut*: "control, pressure".

[14] *Ihtimam*: "effort; taking pains to get a thing done is called *ihtimam*".

[15] "This is a proverb: even if the impossible were to happen."

[16] *Tarz-e mudarat*: "the manner of treating others".

[17] *Ta'ir-e wahshi*: "a bird that runs from people".

[18] "That is, she has just been caught and shut in a cage."

[19] *Parcha lena*: "to settle, to make tame and friendly".

[20] *Safir*: "the song of the nightingale, the myna, the red munia and such birds is called *safir*".

[21] *Shashdar*: "astonished".

[22] *Tahangam*: "repulsive".

[23] *Khana-saz*: "made at home".

[24] *Kar-chobi*: "a kind of gold work which on clothes [illegible: the rest of the note is not printed on this page]".

[25] "A doll's baby is called a *chhunni*."

[26] *Hairat-zada*: "astonished".

[27] "Straight stitching is called *tepchi*."

[28] "In such a context people also say *himmat past ho gayi*, 'her courage fell'."

[29] *Dida-rezi*: "literally, letting the eyes drop out; meaning, making the eyes sore".

[30] "A person who is just beginning some work is called a *mubtadi*."

[31] "A new learner is called a *nau-amoz*."

[32] *Kalap*: "dipping cloth into rice water or something of that kind is called giving it *kalap*, starch".

[33] *Dabiz*: "thick, heavy".
