# The Meeting in the Mango Grove

## The Story Begins, and the Bride of Plain Speech Is Adorned

In a certain country, in the house of a certain raja, there was a son. His mother and father and all the household called him Kunwar Ude Bhan. Truly, a thread of the sun's own light had joined the glow of his youth. His goodness and his charm were not things that anyone could put into writing or words. He had filled out fifteen years and set foot in his sixteenth. His moustache was only just beginning to darken. He was brimming with swagger and cared nothing for anyone. But he had never yet found the landing-place of serious thought, and had never seen how wide the river of longing runs.

One day he rode out on his horse to look at the green country, carefree and boyish, gazing about him as he went. Just then a doe came out in front of him, and his heart was quite carried away. Leaving everyone behind, he gave his horse its head after that doe. But could any horse catch her? When the sun had set and the doe had vanished from his sight, Kunwar Ude Bhan, hungry, thirsty and downcast, yawning and stretching, bewildered, began to look for shelter. Just then he noticed some mango groves and made his way towards them. And what does he see? Forty or fifty young women, each lovelier than the next, have hung up swings and are swinging and singing songs of the rainy season. When they saw him, a great shriek went up: "Who are you? Who are you?" (With one of them, his eyes met and locked.)

She who was on the swing, wearing a red dress, whom everyone called Rani Ketki, felt love for him make its home in her heart too. But for form's sake she put up many objections: "What do you call this pushing in? Suddenly you drop down on us out of nowhere, without stopping to think that women of the house are swinging here. Sir, since you have come striding in so boldly, looking as you do, you had better go back the way you came, while the shade is cool." Then he, crushed and mortified, said: "Do not be so harsh. I am tired out after the whole day; I will lie down under a tree, out of your way. Early at first light I will get up and go wherever my face is turned. I want nothing from anyone. I left all my people behind and gave my horse its head after a doe. As long as the light lasted I thought of nothing but her. When darkness fell and my heart grew very uneasy, I looked for the shelter of these groves and came here. There was nothing to stop me, so that I might have had misgivings and held back. I came on with my head up, panting. How was I to know that lotus-ladies were swinging here, flying high on their swings? But so it was fated: for years to come I shall go on swinging in my mind."

Hearing this, the lady in the red dress, who was the leader of them all, said: "Yes, now, no more teasing and taunts. Tell him he may lie down wherever he likes, and whatever he asks for to eat and drink, bring it to him. No one to this day has killed a guest who came to the door. The look of his face, his cheeks flushed and his lips cracked, his horse's panting and his own heart trembling, the agitation and the shivering, the cold sighs and the way he drops down exhausted: these prove him true. Does a made-up story or a lie ever stay hidden? But put up a screen of some cloth between him and us."

Given this much shelter, Kunwar Ude Bhan made his bed in the shade of five or seven small shrubs in the farthest corner, away from them all. He laid his head on his hand and wished to sleep; but does sleep ever come to one caught in love? He lay there talking to himself. Then what happens? When the night begins to sigh and whisper and all her companions have fallen asleep, Rani Ketki wakes her friend Madan Ban and says: "Listen, have you heard? My heart has gone to him and nothing can hold it back. You know all my secrets. Now let come what may, let my head stay or let it go, I am going to him. Come with me; but I fall at your feet, let nobody hear. Listen: the Maker of us both has made us a pair. That is why I came to these groves."

Ketki, holding Madan Ban by the hand, arrives where Kunwar Ude Bhan lay deep in thought, murmuring to himself. Madan Ban stepped forward and said: "The Rani has come herself, knowing you were alone." Hearing this, Kunwar Ude Bhan sat up and said: "And why not? Heart has found heart."

The Kunwar and the Rani both sat in silence, but Madan Ban kept tickling the two of them. Little by little they all told each other who they were. The Rani's account came out like this: she is the daughter of Raja Jagat Parkas, and her mother is called Rani Kam Lata. "Her mother and father have told her: once a month, go to the mango groves and have a swing. Today was that day, and so she ran into you. Proposals have come from the princes of many great kings, but none of them caught her fancy. You are blessed indeed, that she is here with you, in secret from everyone. Since I am her playmate from childhood, she has brought me with her. Now tell us your story: who are you, and from what country?"

He said: "My father is Raja Suraj Bhan and my mother is Rani Lachhmi Bas. If a marriage knot is tied between us, it is nothing strange or wonderful or surprising; it has always been done so. Like face, like slap: families weigh up the match and settle it. To both kings this match after their own hearts will be welcome. But what we need is the knot between your heart and mine."

At this Madan Ban spoke up: "That is done already. Now exchange your rings, and write each other a pledge as well. Then there will be no more shilly-shallying." Kunwar Ude Bhan put his ring on Rani Ketki's finger, and Rani Ketki slipped her ring onto the Kunwar's finger, and gave him a gentle pinch too.

At this Madan Ban spoke up: "To tell the truth, even this much is a lot. Going further than this is not good, and the blame falls on my head. Now get up and come away, and let him sleep, and let him lie there and weep." The talk had been settled to everyone's satisfaction. In the last watch of the night the Rani took her companions and went back the way she had come, and Kunwar Ude Bhan, taking to his horse's back, found his people again and reached home.

What can I say of the Kunwar's state? It cannot be told. No eating, no drinking, no going about, no talking or listening to anyone. To stay sunk in that one thought, and every moment to brood and brood and beat his head. In time this became the talk of the people. Some even said to the King and Queen: "There is something fishy here. Kunwar Ude Bhan, who is the light of your house, has had a bad, strange look in his eyes these days. He does not set foot outside the house. When the women of the house try to amuse him somehow, he does nothing but heave a long sigh. If anyone teases him too much, he goes to his canopied bed, wraps up his face, and lies there weeping floods of tears."

On hearing this, his mother and father came running to the Kunwar. They embraced him, kissed his face, fell at their son's feet, joined their hands and said: "If something is on your heart, why do you not say it? What sorrow has come over you, that you lie here groaning? Give away the kingdom to whomever you like. Tell us, what do you want? Why is your heart not at ease? What thing is there that cannot be done? Speak out, open your heart. If you hesitate to say it, write it to us now. Whatever you write, we will do it exactly and bring it to you. If you say 'Jump into a well', we two will jump in this minute. If you say 'Cut off your heads', we will cut off our heads this minute."

Kunwar Ude Bhan, who would not speak at all, found hope in the offer of writing, and said only this much: "Very well, please go, and I will write it to you. But never in any way bring up what I write to my face, or I shall be ashamed. That is why I said nothing face to face." And he wrote this:

"Now that my life has come up to my nose and I could bear it no longer, and you have tried to open me up in a hundred ways and searched me so much, I set shame aside, join my hands, force my mouth open, and humbly write this. In this world no one has peace at the hands of love. Who is there that has no sorrow? That day when I went to look at the green country, a doe with her ears pricked up came in front of me, and I let my horse go full tilt after her. As long as the light lasted I followed her, lost in that chase. When it grew dark and the sun went down, my heart was very low. I made for some mango groves and went in among them, and every leaf of those groves bought my heart. This is what happened there: some young women were swinging. The leader of them all is a certain Rani Ketki, daughter of Maharaja Jagat Parkas. She gave me this ring of hers, and she took my ring, and wrote a pledge as well. So this ring of hers, together with the pledge, comes to you with what I have written. Look at them, and do whatever will keep your son alive."

The King and Queen write on their son's letter, in liquid gold: "We have both pressed that ring and that pledge to our eyes. Now do not grieve in your heart. If Rani Ketki's mother and father agree to your suit, they become our kin by marriage, and the two kingdoms will become one. And if they decide against it, then by whatever means it can be done, by the strength of shield and sword, we will win your bride for you. From today do not be downcast: play and leap, talk and go about, and be happy. We will think out a lucky hour and an auspicious moment and send a Brahmin to your in-laws, to settle the match you desire and bring back word."

The Brahmin, who had set out in haste at the auspicious hour, met with very harsh treatment. As soon as he heard, Rani Ketki's father said: "There will be no marriage between them and us. Their forefathers always stood before our forefathers with joined hands when they spoke, and if they saw the least frown, they were terrified. What of it, if they have risen now and climbed high? He on whose forehead we put a mark with the big toe of our left foot becomes a king of kings. Who has the face to bring such a proposal before us?"

The Brahmin, burning with anger, said: "The elders too were of the same mind, and used to say so in full assemblies: there is no match of clan between us and them. But we can do nothing against the Kunwar's stubbornness; otherwise when would such a shabby proposal ever have come out of our mouths?"

At this the King flung a stick of flowers at the Brahmin's head and said: "If I did not fear the sin of killing a Brahmin, I would have you ground in the mill this minute. Take him away and shut him up in a dark cell."

As soon as Kunwar Ude Bhan's mother and father heard everything that had befallen the Brahmin, they resolved on war, gathered their forces, and came on like great banks of rain-cloud rolling in. When battle began between the two kings, Rani Ketki wept like the monsoon months of Savan and Bhadon, and this came into both lovers' hearts: "What kind of love is this, in which blood has begun to rain, and good words are longed for in vain!"

The Kunwar secretly wrote to her: "Now my heart is breaking into pieces. Let the two kings fight it out between them. If it can be done somehow, call me to you. Let us both go away together to some other country. Let come what may, let my head stay or let it go."

A flower-woman, whom everyone called Phul Kali (Flower Bud), wrapped up the Kunwar's letter in the petal of some flower and took it to Rani Ketki. The Rani pressed the letter to her eyes, gave the flower-woman a whole tray filled with pearls, and on the back of the letter wrote this with the betel juice from her own mouth: "O buyer of my heart! Even if you cut me into pieces and gave me to the kites and crows, my eyes would be at peace and my heart content. But this idea of running away is not a good one. It is beyond the bounds of what a son or daughter may do. My life is not dearer to me than you; not one life, but if ten million lives were lost, still I could not stomach any talk of running away."

When this letter full of betel juice reaches the Kunwar, he scatters several trays crammed with gold, diamonds, pearls and topazes as offerings and gives them away, and the letter makes his restlessness four and five times greater, and he ties the letter on his fair upper arm.
