# Trousers and the Lower Garment

## (35)

We have described in detail and clearly the garments of the head and the middle part of the body. Now we turn to the garment of the lower body. Then after that we shall speak of the other accessories of dress and the particular styles of different groups, and after them of women's dress.

For the lower part of the body the Arabs had nothing but the *tahmat* (wrap-cloth). The Arab *tahmat* and the Hindus' *dhoti* are both thin unsewn sheets. The difference is that the *tahmat* is simply wrapped round the waist and tucked in. The *dhoti* is tied with particular fastenings among the different peoples of India. One end of it is passed through the middle and tucked in below the back, and the other some people wrap round the waist, while some pleat it and, tucking it in above near the navel, let it hang in front. The *tahmat* of the Arabs advanced in later times in this way, that its two ends are sewn together to make a ring, and both feet are put into it and it is gathered and fastened at the waist.

At the time of the rise of Islam, and for long ages before, this was the national lower garment of the Arabs. Rich and poor, king and minister, all tied the *tahmat*. The difference was only this, that the proud and haughty nobles of Arabia showed their arrogance and pride in this way, that this *tahmat* was very low and touching the ground, so that the whole of the feet were hidden, and its two ends went trailing and rubbing on the ground. Since this style smacked of pride and arrogance, and whoever went out with so low a *tahmat* thought others base and contemptible before him, Islam strictly forbade this style and gave the command that the *izar* (*tahmat*) should not hang below the ankles. On the basis of this command the *'ulama* have at present given the *fatwa* (ruling) that trousers or any garment of the legs should not be below the ankles. Yet trousers neither existed in those days nor can be included in this command, because the notion of pride and arrogance that arose among the Arab nobles from tying a low *izar* trailing on the ground never arises at all among those in India who wear long trousers.

In the very time of the Prophet of God (peace be upon him) trousers had reached Arabia from other countries and peoples, and in later times they became the national dress of the court of Baghdad and of those Arabs who had left Arabia and settled in other countries. In India before the Muslims there were no trousers, only the *dhoti*. The Muslim conquerors brought them with them to India, and among them were some devout and ascetic men and religious leaders who, following the custom of the Prophet (*sunnat*), came to this land still tying the *tahmat*. Since the *tahmat*, being *sunnat*, was a purely religious garment, it remained reserved for selfless and pious Muslims or students only. But trousers became so general in the society here that, let alone the Muslims, they came into use among the Hindus and the other peoples here.

But the question worth considering is this: of what style were the first and original trousers of the Muslims? Probably the narrow-cuffed short trousers that are called the *shar'i paijama* (lawful trousers) and are current among the pious Sunnis are the first trousers of the Muslims. These were current in Baghdad, these came into use in Iran and Turkestan, and wearing these the Muslims came to India.

In the last period in India so much change took place in their cut that the cuffs (*paincha*, *mohri*) clung round the calf, but the upper flare was almost as much as that of the old *shar'i* trousers. A few days later the cuff became somewhat longer and lower, but did not go beyond the ankles. Down to the last period of Delhi these were the trousers of the Muslims there and in all India. Although Muslims of the lower class, from mixing with the Hindu common people, tied *dhoti*s, and Hindus of honoured rank, though they might keep to *dhoti*s in their homes, when they came into polite company came wearing trousers.

In those very days two opposite kinds of trousers were current in Kabul and Kandahar. The trousers of the people of Kabul were narrow below at the cuff and so loose above at the flare that the lower body vanished into a very large baggy balloon, and one or two whole bolts of cloth went into a single pair of trousers. These can still be seen on the legs of Afghans today. In contrast, the people of Kandahar wore trousers whose upper flare was not great, but both legs were made, by joining gussets (*kali*) together, so large and so full that unless a man tucked them in or kept hold of them with his hand it was hard to walk.

Many Kandaharis came to the court of Delhi and took service in the army. Since those people were regarded as very brave, their style, dress, habits and characteristics began to be adopted among the ordinary soldiers here. And it was by their grace and the influence of their [illegible] that in Delhi the *banka*s (swaggering bravoes) wore trousers with great gusseted legs. In the [illegible] period of Delhi the style and bravery of the *banka*s became so admired that hundreds of young gentlemen joined the *banka*s and adopted their style, and the gentry, most of whom kept to their original style while many had become *banka*s, came to Lucknow.

On coming to Lucknow, a loose trouser with wide legs suddenly appeared. Up to the time of Shuja'-ud-Daula, Asaf-ud-Daula and Sa'adat Ali Khan no trace of it is found, but it appears that in the times of Ghazi-ud-Din Haidar or his son Nasir-ud-Din Haidar, when change was taking place here in dress and social life, these trousers were made by shortening those same gusseted trousers of the *banka*s; they were neither so loose that a whole bolt of cloth went into each pair, nor so narrow, like the old trousers with tight close-fitting cuffs, that it was impossible to roll the legs up. These new trousers were light and very comfortable in the Indian summer. In a few days they became so popular among the nobles and cultivated people that, except for those who laid claim to *bankpan* (swaggering style), these trousers had entered the style of all men of learning and scholarship, ascetics and the pious, and all the gentry and nobles.

Now there were only two kinds of trousers in Lucknow: one, those same gusseted trousers of the *banka*s; the other, the wide-legged trousers that had entered the style of the cultivated people of the whole city, and with such dash that many cultivated and educated people too had them sewn of *gulbadan* and *mashru'*, and a broad edging was put on the legs. The first-mentioned trousers of the *banka*s Nasir-ud-Din Haidar himself took into his own style. He was also fond of English dress, so he wore either coat and trousers or the gusseted trousers, which at present the people of the Punjab call *gharara-dar paijama*. These trousers were so dear to Nasir-ud-Din Haidar that, seeing their resemblance to the gowns of the English ladies, he began to make the *begam*s of his palace wear them too, and their entering the style of the palace had this effect, that the [illegible] women of the city began to wear them, which will be mentioned in the account of women's dress.

During the monarchy the army of Awadh had gone with the English at the time of the conquest of the Punjab and fought the Sikhs. The Sikhs wore a new kind of *oribi*, that is, bias-cut, narrow, close-fitting trouser, which is called the *ghutna*. Many of those who went to the Punjab liked this style very much, and when they came back home they were wearing those same cross-cut *ghutna*s. Most people here liked these trousers very much, and they suddenly came into such use that all the *banka*s, dandies, fashionable men and sons of nobles of Lucknow began to wear the *ghutna*, which was very close-fitting and well drawn, and many rings of its creases were kept at the ankle.

These three kinds of trousers existed in Lucknow when English rule came. The big-legged gusseted trousers vanished from among all men along with the *banka*s and their weapons. By the favour of Nasir-ud-Din Haidar they survive only among women. Among men there were only two kinds of trousers, that is, the wide trousers and the *ghutna*, or else some of the pious Sunnis would wear the old *shar'i* trousers. The first effect of the English period was that the style and cut of trousers remained the same, but trousers of satin, *gulbadan* and *mashru'*, or coloured cotton ones, were entirely given up by men. A few days later, from the social school of Aligarh College, trousers in imitation of English trousers were invented, which are neither so narrow as to cling to the calf nor so loose that the leg cannot be rolled up. Among those receiving English education and most young gentlemen of all India the use of these trousers is now increasing. Although many educated men who have reached the highest heaven (*mala'-e a'la*) of the new civilisation have given up their whole dress and begun to wear coat and trousers, yet in Lucknow even today a handful of serious people can be seen who wear the wide trousers of the old cut and do not give up their style.
