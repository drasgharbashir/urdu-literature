# Furniture, Style and Appearance

## (39)

The second thing in social life is the furniture of houses, that is, the equipment with which houses are furnished. In those days there were no tables and chairs like those of today; rather, the equipment was of a particularly Indian and Islamic taste. In houses there were platforms of planks (*takht ka chauka*), there were beds (*palang*), or delicate and handsome low bedsteads (*palangri*) for spreading on the planks. In the houses of the poor and those of middling means there were beds of *ban* (coarse fibre cord), and in the houses of nobles there were generally beds of *niwar* (broad cotton tape).

The splendour of the houses of refined people was this: it has been swept, the walls are whitewashed, on the ceiling a bright white ceiling-cloth (*chhat-giri*) is stretched, with a pleated fringe hanging all round it. In the verandah, room or courtyard is a platform of planks, on it a cotton rug (*dari*), and on the rug a shining white floor-sheet (*chandni*), spread so neatly stretched that there is no trace of a crease anywhere. At the four corners dome-shaped marble *mir-farsh* (carpet weights) hold down the corners of the floor-covering, so that the sheet should not fly up in the wind or get creased.

Above is a bright *farshi* *pankha* (large hanging fan). This too came into use in later times; originally the ornament of these houses was hand fans, which were made with great elaboration and care according to rank and degree. We shall describe them on some later occasion. On this platform and floor-covering, whether inside the room or outside, on one side, which was fixed as the place of honour, a fine and beautiful *niwar* bed was spread. On the bed in summer there was a *dari* and in winter a *toshak* (thin mattress), and over it a bright sheet was spread. In the bed-sheets, in the royal palaces or women's quarters of equal rank, a low pleated fringe reaching nearly to the ground was stitched all round, which gave the bed a special splendour. At the four legs the four corners of the bedding were tied with a handsome fastening of coloured silk cords, so that in lying down and turning over the bedding should not be pulled, or slip or shift from its place.

At the head of the bed, equal to the width of the bed, there were four very soft thin pillows of oblong rectangular shape. These pillows were mostly of *shalbaf* (*tol*), and on them were drawn white covers of *tanzeb* or fine muslin, through which the red of the *tol* showed its gleam, and they were placed one above the other like the layers of a *paratha*. Then on top of them, on this side and that, were two tiny soft *gul-takiya*s (cheek pillows) of the same cloth, to be under the cheeks when lying on one's side. These *gul-takiya*s were no bigger than the palm of the hand. After this, on the bedding on both sides, along the two side-rails, there were two round bolsters or long pillows, which gave comfort when pressed between the thighs [TN: reading *ranon*, "thighs"] while lying on one's side. At the foot were placed a *dula'i*, a *razai* or a *lihaf* (quilts of increasing thickness) according to the season. And by day, when no one was lying on it, a bed-cover lay over the whole bed.

On the platform in front of the bed, for the seat of honour, a carpet was spread over the floor-covering in the manner of a *sandal* [TN: a rug laid as a seat of honour], and on the carpet, against the bed, was a *ga'o* (large round bolster), which for daily use had a white cover, but on occasions of high ceremony very costly silk and often *karchob*-worked covers were put on it.

And if there was no bed on the platform, then on one side of it, whichever seemed suitable, there was a *masnad-takiya* (cushioned seat with bolster), and the seat was on that.

Although there were sometimes pictures on the walls, pictures were not so customary in those days as they are now; rather, in place of pictures, fine *qit'a*s (calligraphic panels), on which designs were made with great refinement, were set in frames and hung on the walls.

The gentlemen of that time were so fond of these *qit'a*s that calligraphers lived by writing and preparing them. And the truth is that this very fondness produced in that age those famous and accomplished calligraphers who considered copying books, as opposed to writing *qit'a*s, a disgrace to themselves and the work of their ordinary pupils.

Besides the plank platforms, for sitting in the courtyard, the entrance hall (*deorhi*) and outside the door there were *mondha*s (reed stools), which, although they are still to be seen here and there, in those days no gentleman's house was without. They were made of reed (*sentha*) and bamboo, and in houses where more care was taken over them, the dried skin of a goat with the hair on was stretched over these *mondha*s, or for strength the same hairy leather was stretched only over their edges. These *mondha*s were a very useful thing in those days.

Except for the nobles, who kept two houses, one for the women and one for the men, the common people and most of the middle class lived in a single house. Now an effort is generally made that every house should have some outer room near the door. In those days there was no thought of this; rather, people met their friends in the entrance hall, and if there was no room there, by putting *mondha*s outside the door, and no harm was seen in it.

Inside the rooms and verandahs paper bouquets were often placed in the niches for beauty and ornament.

For the arches of the verandahs curtains were generally considered necessary, but the curtains of reed, *sirki* (reed matting) or sacking (*tat*) that are customary today were not customary in those days; rather, curtains of this kind were considered improper. In their place cotton-padded curtains of *tol* or *jajam* (printed floor-cloth) were made, which were mostly kept tied up and were let down only at times of need. On the outer doors of the women's palaces too there were curtains of this kind, beside which some *mama* or *kahari* was often seen standing.

(3) Style and appearance. This has already been mentioned in connection with dress. But here we have to point out that in those days, in the taste of the gentry, it was not considered necessary to wear full clothes at home, inside or outside; rather, to remain bare from head to foot, wearing only a *gharqi* of *tol*, that is, a short *lungi* (loincloth), was not considered improper. This *gharqi* was of such a cut that, as with drawers (*janghiya*), the legs too remained bare apart from the covering of the private parts. At present our gentry consider it obligatory, even at home, inside or outside, to keep on a vest, *kurta* and trousers. But in the period we are speaking of, to keep on so many clothes while sitting idle in every house was against good style. At that time there were many people who put on *angarkha* and trousers only when leaving the house, and in this way made one suit last for months, and the clothes were in such a state that it seemed they had come back from the wash that very day. It was the custom that when an *angarkha* that had come from the washerman was put on, its skirts, edging and sleeves were crimped (*chunna*), and the marks of this crimping stayed as they were for months.

In women's dress, however, there was no difference. At home they kept on as many clothes as they wore when going visiting anywhere. It is another matter that the suit for going and coming was heavy and costly and the one for wearing at home ordinary. When going as a guest to someone's house, both men and women went wearing fine, refined and heavy clothes, and owing to the excellence of dress both men's and women's gatherings were very neat and splendid.
