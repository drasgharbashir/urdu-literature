# Pigeons and Kite-Flying

## (20)

Making birds fight to create amusement and displaying feats of ingenuity had become a very common pastime of the idle men of Lucknow. They advanced so far in preparing and fighting pigeons and quails that now, in whatever city of India and wherever any noble has a taste for these things (and this wretched taste is often found among improvident men of wealth), masters are sent for from Lucknow alone, and the whole establishment of this department is under their management.

## Parrots

The excessive taste for setting birds to fight began to produce novelties, and some enthusiasts turned their minds to the question of what other birds could be made to do the work done by pigeons. So a gentleman named Mir Muhammad Ali achieved marked success in making parrots do the work of pigeons.

The parrot is by nature a most faithless creature. Keep it and rear it all your life, but once it flies from the cage it never turns that way again. *Tota-chashmi* (parrot-eyedness) has become the very name for faithlessness. It speaks, talks, mimics the calls of animals, and repeats by rote whatever phrases you teach it, but it is of no use for flying, because once free of its cage it is no longer in anyone's control. But the said Mir Sahib had, by God knows what device, so changed its nature that he flew a flock of ten or twelve parrots, and they would not dare, when he whistled and called "Come!", not to come down from the sky and go straight into the cage. He used to bring these parrots to Husainabad every day and fly them.

Having described these preparations of birds, we are compelled to say: would that the people of Lucknow had spent on preparing themselves and their own bodies the labour they spent on preparing birds; then the end that came would certainly never have come.

## Kite-flying

The taste for flying kites exists to some extent all over India, and nowadays it is generally a most entertaining game of boys and young men. Seeing how widespread and universal it is, one would think it must be a very old thing in India, but it is not so. This art can hardly be said to be even a century old, and the centre of its advancement is Lucknow.

In Europe boys fly a kind of cloth kite which flies as long as you keep running holding the string, and the moment your step stops it comes to the ground. Of these too it cannot be said how long they have existed or where they were taken from.

It is said that this taste began in Delhi in the reign of Shah Alam the First. At first certain particular people used to fly the *chang*. The *chang* was made with great care. In it two *tukkal*s were set upright one behind the other a little apart and joined together. The shape of the *tukkal*s was this [figure], with rounded corners projecting on three sides. In it a slip of bamboo was pared and fixed upright in the middle, called the *thudda* (spine), and two slips were well pared and made supple and fixed above and below, called the *kanp*s (bows). The upper bow had this form [figure] and the lower bow this [figure]. Between them light thin paper was stretched. This made one *tukkal*. Two such *tukkal*s were set one behind the other, joined with cross-slips here and there in between, and paper was stretched round all four sides too, making a lantern of a particular shape, inside which a ball of cloth soaked in oil was hung tied with wires; this was lit, and people flew it at night on a strong cotton or silk string. The glory of the *chang* was that it looked like a lantern flying in the sky, and, unlike a balloon, it was under the control of the flyer. You could fly it when you wished and bring it down when you wished. It stayed steady in the air. If it ever turned over, it righted itself again.

At the same time some people made a human figure in the same manner and flew it; indeed some reliable people who remember the old days say that this figure was the very first thing invented in Delhi. Then, developing from it, the *chang* was invented, which, its length and breadth being equal, was easier to fly and keep steady in the air. The taste for it was mostly among the Hindus, and it may well have been derived from the idea of their national and religious *hava-dan* (*akas-diya*, the sky-lamp). Then, to cut down this *chang*, or with the idea of flying by day, the *tukkal* began to be flown, which was really half a *chang*, or just one side wall of a *chang*. The merit of the *tukkal* was that it could fly more easily than the *chang*. It had movement. It went dancing and running about the sky in the wind. The *chang* stayed in one place, while the *tukkal* moved about this way and that, and it was so well under control that whenever one wished one could cut another's *chang* by rubbing it with one's string.

The *tukkal* in fact made people forget the idea of flying a lantern or a lit figure, and turned them to flying in the air something that would be more under control and would run and dance about the sky. The taste for the *tukkal* grew among Muslim nobles and respectable Hindus. Money began to be spent on it. The finest *tukkal* came to be known as the *patang*, whose spine was of Murshidabad bamboo, which cost eighty rupees. The paring and shaping cost twenty rupees. Two rupees' worth of paper went on it. And five rupees went on the making. In short, one *patang* was made for a hundred and seven rupees.

In any case, in Delhi things had advanced only as far as the *tukkal* and the *patang* when the court of patronage moved from Delhi to Lucknow, and with it the enthusiasts of the age came here too. Now from flying the *patang* arose the taste for fighting it. Such powerful *tukkal*s began to be made that a man of ordinary strength could hardly hold them. Strong string of eight strands was wound on reels, and by means of these reels the pull of the *tukkal*s was controlled. The manner of the fight was that the strings of two *tukkal*s were crossed and slack was let out on both sides. Both *tukkal*s went spinning and climbing higher and higher, and on both sides reel after reel was emptied. Lucknow's enthusiasm may be judged from the fact that Nawab Asaf-ud-Daula's *tukkal* carried a fringe of five rupees' worth of gold tinsel (*muqaish*). Whoever brought it back when it was cut loose was given five rupees and the *tukkal* taken from him. And if he did not bring it back, he could still sell the fringe wherever he liked for five rupees.

The famous old masters of kite-flying in Lucknow were Mir Imdu, Khwaja Mitthan and Shaikh Imdad. A weaver too had attained mastery in this art in those days, because of which he was greatly valued in the gatherings of the nobles.

In the time of Amjad Ali Shah the *guddi* was suddenly invented, which was shaped like a lozenge. It was easier to make than the *tukkal*. The *tukkal* had two bows and one spine. In the *guddi* only one bow and one spine remained. In the time of Wajid Ali Shah the *derh-kanna* (one-and-a-half bridle) *kankauva* was made, which had the shape of the present kite, but at the bottom, in memory of the *tukkal*, there was a small paper tassel. Now Nawab Muhammad Husain Khan Salar-Jangi, Agha Abu Turab Khan and one or two noble enthusiasts put a leaf at the bottom in place of the tassel and made the kite that is now current, in which no further improvement yet seems possible. At present throughout India the leaf-tailed kite or the tasselled kite called *derh-kanna* is flown, but it was invented in Lucknow itself. From here it went everywhere and became universally popular.

In fighting kites too, as with the *tukkal*, the custom at first was to let out slack. Large kites were made and pounds of string were let out after them. Finally there was the famous master of the royal period and the beginning of the English period, Vilayat Ali, who was called Vilayati; Ilahi Bakhsh Thande, who became famous after going to Matiya Burj; and there were hundreds of accomplished masters of Lucknow whose names I do not now recall; but the truth is that they were kings of the long-string (*lamdori*) entanglement fight.

At the beginning of English rule the custom of the pull (*khench*) fight arose. It began with beginner boys who had only a little string, and who, entangling another's kite, out of their poverty of string would pull in wildly and cut it. The old masters in those days looked on these people with contempt and kept their kites away from them. But in the end the pull became the highest art of kite-fighting, in which great masters arose. And today in Lucknow there are scores of masters who have become masters by squandering hundreds of thousands of rupees on this passion, and, ruining their homes, have won such pre-eminence that they are invited with great eagerness to the kite-flying grounds and received with respect and honour and treated with the greatest regard.
