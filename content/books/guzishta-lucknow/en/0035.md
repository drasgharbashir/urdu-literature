# Turbans

## (34)

Although in India, and especially in Lucknow, the national headwear is the cap, it should not be thought that the love of delicacy here wiped out the turban. At court turbans were generally in use. The weighty noble turbans (*dastar*) of Delhi certainly did not survive here, and only caps remained on the heads of the nobles and the royal kinsmen; but for the court turbans were prescribed down to the last period, and it was the moral duty of ordinary servants, and still is to a very large extent, to tie a turban on their heads when they go before their master.

On the heads of the rulers themselves the old *dastar* remained until the time of Nawab Sa'adat Ali Khan. On the heads of Nawab Burhan-ul-Mulk, Nawab Shuja'-ud-Daula and Nawab Asaf-ud-Daula there used to be the same white *dastar* as that of the officers of state of Delhi, on which on occasions of great *darbar*s jewelled plumes (*kalghi*), jewel-set aigrettes (*jigha*) and turban ornaments (*sarpech*) were fixed. But in themselves those turbans were plain and white. On Nawab Sa'adat Ali Khan's head, however, we see a new kind of turban which the people of Lucknow in their own language called the *shamla*. This *shamla* was made here in this way: with padding, a broad, thin, round ring of cloth was made to the measure of the head, which remained empty and open in the middle. Then a thin, very long roll was made of some fine silk or shawl cloth, and scores of turns of it were wound evenly below and above over this ring of cloth and stitched on. To the top of this ring a broad band of the same silk or shawl cloth was joined, so that it should keep the ring from slipping down. But it could not cover the whole crown of the head, so under it there was always some ordinary *dupalli* or *chaugoshiya* cap. This was the original *shamla* of Lucknow, which Nawab Sa'adat Ali Khan first wore. And probably it was derived from those turbans of the Hindu and Muslim courts of Central India which were made by winding rolls of some fine coloured cloth hundreds of yards long in particular arrangements. Nawab Sa'adat Ali Khan not only wore this *shamla* himself but also granted it to the dignitaries of the court, the grandees of the state and the ministers.

The British Government made Ghazi-ud-Din Haidar a King and put a crown on him, which was in fact not a royal crown of India and Asia but a kind of European crown. From that time the rulers of Lucknow entirely gave up the *shamla* or *dastar*, and with them all the princes, Nawab-zadas and grandees of the city too bade farewell to the turban. On special occasions the princes wore crowns, but generally the pointed (*nuke-dar*) caps of heavy work with trimmings, and the other dignitaries of the city imitated them.

But the officers of state, ministers and officials were ordered to come to the court of the kings and ministers wearing the *shamla*. From the time of Ghazi-ud-Din Haidar to the reign of Amjad Ali Shah the same *shamla* whose picture we have shown our readers in words was on the heads of all officers. When Wajid Ali Shah devised the special cap of his court, the *Alam-pasand* (*jhola*), it became the rule that those who were admitted to greater intimacy and were honoured with the title of "*Daula*" were also granted the *Alam-pasand*. It was their duty to come to court wearing the *Alam-pasand*. Those of lower rank admitted to the presence, who were superintendents (*daroga*) of some workshop or department, were granted the *shamla* along with the title of superintendent, and they attended wearing the same old *shamla* that people had first seen on Nawab Sa'adat Ali Khan's head. All other people were ordered to come to court with some kind of turban tied, and if they had no turban, to take off their caps. A *shamla* of the same kind as that of the officials we have mentioned was probably found at the court of Murshidabad too, and it was its influence that fifty years ago we used to see the Bengali pleaders of the Calcutta High Court wearing a *shamla* of the same kind; but that *shamla* was lighter than the *shamla*s of the court of Awadh and in our eyes a little shallow.

By now all the well-to-do people and dignitaries of the city, except officers, had altogether given up the turban. But even so, what greater proof can there be of the honour in which the turban was and is held in people's hearts at court and also among the common people than that at weddings, in every class high and low, there is always a turban on the bridegroom's head, and among the gentry of Lucknow the *shamla* of heavy *kamkhwab* is generally customary.

Besides the turbans mentioned, the court here had also prescribed turbans of distinct styles for different classes of servants. For the men of the pen, that is, the clerks, a turban of white muslin like the said *shamla* was prescribed. The court runners (*harkara*) and mace-bearers (*chobdar*) also wore turbans of the same cut (for those turbans were not tied but worn like a cap). The difference was that the runners' turbans were red and the mace-bearers' shining white, with a flower of *muqaish* (gold or silver wire) stitched on in front on the right side. The palanquin-bearers' (*kahar*) turbans resembled the runners' turbans. On their turbans silver fishes were stitched on the edge on the right side, and on their bodies were loose cloaks (*chugha*) of red broadcloth.

Besides these, turbans were also customary in all the armies and among the attendants of honoured people, which were distinct in their style and as if self-grown.

The most honoured and revered turbans were those of the *'ulama* (religious scholars), and it seems fitting on this occasion, in connection with turbans, that we should discuss the whole attire of the scholars along with the turbans of the noble *'ulama* and leaders of the community. In Lucknow there are *'ulama* of two sects of Muslims: first, the *'ulama* of the Sunnis; second, the *mujtahid*s and learned men of the Shi'as. The styles of the two are distinct. The Sunnis see the dignity of sanctity and authority in the dress of the Arabs, and the Shi'as in the style of the scholars of Persia and Iran. The dress of the *'ulama* of both groups accords with this taste and inclination.

In the blessed time of the Prophet (peace be upon him) the turban of the Arabs was no more than some short piece of cloth wound round the head, which had nothing to do with either cut or style. But when in the time of the Abbasid Caliphs Iraq became the seat of the Caliphate, Persian and Sasanian dress entered the style of the Arab grandees and great men. In any case, the great and magnificent turbans and *tailasan*s (scholars' hoods) and so on that the Arab scholars of the period of the Caliphate adopted can hardly be called Arab dress. The Sunni *'ulama* of India in earlier days had given up that Arab dress and adopted the court style of Delhi, and kept up that dress with such constancy that today all their fellow countrymen in India have given it up but they still hold to it.

Thus to this day the original style of the *'ulama* of Farangi Mahal is that they tie a plain round turban, in the tying of which no attempt at all is made to produce the shape of an arch (*mihrab*) on the forehead. On the body is the *jama* of former times, which has now everywhere become entirely a dream and a fancy. On the legs are trousers with broad wide cuffs, above the ankles, and a thin scarf (*dupatta*) lies round the neck. In this style one or two of our elders of Farangi Mahal still come to lead the Friday prayer. But at home they wear the ordinary plain *dupalli* or *chaugoshiya* cap, a long *kurta* with the neck-slit in the middle or an *angarkha*, and wide-cuffed trousers. At present the young *'ulama* of Farangi Mahal have now begun to give up this style and adopt the style of the scholars of the Two Holy Places (*Haramain*) and of the leaders of Syria and Egypt, which in the end Maulana Shibli Nu'mani also chose for national and government *darbar*s. The shoes of these elders too were in earlier days the *ghetla*, but now they are either slippers (*zer-pa'i*) or the upturned shoe of Lucknow or Delhi.

The style of the Shi'a *'ulama* is entirely different from this. First, they wear a *dupalli* cap on the head, but, unlike ordinary people, its seam, instead of running from front to back, runs across, that is, from one ear to the other. Over it is a turban with a high, tall dome, resembling the tying of the Persian turban. On the body is a long *kurta*, but its neck-slit, instead of being in the middle of the chest, is near the left shoulder. In earlier days the neck-opening in the *kurta*s of the Shi'a *'ulama* used to be on both shoulders, but this style has now fallen out of use. Those *'ulama* who have been to Iran and Karbala wear over the *kurta* the former *tailasan*, which is here called the *qaba*. On the legs are wide-cuffed trousers, and generally they wear *kafsh* (slippers), which will be mentioned in the account of shoes.
