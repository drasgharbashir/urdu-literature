# The Manner of Speech

## (43)

(7) Manner of speech. The seventh important thing in the manners of social life is conversation and the manner of speech. In the world the first estimate of every person's polish and literary ability is made from his words and his manner of conversation. Every prosperous people in the world first of all reforms its language and advances it.

Culture and polish demand that coarse and obscene words should not come on the tongue; that words and ideas disagreeable to the person addressed should not be uttered before him; and if it ever becomes necessary to express disagreeable matters, they should be expressed in such words and in such a manner that they do not weigh on the hearer, and if they do weigh on him, a certain agreeableness and charm should arise in their weight.

The mastery in this particular matter which the native speakers of Lucknow and the polished people here possess will not be seen in the people of any other city of India. Although present-day education and culture have to some extent produced this excellence in the manner of conversation everywhere, if you look at it stripped of English influence, this polish and refinement of language will appear in itself to be the share of the people of Lucknow alone.

Outsiders so acknowledge its supremacy that they are bashful about conversing before the people of Lucknow and forget even such polish as they have. And afterwards, when they sit in their own company, they put away the charge of their weakness by saying: "We speak plainly and simply, and we do not know the fuss and ceremony (*chunan-chunin*) of the people of Lucknow." But in fact this excuse is worse than the offence. I have seen Indians forget how to speak before Iranians. In England I have seen that before the French it becomes hard for even a single word to come out of the mouths of the English. In the same way the eloquence of the Arabs was at one time such that the tongues of foreigners could not open before them, and the Arabs had come to think: "God has given speech to us alone, and the whole world is dumb beside us." It was the result of this idea that they called all the people of the world except the Arabs "*'Ajam*", the literal meaning of which is the dumb. Exactly the same is the case in India with the people of Lucknow as against the people of every city: in eloquence and wit they overpower everyone and dominate the company, and let no one else open his mouth before them.

The very first thing in polish of speech is with what pronouns the person addressed should be named. In all languages there are two pronouns for the person addressed, one singular and one plural. And for an honoured person addressed, in every language the plural pronoun is used in place of the singular as a mark of respect. In Persian the pronoun of the second person singular is "*tu*" and of the plural "*shuma*". In Arabic, of the singular "*-ka*" and "*anta*", and of the plural "*-kum*" and "*antum*". In English an honoured person is addressed with the word "you". Unlike all these languages, in Urdu there is indeed only one singular pronoun for the person addressed, "*tu*", but there are two plural pronouns, "*tum*" and "*ap*". And for these three pronouns the degree and rank of the person addressed is fixed. A very low person will be called "*tu*". One who has a little distinction among people of low degree, and one's own juniors, will be called "*tum*". And one who is an equal, an honoured and educated gentleman, will be called "*ap*". Although people of honoured rank sometimes in informality call their peers and fellows and those of their own age "*tum*" too, to call "*tum*" those with whom one is not on informal terms is a moral and literary offence in Urdu, and especially among the people of Lucknow.

In the Urdu language, and especially among the people of Lucknow, there are not only these degrees of address, but beyond them many words which it is obligatory to use towards gentlemen and dignitaries: *Janab*, *Janab-e wala*, *Janab-e 'ali*, *Hazrat*, *Hazrat-e wala*, *Huzur*, *Huzur-e wala*, *Huzur-e 'ali*, *Qibla*, *Qibla-o-Ka'ba*, *Sarkar*, and a few more words of this kind are used in Urdu for an honoured person addressed according to his degree, which are current on the tongues of the people of Lucknow, and whose correct use the people of Lucknow know as the people of no other city do.

Our claim is that there are not so many honorific words of address in any language in the world. In India the time has passed when Urdu was the teacher of manners to all the languages here, and now, freed from pupillage to Urdu, all the languages are beating the drum of sovereignty. Bengali, Punjabi, Gujarati, Sindhi, Marathi, Kannada, Telugu and the rest all lay claim to literary advance and eloquence. But we challenge the said Indian languages, and with them the famous languages of the whole world, Persian, Arabic, English and French too: if they claim greater literary range and eloquence than Urdu, let them produce from their dictionaries as many words for the person addressed as exist in Urdu. The truth is that despite its youth and its limited domain, Urdu had in a few days reached a degree of perfection in polish, delicacy and the proprieties of social intercourse which no language in the world possesses. The real fact is that Urdu was not the language of any country, any province, any group or any religion; rather it was the language which, beginning from the royal court, had become current in every city of India on the tongues of cultivated and polished people, those of genuine society, men of learning and scholarship, poets and lovers of letters and manners. So its very foundation was laid by the hands of culture and polish, and to the end it remained reserved for those of genuine taste and lovers of speech. It is the result of this that there is no majority of Urdu speakers in any province, but it should be remembered that the cultivated and polished people of every place are its speakers. It arose precisely in order to produce in India a society of the highest class and more polished than the whole world. But unfortunately, in the English period, when Western social life and letters took hold, the mutual and ancient natural prejudices of the Indians showed this colour: the Muslims began to pride themselves that it is our language, and the Hindus, thinking that in this language we shall not be able to compete with the Muslims, flung it at the Muslims' heads and, shaking off their skirts, stood apart. Urdu has suffered harm from this and will suffer more harm day by day. But despite this it cannot be denied that the juiciness and the literary excellences that are in it are neither in the newly produced Hindi language nor in any other language of India.

Whether English or Arab, Afghan or Iranian, when they speak Urdu no word but "*tum*" comes into their minds for the person addressed, because no other word of this kind that is more polished and advanced than "*tum*" exists in their language.

In English there are other words of address, for example "Your Honour", "Your Excellency", "Your Highness", "Your Majesty" and so on, but they are reserved for nobles of the highest rank and kings, and cannot be used of anyone else. There are words of this kind reserved for particular persons in Urdu too, for example *Jahan-panah*, *Sahib-e 'Alam*, *Murshid-zada*, *Nawab Sahib*, *Nawab-zada*, *Sahibzada*. These are the titles of people of a particular upper class, with which one may combine the words *Janab* or *Huzur* in addressing them. And probably particular titles of this kind exist in every language. But the said honorific words which in the Urdu language can be used of every honoured and polished person are not seen in any language but Urdu.

Look at the enquiry after health: in every language there are ordinary words for it, but in Urdu, to preserve respect and reverence, they ask after the well-being of the honoured person addressed by saying "*Mizaj-e 'ali*", "*Mizaj-e mubarak*", "*Mizaj-e aqdas*", "*Mizaj-e muqaddas*", "*Mizaj-e mu'alla*" and so on. Although these words are now spoken everywhere and in every city with the advance of Urdu, the faculty of discretion in their use which the gentry of Lucknow possess the people of no other place can attain.

A particular thing among the gentry of Lucknow is that *sh* and *q* will remain correct, and they will pronounce all Arabic letters as far as possible from their original points of articulation. In Persian compounds the *izafat* (connective vowel) will be clearly pronounced. When they talk with scholars and learned people they will use Arabic and Persian words more, and pronounce them correctly. If the conversation is with physicians, they will bring the medical terms of Arabic into the conversation. When they talk with ignorant servants and the common people they will avoid Arabic words. If there is talk with women, they will use in the conversation the idioms and proverbs of their taste.

When a junior talks with an elder, a lower with a higher, or a layman with a scholar, he will keep respect and reverence in mind in every word and every phrase. The voice will remain low and soft to a suitable degree. In the same way, when elders talk with juniors, those of the upper class with the lower, scholars with the common people, feelings of kindness and affection will be implicit in their tone, their manner and their words.

By keeping these things in mind and using the said words and pronouns of respect and reverence, the language of the people of Lucknow has become so polished and refined that the common and ignorant people here speak better Urdu than most of the poets and eloquent men of other cities, and the polish and good manners that show themselves in them cannot show themselves even in the able and learned people of any other place. But alas, Lucknow is passing away. Now such a storm of ill-breeding from outsiders is raging here, the polished people here have so become idle and retired into corners, and legal liberty has made the ignorant and common people so bold and ill-mannered, that all these literary excellences are being laid in the dust, and a few days hence perhaps not even a trace of them will remain.
