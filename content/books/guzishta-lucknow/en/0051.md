# The Huqqa and Pan

## (50)

We have described the *majlis*es and gatherings; now it seems necessary that we should also set out at length the requisites of company, because these are the things by which the state of social life and the manner of company become as clear as in a mirror. The requisites of company are very many, indeed countless, and we shall point them out from time to time. But for the present the foremost things are the *huqqa*, the *khasdan*, the *lotiya* (small water pot) and the *ugaldan* (spittoon). These are such necessary things that they are always present with the attendants who accompany gentlemen. Some time ago, with wealthy men of the upper class, a *huqqa* also used to be in the hand of an attendant, but now this practice has been given up. The *huqqa* is in fact an invention of Delhi, and there in the royal *bhandi-khana*s (stores) *huqqa*s of different styles were made. Whatever advance Lucknow made relates first of all to the reform of the shape and cut of the *pechwan*s (coiled-pipe *huqqa*s), the *chilam*s (bowls) and the *chambar*s (bowl-covers). The *huqqa*s of Delhi were clumsy and ugly; in Lucknow they were made most well-proportioned and handsome. Then, besides *huqqa*s of copper, brass, bell-metal and zinc, earthen *huqqa*s were made so handsome that people liked them very much for their refinement and delicacy, and to many people delicate, light, handsome and fragrant (*sondha*) earthen *huqqa*s seemed better than elaborate costly ones.

After reform and advance in the shape of the *huqqa*, wonderful refinements and excellences were produced in the tobacco itself. Pounding tobacco mixed with *gur* (raw sugar) or treacle is probably an invention of Delhi itself, by reason of which India has superiority over all the countries of the world and all the peoples on the face of the earth in the improvement of smoking tobacco. Tobacco is smoked all over the world. Although Europe has made endless efforts in improving tobacco for cheroots, cigarettes and pipes and has produced all kinds of refinements, it never occurred to anyone to remove the bitterness and harshness of tobacco by mixing treacle or *gur*, and to produce pleasure and body in the smoke. After this Lucknow made the advance of making *khamira* (fermented tobacco paste) and adding perfumes, and so rendered so foul-smelling and disagreeable a thing as tobacco so pleasant and delicate that as soon as a *chilam* is filled and set down the whole room is filled with fragrance, and even those who do not smoke the *huqqa* begin to wish to take one or two puffs. The tobacco of some regions of India is very good, and tobacco has even become famous under the names of those cities, but that fame is not the result of any human effort. The refinement that Lucknow has produced in tobacco by effort and contrivance no other city has achieved. The people of many cities do not like *khamira*, or complain that it gives them catarrh. But this is only because they are not used to it, and is just like the English disliking *qorma* or being unable to digest it. Along with tobacco all the requisites of the *huqqa* advanced. The *chilam*s too became more delicate, refined and handsome than before. The *chambar*s too kept advancing. Beautiful triple silver chains were fixed to the *chambar*s. All kinds of *mahnal*s (mouthpieces) were invented. Then refined and charming *huqqa*s of flowers were invented. In short, the society here adorned and decked out the *huqqa* and made it a bride.

Not after the *huqqa* but even more important than it among the requisites of company is the *khasdan*, which is needed again and again, and is kept with the attendants when going out and coming in. The *khasdan* is the thing in which *gilauri*s (folded *pan*s) are made and kept. *Pan* is an ancient thing of India. Its importance has come down from the time of the Hindus. In former days, when some great expedition faced Rajas and kings, or some responsible task was to be entrusted, they would make a *bira* (*gilauri*) of *pan*, set it before them and say: who will take it up? which meant, "Who will go on this expedition?" or "Who will perform this responsible task?" Whoever among the pillars of the state, the chiefs or those generally present at court took up this *bira* as good as promised that he would perform the task or carry the expedition through. This custom has died out, but the saying is still on people's tongues to this day: "So-and-so has taken up the *bira* of this task", that is, has taken it upon himself.

At the old courts *pan* too was bestowed on those present along with rewards and favours, which Ibn Battuta too mentions in his travels, which proves that *pan* is a historic thing of India. So it should have been that with the passing of time *pan* and its equipment advanced day by day. But we see nothing at all of what advance *pan* made while it was in Delhi. The ingredients of its *masala* (condiments) that had come down from ancient times remained the same to the last, and no reform of any kind was made in them either. Of its condiments, *katha* (catechu), *chuna* (lime), betel nut and cardamoms had already been chosen in ancient times. Tobacco too had become one of its ingredients before it came to Lucknow. But there is no trace at all of what particular advance the scores of past centuries and the hundreds of past courts and empires gave it.

In Lucknow the use of *pan* became much greater than in Delhi. Special kinds of vessels were invented for it, and all its things advanced separately. First the *pan* itself, that is, its leaves, was improved. The *pan*s of some cities of India, for example Mahoba and others, are naturally very good and of a high class.

Although *pan* grows abundantly round Lucknow, there is no particular excellence or superiority in it in itself. But through the attention of the progressive nobles here, the *tamboli*s (*pan* sellers) began to advance the *pan* on industrial principles and brought it to such a degree that the *pan*s here surpassed those of everywhere else. They keep the *pan*s buried in the ground for months, until their rawness goes, no green smell (*hara'ind*) at all remains, the veins become delicate and soft, and whiteness and ripeness come into the colour. The kind of sharpness that is in raw *pan* also goes, and it becomes so soft, delicate and fine that the *pan* of no place can rival it in taste and pleasure. These seasoned (*baithe hue*) *pan*s are called *begami pan*s, which go to distant cities and are taken with great eagerness and appreciation.

After the *pan* leaf comes the lime. In every place and every city ordinary lime is used, which is often not even sifted and clean. Besides this, lime is a very sharp and caustic thing. If the lime is new and fresh, or too much is used, the mouth gets cut. To avoid these harms the device used here is that it is well sifted and cleaned, and a little cream (*balai*) or the whey of fresh curds is strained and mixed into it. In this way such good, pleasant, delicate and harmless lime is to be found in the *pandan*s of the refined people of Lucknow as cannot be had elsewhere.

The second thing among the requisites of *pan* is *katha*. *Katha* in itself is a very astringent, bitter and unpleasant thing. In *pan* it is used only to correct the lime and to produce a good colour, but its astringency is very disagreeable, which may become tolerable through habit, but it cannot be denied that it is an unpleasant thing. The method of preparing *katha* that is common everywhere is that it is cut into small pieces and boiled in water, and when, coming to the boil, it becomes like a red syrup, it is strained through cloth, put in water and set. Generally everywhere only this much is done. But here they fill a dish or griddle with ashes, put a cloth over them, and spread this set *katha* on the cloth like a loaf, and keep sprinkling water on it again and again. The water, taking with it its redness, in which the astringency lies, is absorbed into the ashes. In this way, by cleaning and cleaning, only the most delicate part of the *katha* remains, which is white as washed cloth and very fine. Then they give it the fragrance of *kewra*, or keep it among *kewra* flowers, and dry it. This device has now begun to be practised in some other places too, but this invention is Lucknow's own, and the perfection with which it is practised here cannot be attained anywhere. Now many merchants have also begun to prepare *katha* of this kind in Lucknow and sell it, among whom our honoured and kind Qazi Muhammad Yunus Sahib, residing at Mahmudnagar, Lucknow, has won great fame. But the white, good and clean *katha* that is made at home in the houses of refinement-loving nobles is so fine that the *katha* prepared by the bazaar people, however good it may be, cannot reach its refinement. In the cities of the Deccan, Poona and others, a new kind of prepared dry *katha* is found in the bazaar, which is put into the *pan* dry. The people there like that *katha* too, but despite our efforts we could neither feel nor understand its merits, because apparently it is gritty too, and there is even more astringency in it than in the original unprepared *katha*.

The third of the condiments of *pan* is betel nut, which is cut with the *sarauta* (betel-nut cutter) into small pieces and put into the *pan*. Cutting it was an ordinary thing, but in Lucknow cutting betel nut too became a craft, because now many ladies cut it as fine as grains of millet (*bajra*), in which all the grains are equal and uniform, and that too on condition that not much dust comes out and no part of the nut is wasted.

In cardamoms no room for any reform has yet been felt, because they are used just as they come. But refinements have at least done this much, that on special ceremonies and special occasions silver leaf is put on them, and when they are placed in the *khasdan* or a tray it seems that gleaming pieces of silver have been placed there.

After this comes tobacco. Just as the use of tobacco in the form of smoke is universal, so the custom of eating it too is increasing. In England I saw many Englishmen who rub dry tobacco leaf and toss it into the mouth. In India too the custom of eating dry tobacco has come down for a long time, which in Delhi is called *zarda* because of its golden colour. At first only the unprocessed and unimproved leaf was put into the *pan* and eaten. But even in earlier days it was also the custom that in many houses, by boiling the stalks of tobacco in the leaf and mixing into its liquor some fragrant tempering spices, the bitterness of the tobacco was decreased or increased according to one's taste, and along with delicacy and pleasantness a reviving fragrance was also produced in it. But this device was limited to particular houses and families. Ordinary people ate the tobacco leaf itself without preparation, which was always present in every *pandan*. But now, about twenty years ago, Munshi Sayyid Ahmad Husain Sahib by his own invention presented to the country a special kind of prepared tobacco, whose appearance is like granulated gunpowder, and it became so popular that within a few years the custom of eating the unprepared leaf almost died out.
