# Fights of Wild Beasts

## (18)

There is a well-known saying in Awadh that "In old age a man's lust moves into his tongue." In the same way it has often been observed of the courage of brave and daring men that when weakness comes, or the strength of their hands and feet gives out, all their bravery and courage leaves their arms and gathers in their tongues and eyes. Now they tell tales of their past courage and renown, and instead of performing deeds of valour themselves they watch the spectacle of them through fighting animals, and show it to others in hope of applause.

So it was with Lucknow. When people were free of conquest and battle-array and no longer had the heart to stand on the field of war, their warlike feelings produced the pastime of making animals fight and watching the spectacle of daring and bloodshed. This taste exists to some small extent everywhere, but the absorption in it that the people of Lucknow developed, and the degree of perfection to which they brought these fruitless, indeed hard-hearted, amusements, never entered the dreams or imaginings of the people of other places. And if you look closely you will have to admit that such marvels and attractive spectacles of this taste and these pastimes as were seen in the neighbourhood of Lucknow were probably never seen, never mind at Delhi or any court of India, in any city of the whole world.

In Lucknow this taste for venting one's feelings through the courage of others was satisfied in three ways: (a) by making wild beasts and quadrupeds fight, (b) by making birds fight, and (c) by fighting kites, that is, through kite-flying. We wish to describe each of these three kinds in detail separately, as far as our enquiries and information allow.

The first kind, the spectacle of the fighting of wild beasts and quadrupeds, was seen here by setting the following animals to fight: (1) lions, (2) cheetahs, (3) leopards, (4) elephants, (5) camels, (6) rhinoceroses, (7) *barasingha*s (swamp deer), (8) rams. The taste for making wild beasts fight had not been heard of anywhere or at any time in ancient India. [1] This taste really belonged to the ancient Romans, among whom men and wild beasts were made to fight, sometimes together and sometimes each other. With the rise of Christianity this taste was given up there too, but to this day in Spain and some other countries of Europe wild bulls are made to fight one another and sometimes men. In Lucknow it was probably his European friends who gave King Ghazi-ud-Din Haidar the taste for watching fights of wild beasts. The King at once took to it, and in a few days the royal interest in these terrible and savage fights grew so much that no possible effort was spared. In the Moti Mahal, right on the river bank, two new mansions were built, Mubarak Manzil and Shah Manzil. Opposite them, across the river, a refreshing green meadow stretched for miles, in which a wide park (*ramna*) had been made, enclosed by an iron railing. Thousands of animals of every kind were brought and let loose in it, and the wild beasts were kept shut in cages. As part of this same park, on the river bank, great arenas for the fights of wild animals were secured with bamboo screens or iron palisades, directly facing Shah Manzil across the river. The river is very narrow there. The King and his guests and companions would sit on the upper terrace of Shah Manzil in the shade of gold-and-silver-embroidered awnings and watch the show in comfort and at ease, while in the enclosed arena on the far side the tumult of the wild beasts' doomsday fighting raged.

Making wild beasts and elephants in *must* (rut) fight is easy, but controlling them is most difficult. If one elephant in rut or one lion gets loose from its cage, there is a stampede in the city and many lives are lost. But here people had become so skilled in this dangerous work that the European travellers then present at the court themselves admit in their writings that there are no better men in the whole world than those of Lucknow for keeping, training, tending and controlling wild animals. It was these men who brought the elephants and wild beasts and let them loose, kept them under control, and when the fight was decided brought both the victor and the vanquished under their control. For this work hundreds of men with whips and men with lances were employed, who beat them and protected themselves from their attacks. With red-hot iron bars and fireworks they turned them wherever they wished and drove them wherever they pleased. They shut the lions and leopards in their cages. In short, the agility, cleverness, nimbleness and skill of these men were more interesting and astonishing than the fighting of the animals themselves; seeing these things, one realised in a moment by what means man has prevailed in the world over these huge giant-bodied animals and fearsome wild beasts. Now hear the separate account of the fighting of each of these animals, which will probably not be without interest.

## (1) The lion

The King had collected many lions, which were caught and brought from the Nepal Terai. Some of them were very large. Some, having won in various fights, had become very dear to the King. For a fight their cages were brought near the palisade of the arena and opened. As soon as they were free the two opponents growled and attacked each other, and grappled, wounding each other with teeth and claws. Now this one would throw the other down and sit on top of him, now that one would get him under. For a long time a most terrible fight went on, in which sometimes one opponent was killed, and sometimes, badly wounded, he lost heart, and weakened by the loss of much blood he fled, while his opponent pursued him in fury. At that moment the skill of those in charge of the fight, their running about and their exploits in controlling and mastering the two of them, were worth seeing.

Lions were often set to fight leopards, but there were such formidable leopards here that a lion could very seldom win against them. Their fighting was of the same kind as that of lions fighting each other. Sometimes a lion and an elephant were also set to fight, but they were not an even match, and the results too were of various unexpected kinds. If the elephant was really spirited, the lion could seldom get the better of him. The most entertaining fight was between a lion and a rhinoceros. The rhinoceros, except for the underside of its belly, is invulnerable. Neither the lion's teeth nor its claws make any impression on it. Proud of this natural strength, it cares nothing for even the most formidable opponent, and when it lowers its head and thrusts under the opponent's belly, it drives the fearsome horn above its nose into the belly so that the entrails spill out and the opponent is finished. Only very rarely did it happen that a lion threw a rhinoceros flat on its back and tore its belly open with its claws and teeth; otherwise it usually happened that the rhinoceros plunged its horn in and killed the lion.

But the most astonishing contest of all is this: once, in the time of King Nasir-ud-Din Haidar, the lions suffered a great humiliation against a horse. This was an extraordinary horse that surpassed even wild beasts in savagery towards men. No one dared go near it. Its fodder was pushed towards it from a distance, and whenever it got loose it killed many people. Whoever came in front of it it would kill and chew up his bones and ribs, and so mangle the corpse that it could not be recognised. In the end it was decided to set lions on it. So a lion named Bhura, who was dear to the King and had won many contests, was let loose on it. Instead of fearing the lion, the horse made ready to fight, and as soon as the lion sprang at it, it lowered its forequarters so that the lion fell on its back and drove the daggers of its claws into its hindquarters. At the same moment the horse kicked out with such force that the lion went somersaulting far away. But he recovered, and after circling about this way and that for a few minutes in a rage, he sprang again and landed on the horse. The horse did the same again and lowered its forequarters. The lion landed on its hindquarters and meant to bring it down with his paws and kill it, but this time the horse kicked out so hard that the lion's jaws were broken and he fell flat on his back far away. And the lion had so lost heart from this blow that he turned his back on the horse and began to run, and the spectators were left amazed. Then a second, bigger lion was let loose. He would not even face it. That lion too had to be taken away, and three wild buffaloes were let loose. They too would not take the horse on, and the horse went forward unprovoked and kicked one buffalo so hard that it bellowed in pain, and its two companions began to nod their heads as if applauding: "Yes! That's the way!" At last the horse's life was spared, and Nasir-ud-Din Haidar said: "I shall have an iron cage made for it, and provide for its keep too. By my father's head, it is very brave."

## (2) The cheetah

All wild beasts are kept hungry for a day or two before a fight, but with the cheetah special care has to be taken over this, because the cheetah, cruel and bloodthirsty as it is, sometimes proves equally cowardly. It is generally thought to want flattery, like a spoiled young nobleman. So in the arena it fights when it feels like it, and when it does not, try as you may, it will not fight. In a fight it goes at its opponent sidling and edging round him. First they try to wound each other with a spring. After one or two such springs both stand up on their hind legs and begin to fight with their paws. This is a very bloody fight, in which both keep growling and striking at the opponent with their paws. At last the stronger throws the weaker down and finishes him off with blow after blow, but is himself wounded from head to foot.

## (3) The leopard

The leopard is a lion on a small scale, but it is said that in Lucknow there were leopards that often fought lions, fought like doomsday and often got the better of the lions. A leopard's fighting is exactly like that of lions. As they fight both opponents are badly wounded, and the vanquished one sometimes falls dead in the arena and sometimes, beaten by the enemy, takes to flight.

## (4) The elephant

In Lucknow elephant fights were much liked and thought most entertaining, and this taste had grown so much that in the time of King Nasir-ud-Din Haidar there were a hundred and fifty fighting elephants which had nothing to do with riding. The condition for elephant fights is that they should have come into rut, for elephants will not fight until they are in rut, and if they do fight, they lack the true ardour and fury for victory and overcoming the opponent.

At the time of the fight a rope is tied from their necks to their tails. As soon as they face each other, both opponents raise their trunks and tails and, trumpeting loudly, rush at each other, and there is a tremendous collision. After this blow follows blow, and their sound carries far. Then the two put their heads together, lock tusks, and begin to shove and push each other, and the writhing and straining of their bodies shows what force they are exerting. The *mahout*s keep goading them with the elephant-hook (*ankus*) to push harder. At last one of the two elephants weakens and, unable to bear the pressure, falls to the ground. The victorious elephant then often rips open his belly with a tusk and finishes him. But it is often the habit of elephants, as soon as they weaken, to free their tusks and run, while the winner pursues. If he catches him, he knocks him down with blows and often rips his belly open with his tusks and kills him; and if he gets away, his life is saved.

In Lucknow rhinoceroses too were often set to fight elephants. But the difficulty was that these two animals would not fight each other at all, and if they ever did, there was a hard fight that lasted a long time. If ever the elephant pushed the rhinoceros over, its tusks would pierce its belly and finish it; and if the rhinoceros found a chance to drive its upper horn into the elephant's belly, the skin would be torn open a long way. But with the help of its trunk the elephant would not let the rhinoceros's horn go far into its body, and would escape a mortal wound.

## (5) The camel

Every living thing in the world can fight, but no animal can be less suited to fighting than the camel. Yet in Lucknow camels too were brought into rut and excitement and made to fight. The camel's grip is well known, and falling awkwardly is most dangerous for it. The camels' excitement shows in their frothing and foaming at the mouth. They run with froth flying, and the fight begins with abuse and spitting in each other's faces, that is, with gurgling and spraying foam. Whichever gets the chance seizes his opponent's hanging lip in his teeth and begins to pull. The camel whose lip is caught in his opponent's teeth usually falls and loses, and with that the fight ends.

## (6) The rhinoceros

No stronger animal has been created than the rhinoceros. It may be smaller in size than the lion and the elephant, but it is made so invulnerable that neither the elephant's tusks nor the lion's claws and nails have any effect on it. Only the skin of its belly is soft. If any animal can strike at that it wins; otherwise every animal tires itself out exerting its strength, and in the end the rhinoceros plunges the formidable horn on its nose into its belly and kills it.

In Lucknow rhinoceroses were set to fight elephants, lions, leopards and other rhinoceroses. In the time of King Ghazi-ud-Din Haidar, besides fighting, some rhinoceroses had been trained so well that they were yoked to carriages, and *howdah*s were strapped on their backs as on an elephant and they were ridden. The rhinoceros is not by nature a fighting animal; rather it avoids a fight as far as possible. But if it is provoked it makes ready to fight and becomes most dangerous. In the time of Nasir-ud-Din Haidar there were fifteen or twenty fighting rhinoceroses, which lived in Chandganj. When the riders drove them and set them facing each other, they lowered their heads and ran at each other, and the collisions began. Each tried to rip open the other's belly with its horn, and in this effort they went on shoving, pushing and thrusting each other for a long time. They growled very loudly and clashed horn against horn, and in the end, as they fought, they locked heads together and kept pushing the opponent, until the one who weakened slowly began to give ground and yield his place, and when even then he could not get free, he ran. But the victor would chase and strike him. At last the weaker one would disengage his horn, turn from the contest and run with all his might. If it was an enclosed arena, the victor would attack him as he ran, bring him down, plunge his horn into his belly and finish him; and if the arena was wide and open and the beaten rhinoceros could run, he would save his life by flight. Then the riders, chasing and striking the victor with hot iron bars, would stop him from pursuing the vanquished and lead him away. The whole art of a rhinoceros fight depends on keeping the head down and protecting the belly. If anyone's head goes up even by mistake, the opponent does his work. So once a rhinoceros had won and his opponent began to run. Seeing him run, the victor raised his head, and at that very moment the beaten rhinoceros ran at him like lightning, thrust his head into his belly and ripped it open.

## (7) The barasingha

This is a small, delicate and beautiful animal, and perhaps nowhere but in Lucknow has it been made to fight for amusement. But its fighting is very beautiful. The deer is the likeness of the poets' beloved, and so in its fighting too a beloved's graces appear. When they meet, the two opponents first change their stances with great beauty, and at last the clashes begin, in which they use their antlers as both sword and shield. At last, after clashing for a long time, their antlers become so entangled that it seems they are locked together. Now they go on shoving and pushing each other. In this pushing and shoving one weakens, and such terror of defeat comes over him that his delicate legs begin to tremble and his whole body shakes. But instead of taking pity, the opponent pushes harder, and pushes him to the end of the arena, that is, to the screen. Now the vanquished one is wholly without hope. Large tears fall from his eyes and drops of blood from his antlers, and he frees his antlers and turns away from the fight. Then his opponent begins to wound his body with his antlers, and the beaten *barasingha* runs with all his might. With whatever speed he runs, the victor pursues him with the same speed. This race is worth seeing. Both run like the wind, and the eye cannot follow them. But the merciless enemy does not let the vanquished go. Wherever he catches him he wounds him. At last, wounding him till he is broken, he kills him, and after he is dead he shakes the carcass with his antlers before he moves off, proud of his victory.

## (8) The ram

This is a most humble and harmless animal, but its butt is very formidable. It seems as if two mountains had collided. So people make them fight to watch the spectacle of these butts, and their fighting has been watched not only today but from ancient times. The fighting of rams began in India with the Baluchis, and from them the taste spread to other places. But the work of rearing and preparing them for fighting mostly remained with butchers and people of the lower classes. Nobles and gentlemen would have them brought before them and watch the spectacle of the fight. It is said that Nawab Asaf-ud-Daula and Saadat Ali Khan were very fond of watching ram fights. Rams were also often made to fight before Ghazi-ud-Din Haidar and Nasir-ud-Din Haidar. Wajid Ali Shah had some taste for it even during his stay in Calcutta. For his amusement Munshi-us-Sultan Bahadur often kept many pairs ready under the management of butchers, and I saw several times a formidable ram deliver such a butt that his opponent's head was split. When a ram is beaten and cannot bear his opponent's butt, he dodges it and runs. I remember that once, on the fixed annual day for viewing the King's park, hundreds of Englishmen of Calcutta were gathered. His Majesty, contrary to his custom, came out riding in a *bucha* (open sedan chair), and to please these guests ordered rams to be brought and made to fight. So the uproar of their butting rose, and the Europeans made a still greater noise, raising "Hurrahs" and cries of delight, and a scene of wonderful excitement was to be seen. In Lucknow, even after the annexation of the kingdom, Nawab Mohsin-ud-Daula Bahadur was very fond of watching ram fights. Now nobles and gentlemen have given up this pastime; it survives to an ordinary extent among the lower classes.
