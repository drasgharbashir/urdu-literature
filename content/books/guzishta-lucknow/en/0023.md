# Music under the Later Kings

## (22)

Although we wish to say something more about the music of Nasir-ud-Din Haidar and the rulers who came after him, it seems fitting first to let our readers hear the remaining part of the late Asadullah Khan Kaukab's letter, from which the opinion of an authoritative expert on the music of Lucknow will be learned.

He writes: "In the time of Wajid Ali Shah a large body of accomplished musicians had gathered in Lucknow, but the influential and titled singers of the court were not masters of the art. Only Qutb-ud-Daula, a native of Rampur, played the *sitar* well and was good at his art. Anis-ud-Daula, Musahib-ud-Daula, Wahid-ud-Daula and Rahmi-ud-Daula were singers, it is true, but not so accomplished. They had become *Daula*s [TN: holders of titles ending in *-ud-Daula*, 'of the State'] merely by royal favour. Among the masters of the art were these: Pyar Khan, Jafar Khan, Haidar Khan, Basit Khan. All these were memorials of the family of Mian Tansen. Two famous men of this family are still living: one, Wazir Khan, who is in the state of Rampur; the other, Muhammad Ali Khan, who is employed in the state of Pratapgarh. Muhammad Ali Khan's father was Basit Khan, whose name has been mentioned above."

At this point the late Kaukab Khan tells us: "My late father, Ni'matullah Khan, learned the science of music from this same Basit Khan. Ni'matullah Khan stayed with Wajid Ali Shah at Matiya Burj for about eleven years. Then after that he stayed for thirty years at the court of Nepal."

After this he writes: "In the time of Wajid Ali Shah music was much talked of, but the science of music had fallen from its high station and come down to little things. In Lucknow Kadar Piya composed *thumri*s and spread them among the people, and made music insensible. So most lovers of music abandoned the *rag*s and *ragini*s of the highest order and began to prefer Kadar Piya's *thumri*s. The decline in musical taste had begun as early as the time of Muhammad Shah Rangile, when Mian Sarang composed the *khayal*, through which the art of music became defective in principle. But far greater harm arose from Kadar's *thumri*s, and now the state of the people and the nobles was such that if they listened to music of the higher kind at all, it was not with interest and pleasure; rather they disliked it.

"Among Wajid Ali Shah's companion singers, Anis-ud-Daula and Musahib-ud-Daula had learned music from Pyar Khan, who was a very great and accomplished master. And what he taught these two pupils was certainly on a high scale; but what could be done when such music was no longer valued at court? The *rahas* that took place in the Qaisar Bagh, in which Wajid Ali Shah himself played Kanhaiya, was music of a very vulgar order. But there is no doubt that, even without real inclination, men of accomplishment were greatly valued at the royal court, the real reason being that Wajid Ali Shah too had learned the art of music from Basit Khan and had full insight into the art. With his lofty mind the King composed new *ragini*s in his own style, which in his fancy he named *Jogi Kambra*, *Juhi*, *Shah Pasand* and so on. Wajid Ali Shah held the rank of a master in this art. He was accomplished, but he cannot escape the charge that his popular taste made music in Lucknow light and easy to understand. Seeing this colour of the times, singers of refined temperament too abandoned the difficulties of the *rag*s and *ragini*s and based music on little, simple, attractive and easily understood things. *Ghazal* and *thumri* became popular among the people, and no attention at all was paid to *dhrupad*, *hori* and the like, which are very heavy and difficult things. *Khamach*, *Jhinjhoti*, *Bhairvin*, *Sendura*, *Tilak Kamod*, *Pilu* and other little delightful *ragini*s were chosen for the amusement of people of taste, and these were the very things the King liked by nature. These *ragini*s so permeated the taste of Lucknow's appreciative society that today, all over India, Lucknow's *Bhairvin* has become as famous as Lucknow's *safeda* melons. And the truth is that *Bhairvin* is Lucknow's own portion. Such *Bhairvin* is sung in no other part of India.

"The *soz-khwan*s (chanters of elegies) too spread these popular and easily understood *ragini*s, which, with the recommendation of religion, went down into the throats of even the women who stay at home, so that great accomplished singers are left like images of astonishment on hearing their dirges. Most of the *soz-khwan*s were pupils of Pyar Khan and Haidar Khan.

"*Lay* is an important element of music, which in common terms is better called 'time' or measure. Wajid Ali Shah had a very great gift for it, which should be called a gift of nature. In some degree everyone has a gift for *lay*. The metres the poets have fixed also relate to *lay*. The science of prosody is really the science of *lay*. The feet are the elements of the *tal* (rhythmic cycle). It is self-evident that in a man whose natural gift for *lay* is very great, an involuntary, rapt movement will arise in every limb and every hair, and every limb will throb to the measure. In the eyes of the common people this movement seems worthless and absurd. But the man from whom it proceeds is helpless. He does not do it on purpose; his limbs move to the measure of their own accord. It is this action of Wajid Ali Shah's of which people say that he danced, whereas he did not 'dance'; rather, absorbed in keeping time, such movements began to proceed from his limbs. Those ignorant of the principles of music began to say: the King dances. In fact Wajid Ali Shah never danced at any time. This was all his dancing was. The reason was that in keeping time not even a consummate master singer of the highest order could compete with the King. I have heard from reliable singers of his circle that even in his sleep the King's big toe moved in time.

"*Nirt*, which is called *bhao batana* (showing feeling through gesture), is also a particular part of the science of music. The purpose of *nirt* is to express what is in the mind through movements and gestures, which in English is called 'motion'. Motion is found in great and eminent speakers and lecturers, but no one makes them a target of reproach. Yet poor Wajid Ali Shah is defamed merely for his keeping of time."

This is what we learn from the late Kaukab's writing about the music of Lucknow and Wajid Ali Shah. It shows clearly that, even if Lucknow did not make music of the highest order current, it became a very great and powerful school for refining music and making it popular.

In Ghazi-ud-Din Haidar's time *qawwal*s of the highest order were famous here. Bajhu Khan and Ghulam Rasul Khan were regarded as masters of the art. Shori was so great an innovator that he is regarded as the inventor of the *tappa*. Bakhshu and Salari were regarded in those days as the masters of *tabla* playing, and no one dared touch a *tabla* against them.

In this last period Sadiq Ali Khan was regarded throughout India as an unrivalled master. The singing of the younger and the elder Manne Khan had such savour and charm that, consummate masters though they were, they captivated with their song even the common people who knew nothing of the art.

At Matiya Burj I myself heard all the *dhari*s (professional singers) employed at Wajid Ali Shah's court. Ahmad Khan, Taj Khan and Ghulam Husain Khan were regarded as the great masters of that time. Dilli Khan, who made a stir throughout Calcutta and captivated high and low with his enchanting voice, was from Lucknow and was trained in Lucknow's school of music. Besides the male singers, some courtesans in Lucknow attained such mastery that great *dhari*s took hold of their ears before them [TN: a gesture of humble acknowledgement]. Zohra and Mushtari, who were poets too, had no equal in singing. Chunewali Haidar won such renown that people counted the days waiting for Muharram to hear the *soz* (elegiac chant) from her throat. And in Muharram hundreds and thousands of enthusiasts from outside came to Lucknow and sat for hours in Haidar's *imambara*, waiting for Bi Haidar to begin her song of grief.

In *tabla* playing the master of the last period was Muhammad-ji, who was famous throughout India. About thirty years ago I met in the Chowk a Maratha gentleman, dressed in coat and trousers and employed in some respectable post. Meeting me, he said: "I have come to Lucknow solely from the wish to see the art of the masters of music here." I asked, "Who are you?" He said: "I am a hereditary singer, and my forefathers were singers at the court of Shivaji. Although, after receiving an English education, I have now taken employment, I also know my family art." By chance at that moment another gentleman came up who frequented the house of "Muhammadi", the famous singer of Lucknow. He said, "Come, come with me." The Maratha gentleman dragged me along with him too, and we all arrived at Muhammadi's. By chance Sadiq Ali Khan too was present there, and all displayed their art. The Maratha himself also sang. After this we all went to the Chaudhrain's, a house regarded here as the greatest club of the masters of the art. There both the Manne Khans were sent for. They sang and displayed their art. At last the Maratha said: "The only wish that brought me here is that I should sing a *tarana* and Muhammad-ji should play the *tabla* with me." Muhammad-ji was sent for at once, and everyone present greatly enjoyed the Maratha gentleman's singing and Muhammad-ji's playing. All were enraptured. And at the end the Maratha admitted: "I have been everywhere, but I had never till today seen with my eyes a *tabla* player more accomplished than Muhammad-ji."

Music had risen so high in Lucknow that, unlike the nobles and men of wealth of other cities, the nobles here have true taste: they understand, they recognise the tunes, *rag*s and *ragini*s, and after hearing only one or two *tan*s (runs) they understand what rank a singer is of. An ordinary singer cannot flourish in the gatherings here. Bazaar people, and the boys generally who go about singing in the streets and lanes, render various pieces in such true notes that it seems the *ragini* and the measure have gone down into their throats. In most cities you will find many people who cannot recite verses in correct metre. Here, by contrast, you will not find, search as you may, an illiterate man who cannot recite verse in correct metre. This proves that keeping time has entered the veins and sinews of every child here. Sometimes a bazaar boy has been heard singing *Bhairvin*, *Sohni*, *Bihag* or some other tune so beautifully that the listeners were rapt and great singers began to envy him.

In connection with music it seems fitting that we should also describe the instruments and apparatus of music.

In music there are two things: *sur* (pitch) and *lay* (time). Going wrong in either is an unpardonable fault in singing. So, to guard these two, just two instruments were needed. So at present the *sarangi* is used to help keep to the pitch and the *tabla* for the need to keep time.

The old Indian instrument for helping the notes was the *bin*, in which two gourds were fixed at the two ends of a hollow wooden tube, and seven strings for the seven notes were stretched over it, whose sound ran through the tube to both sides and resonated in the two gourds. The Muslims brought with them the *rabab*, the *chang* and the *sarod*. The *rabab* was probably an Arab instrument that had advanced greatly in the Abbasid period. The *chang* and *sarod* were Persian instruments. Of these the *chang* is a very ancient instrument, traces of which are found in Assyria, Babylon, Egypt, Greece and Rome, in short among all the ancient nations. The *sarod* was a purely Persian instrument, which the singers of the Abbasid period adopted and greatly developed. After coming to India, when the melodies of Hindus and Muslims mingled, first the *tambura* was invented, which was really an abridgement of the *bin* and served only to hold the notes; it was not a thing to play alone. Some time later Amir Khusrau invented the *sitar*, which was really an easy and popular adaptation of both the *bin* and the *tambura*. But neither *bin*, *tambura* nor *sitar* could fully accompany the voice. Seeing this want, Mian Sarang, the great and famous singer at the court of Muhammad Shah Rangile, invented the *sarangi*, which is named after him. The *sarangi* left the *bin*, the *tambura* and the *sitar* all behind, and won such a hold in gatherings of dance and song that even the players of those earlier instruments died out. Among these old instruments there was here also a *qanun*, which the Muslims had certainly brought with them from Syria and Iraq. Players of this too are now seen only very rarely. In short, the *sarangi* drove all of these out of the gatherings of pleasure and delight, and the state of these ancient instruments is now this, that among master singers of the highest order one occasionally sees some specialist who has mastery in playing the *bin* or the *sarod*, the *rabab* or the *qanun*. The *sitar* has remained for the amusement of young men, who play and listen to it without singing, and sometimes someone sings with it too.

There remains the *tabla*. Although it is a most necessary thing for keeping time, nothing of the kind was known among the ancient peoples of other countries. In battle the war-drum was beaten. In the *naubat* (ceremonial music) the kettledrum was played. But apart from India there was nowhere in former times anything of the kind to accompany dance and song, except the *daf* (frame drum), which the Arabs had and played with singing. Here too the *daf* seems to have been the first used with singing, played along with the *bin* and helping to keep time. After this, in ancient times, came the *mridang*, which probably existed in the time of Shri Krishna-ji, and along with the melody of his flute the boom of the *mridang* too was heard in the forest of Braj by the bank of the Jamna. After the *mridang*, the next advance was the making of the *pakhavaj*, which could accompany high music well. After this the *dhol* came into use among the common people and the women who stay at home; it derived from the *mridang* and the *pakhavaj* and became popular; and for the high gatherings of accomplished musicians in particular the *tabla* was invented, in which the two faces of the *pakhavaj* were divided between two separate instruments and became known as the *dahna* (right) and the *bayan* (left). The *tabla* is certainly an invention of the period after the coming of the Muslims, although we do not know when, or at whose hands, the advances described above were made in these instruments of time-keeping.
