# Religious Gatherings: Majlis and Maulud

## (49)

The tenth thing in the manners of society is religious gatherings, that is, the *majlis*es of mourning (*azadari*) and the gatherings of the *Maulud Sharif*. *Majlis*es are generally customary among the Shi'as and the *Maulud Sharif* among the Sunnis, although people of both parties take part in both; indeed it also happens that sometimes some Sunnis who love the Prophet's Family (*Ahl-e Bait*) hold a *majlis* of mourning, and a gathering of the *Maulud Sharif* is held at the houses of Shi'a gentlemen. But the special thing of Lucknow, which influenced the society of Lucknow and by which society was also influenced, is the *majlis*es. In the gatherings of the *Maulud* there is no special character. As they are held throughout India, so they are held here too. Although there is no doubt that at the houses of some nobles almost the same polish and culture is seen at the *Maulud* too as has come into the *majlis*es owing to the polish of the Shi'as.

[illegible] *majlis*es of mourning are held in very great numbers, and if a man wishes, and keeps finding them out, he can feed himself all year round without toil or labour merely by attending *majlis*es, and live solely on the generosity of liberal and devoted Shi'as. It is by the blessing of the *majlis*es that various kinds of *zakir*s (reciters of the sufferings) have arisen here, who, describing the sufferings of the Lord of Martyrs (upon him be peace) in different ways, weep and make others weep. First among them is the discourse of the scholars and *mujtahid*s. After them come the *hadis-khwan*s, who, recounting traditions, describe the virtues of the pure Imams and the sufferings of the Prophet's Family in such a pained and tender voice that the hearers begin to weep helplessly, and however stony-hearted one may be, one cannot restrain one's tears. Similar to them are the *waqi'a-khwan*s, who narrate the events of the sufferings of the Prophet's Family in such words and such eloquent and expressive language that one wishes to go on listening and go on weeping. The eloquence of *waqi'a-khwani* has in fact made *dastan*-telling insipid. After them are the *marsiya-khwan*s or *taht-ul-lafz-khwan*s, who recite *marsiya*s in a poetic manner. But even in this simple recitation, by the movements of eyes and eyebrows and of hands and feet, they draw so true and complete a picture of the events that the hearers, if they get respite from their tears, are compelled to applaud. It was the need and appreciation of this *marsiya*-recitation that produced Mir Anis and Mirza Dabir, who reached the highest *shah-nishin* of poetic mastery. At one time the proverb was "a failed poet becomes a *marsiya* writer", but the mastery of *marsiya*-writing in Lucknow made all India acknowledge that in the world of poetry the rank of the *marsiya* is far above the other genres of verse. Appreciation produced scores of *marsiya* writers and hundreds of *marsiya* reciters, who in Muharram and on other days of mourning go out from Lucknow and spread to distant cities of India, and come back after stamping the mark of their accomplishments on the gatherings there. After the *marsiya* reciters come the *soz-khwan*s. These people sing *nauha*s (dirges) and *marsiya*s observing the principles of music. They generally form a group of three men. Two give the drone and are called "*bazu*" (arms), and the third, who sits in the middle, sings the *soz*. These people too have advanced so far in the use of the principles of music and in rendering *rag*s and tunes that they have left the singers behind. And in Lucknow many *soz-khwan*s of such standing arose that great master singers began to hold their ears before them. In any case, the degree of mastery that the *marsiya* writers attained in poetry, the *soz-khwan*s attained in music.

All these arts arose purely by the blessing of the *majlis*es of mourning, and arose in Lucknow especially. And all of them, besides advancing Urdu letters beyond measure, produced this special quality in the world of Urdu verse and prose, that they could move human feelings as they wished, and produce whatever kind of feelings and whatever ardour they wished. This art had been systematically advanced by the Greeks, who, to make their speeches effective, had discovered by what words, what movements, what tone and what sounds the ardour of joy or grief or pity or wrath and anger can be produced in the human heart. After that no people ever paid attention to this art, until now the orators and speakers of Europe have begun to revive it. But in Lucknow, solely through *zakiri*, this art advanced of itself so far that perhaps even the Europeans have not been able to go beyond that degree.

At the close of *majlis*es it is obligatory to serve sherbet or distribute sweets or food. But cultivated and wealthy people have now adopted the very polished practice of sending the portion along with the invitation cards to the gentlemen who are to be invited. To walk back from the *majlis* with a portion in one's hand seemed to many cultivated and well-to-do people contrary to culture and very vulgar. Although the common people and the people of the bazaar see no harm in it, it weighed on the well-to-do and men of style. And if no attendant was present, many people were compelled to give their portion to some friend or poor man in the *majlis* itself.

The arrangement of the seating at a *majlis* is this: a wooden pulpit (*mimbar*) with seven or eight steps is placed at one side of the verandah or room, and people sit all round along the walls on an elaborate floor-covering. And if the gathering is large, the middle space too is filled. When enough people have gathered, the *zakir* Sahib takes his seat on the pulpit and first raises his hands and says "*Fatiha*". At once all those present raise their hands and silently recite the *Sura-e Fatiha*. After this, if he is a *hadis-khwan* or *waqi'a-khwan*, he opens his book and begins his discourse, and if he is a *marsiya-khwan*, he takes the leaves of the *marsiya* in his hand and begins to recite it. People listen to the discourse of the *mujtahid*s and *hadis-khwan*s in silence and respect and weep bitterly at the moving passages. But while listening to *marsiya*s, except at the stanzas of pathos, when they have no respite from weeping, cries of "Bravo!" and "Well done!" keep rising continually from the assembled hearers.

The *soz-khwan*s do not sit on the pulpit, but sit to one side among the people and sing *nauha*s and *marsiya*s, and often receive applause as well.

At many *majlis*es different *zakir*s recite one after another, and generally *hadis-khwani* is followed by *marsiya-khwani* and that by *soz-khwani*. Since *soz-khwani* is in fact singing, although it has become very common not only in Lucknow but throughout India, there is no *soz-khwani* at the *majlis*es of the *mujtahid*s and of serious elders who observe the law. At the *majlis*es held at the houses of the *mujtahid*s great care is taken for the observance of religion. In particular the *majlis* held here on the ninth of Muharram in the *imambara* of Ghufran-ma'ab has a special dignity and distinction, and people come from far and near eager to attend it. In it, in the course of the discourse, camels are brought before those present, on which there are litters (*kajawa*) or *mahmil*s with black coverings over them. And the faithful see the scene of how the plundered and ruined caravan of the Prophet's Family went from the plain of Karbala towards Syria in a state of oppression and suffering. This sorrowful scene has such an effect on those present that out of thousands present ten or twenty always faint, and are carried to their homes with great difficulty.

The outcome of the beginning of this dramatic quality in the *majlis*es by the family of the *mujtahid*s was that many devoted nobles began to make innovations, and some elders advanced so far as to turn the *majlis*es entirely into drama. Thus at the *majlis*es of the late Maulvi Mahdi Husain Sahib, from time to time theatre curtains were opened by means of which sorrowful scenes of the events of Karbala were set before the eyes, and a strange state of pathos came over those present. Still greater advance took place at the late gentleman's women's *majlis*es, at which thousands of women of the city gathered, and instead of the *zakir*s reciting traditions, the scenes of Karbala were shown on a stage by means of living actors and actresses.

As far as I know, the scholars and *mujtahid*s have not approved of these innovations (*bid'at*), but the interest of the common people in them keeps increasing day by day.

The real truth is that the *majlis*es of the Shi'as have had a very marked influence on the social life of Lucknow, and through them the manners of society and culture and polish have advanced very greatly. And the taste for *marsiya*s has not only revived poetry and music, but has extended a true taste for both these arts beyond the men to the respectable ladies who keep *purdah*. And I think that this thing could not have arisen in any city of Asia, except in Europe, where dance and song form part of girls' education.

Besides the *majlis*es, another kind of gathering is also held among the Shi'as, which is known by the name of "*suhbat*". Its period begins from the 9th of Rabi'-ul-Awwal, that is, the day of '*Id-e Shuja'*, and lasts for some days. Just as the *majlis*es of mourning are for weeping and shedding tears over the sufferings of the Prophet's Family, so these *suhbat*s are held for the purpose of insulting and degrading the enemies of the Prophet's Family in a dramatic manner, and abusing them without restraint. And since in the Shi'a view the greatest enemies of the Prophet's Family were the Mother of the Faithful 'A'isha Siddiqa (may God be pleased with her) and Hazrat 'Umar Faruq (may God be pleased with him), insulting these two revered names and making effigies of them and burning them with contempt and hatred has become the real object of these *suhbat*s. There is no reason for any Sunni to go to them, because they cannot tolerate the insulting of their leaders. But it is said that these are shameful gatherings of the greatest ill-breeding and obscenity, in which vulgarity advances to such a degree that even a cultivated Shi'a cannot come back without suffering spiritual pain. These *suhbat*s too have had a great influence on the taste of the Shi'as, and it is the result of this influence that fights break out between Sunnis and Shi'as over the slightest things. After these *majlis*es and *suhbat*s of the Shi'as come the *majlis*es of the *Maulud Sharif* of the Sunnis. Their seating and character are the same as those of the *majlis*es. But the difference is that among the Sunnis there is no pulpit; rather some *chauki* is set at a distinguished place, an elaborate covering is spread on it, and sitting on it the preacher or *maulud-khwan* Sahib recites the *Maulud*. The former practice was that some Maulvi Sahib would describe the circumstances of the birth of the Lord of the World, and at the mention of the birth everyone would stand up. The *maulud-khwan* Sahib would recite some poem in joy at the birth, and *kewra* would be sprinkled on the people from a rose-water sprinkler. Or if no preacher was to be had, some educated man would read out the *Maulud Sharif* of Maulvi Ghulam Imam Shahid. But this manner of *maulud* recitation did not prove satisfying to the common people, and in imitation of the *soz-khwan*s such *maulud-khwan*s arose as have two people with them to join in the drone, and sitting between them they narrate the events of the birth in a special melodious tone, and in between poems and *qasida*s (odes) are continually sung, in which both the *bazu*s accompany them. But whereas the *soz-khwan*s revived music, the truth is that the singing *maulud-khwan*s have left nothing undone in strangling music.

But as regards *maulud* recitation Lucknow has no special character, because *maulud* recitation in the same style and of the same kind is current among the Sunnis throughout India. And the same is the case with the gatherings of *hal-o-qal* (ecstasy and recitation) of the Sunni Sufis, which are the same everywhere. From the very time of the Muslims' coming to India the foundation of the *majlis-e sama'* (gathering for listening to music) was laid, but apart from the rise of a class of *qawwal*s, who are considered lower in rank and musical knowledge than the *dhari*s and singers, the art of music could gain no marked benefit from it. Whereas *soz-khwani* within a single century made music its handmaid and began to dispose of it with sovereign authority.
