# The Language of Every Class: Wit, Storytelling and Speech

## (12)

But the advances the Urdu language made in Lucknow are not confined to poets, men of letters, prose writers and authors. In different circles and classes new forms of the growth and spread of the language arose, which created particular pleasures for the people of every group.

The most notable of these is *dastan-goi* (the art of the storyteller), which is really the name for composing extempore. This art properly belongs to the Arabs, among whom gatherings for storytelling were held even in the Jahiliyya. But as for the storytelling of India, we do not know whether it has any connection with the story-reciting of Arabia. The *Dastan of Amir Hamza*, which is the real and true arena of the storytellers, was originally in Persian, and it is said that an able man named Amir Khusrau composed it in the time of the Emperor Akbar. History proves that the *Dastan of Amir Hamza* already existed in the Tughluq period [TN: the Urdu reads "لوگ تغلق", evidently a misprint; the sense is "in the Tughluq period"].

The famous storytellers of Delhi began to come to Lucknow. Here the opium-eaters valued them so highly that they made listening to stories a principal element of their gatherings. Within a few days this so flourished in Lucknow that there was no man of wealth in whose establishment a storyteller was not employed. Hundreds of storytellers arose. The truth is that not one of our most popular speakers of today has yet attained the degree of eloquence that the masterly storytellers possessed. In Delhi too one or two accomplished storytellers survive to this day, but in Lucknow their number is much greater, and their manner of speaking has had an enormous effect on the speech of the common people of the city. After the taste for novels arose, when efforts were made to have the *dastan* written down in the storytellers' own language, it was Lucknow alone that could produce storytellers accomplished enough to write huge volumes and spread them among the Urdu-reading public. So the works of Jah and Qamar are held in high esteem in the country.

Four arts of the *dastan* have been established: *razm* (battle), *bazm* (feasting and revelry), *husn-o-ishq* (beauty and love), and *ayyari* (trickery).

In all four of these arts the storytellers of Lucknow have shown such mastery as cannot be imagined without seeing and hearing it. To paint pictures in words, and to leave the deepest, most lasting impression of those pictures on the hearts of the listeners, is the special skill of these men.

Under the heading of social pleasantry, humour and fun too, several arts of language arose in Lucknow in which no place can compete with Lucknow. One of these is the art of the *phabti* (the cutting comparison). This is really related to the poetic simile and metaphor, but its distinctive feature is that it is limited to caricaturing someone, bringing out his defect, and producing on the spot some odd, laughable comparison that exposes his fault or flaw. In Lucknow the lowliest boys, women of the bazaar, illiterate shopkeepers, and even craftsmen of the lowest classes come out with such apt *phabti*s that outsiders are astonished. A gentleman came back from pilgrimage to Karbala the Exalted, put on spotless white clothes, and had just come and sat down among his friends when a lad said: "Aha! Where has this heron of the Euphrates come from?" An old bridegroom dyed his hair and came to marry a bride, bringing a very grand wedding procession. He was coming out of the women's quarters into the gathering. He bent down to take off his shoes and went a few steps on the carpet on his knees. Someone said, "Where is the bridegroom?" The pert courtesan who stood performing her *mujra* (dance) laughed and said, "Why, there he comes, crawling along on all fours." [TN: *menun menun*, rendered here as "crawling along on all fours", is uncertain.] A hunchback was selling sugar cane (*paunda*) in the Chowk, and his cry was, "Hey, brothers, who'll catch the kites with these?" Can there be a more witty metaphor than this? The most delicate metaphor is one in which neither the thing compared nor that to which it is compared is named, and charm is brought into speech merely by naming some property of the latter. What better example of this could there be? He named neither the sugar cane nor the long pole with which kites are caught, and simply by saying "Who'll catch the kites?" told you that these canes were the best kind of poles with which kites are caught. And no comparison could be more apt or more to the taste of the people of the bazaar. There are hundreds and thousands of examples like this, which can be heard at every moment in the gatherings here.

The second is *zila* (punning on associated words). This is really the poetic *ri'ayat* (play on associated words), which has entered the talk and banter of ordinary people and taken on a special colour. In *zila* the effort is to bring into the conversation, from one angle or another, all the things associated with whatever has been mentioned. Azad Faqir, who had a particular manner, was considered a master of *zila*. In his poetry Amanat strove so hard for *ri'ayat* that, disregarding all poetic merits, he made *ri'ayat* alone his aim. The result was that his verse left the rank of poetry and entered the science of *zila*. But in their informal gatherings most of the common people of Lucknow have carried this art so far that Amanat's poetry has been left behind. The truth is that the people of no other place can come within a tenth of a tenth of the level of the people of Lucknow in *zila*. A book on this art has also been published.

The third art is *tukbandi* (impromptu rhyming). This is the rhyme-mongering of poetry. When many illiterate people turn to it, they use rhymes extempore in answer and question in such a way that great poets are astonished. In our student days we saw a Hindu seller of *budhiya ke kate* [TN: a spun-sugar sweet, literally "old woman's yarn"], who used to go out in the morning with his tray. As soon as he appeared, hundreds of bazaar lads would surround him, and he would set his tray down in the road and sit. At once a contest in *tukbandi* would begin between him and the lads. The whole crowd would be on one side. There was a shower of abuse on both sides, but the rule was that no abuse should be out of rhyme and no rhyme be missed. We saw him scores of times. The contest with him went on for hours, but we never saw him at a loss for an answer. He would always find some rhyme or other and bring it out.

In the same way all sorts of flights of fancy arose in banter and conversation, and the illiterate common people would sometimes come out with ideas that left great poets amazed. This was in truth Lucknow's golden age. Poetry and literary graces had entered people's very veins and sinews. Everyone who acquired an ordinary smattering of reading began to try his hand at verse. Even among the illiterate and common people, the lower classes and the women who stayed at home, a poetic suppleness and literary delicacies had arisen. Unlettered cloth-sellers were poets, and the speech of even the illiterate was so polished and refined, so full of words that kept due regard for rank, and so brimming with social courtesies, that learned men were often amazed to hear them talk, and no one would have suspected them of being illiterate. The cries of street vendors were so adorned and embellished with poetic subtleties and the niceties of eloquence and rhetoric that it was hard for others even to understand them.

The lower classes too had created particular literary pleasures to suit their own taste. For example, an art of *khayal* arose. People would compose verses extempore and sing them to the *daira* (frame drum). It was named *khayal* (fancy) because each person was to show the power of his imagination and produce something new. Many masters of this art arose here who had no connection with high society and the gatherings of the educated, but which, if you consider it in itself, was real and natural poetry, poetry of the very kind that existed in the Jahiliyya of Arabia.

In the same way a group of *dande-vale* (stick-beaters) arose. Their way was to put into verse, with perfect freedom, the important and famous events of the recent past. Whatever a man was like, however great his influence and wealth, they would show him as he was with great boldness, and prove what benefit the country and the community had had from him, or how great an injury he had done. Then they would recite these poems in a particular style of verse recitation, beating time with sticks.

In every country and every nation the speech of women is more polished and charming than that of men, but in Lucknow it was a special feature that the speech of the honoured begums of the palaces and of respected families had acquired, besides feminine charms, literary and poetic delicacies. When they spoke it seemed that flowers were falling from their lips, and if you paid attention, the correctness of their words, their lovely turns of phrase and the delicacies of their manner of expression showed to what high perfection the beauties of the language had reached in this land.
