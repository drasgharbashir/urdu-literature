# Food and the Kitchen

## (28)

In human social life the most necessary and most important thing is eating and drinking, and when any group or nation advances, its taste first displays its refinement and inventiveness at the dining cloth (*dastarkhwan*). So now we wish to show what colour the Eastern court of Lucknow displayed in the kitchen and at the dining cloth, what novelties it devised, and to what degree the people here advanced in this art.

The history of the civilisation of Awadh begins with Shuja-ud-Daula, and indeed with his last period, that is, from the time when, defeated at the battle of Buxar and having made a new treaty with the English, he settled down quietly and turned away from military activity. At that time the superintendent of his kitchen was Hasan Raza Khan, known as Mirza Hasnu, who was of a respected and noble family that had come from Delhi. Maulvi Fazl-e-Azim, a Shaikhzada of Safipur in the district of Unnao who had come to Lucknow as a student, was fortunate enough to gain entry to Mirza Hasnu's house and had grown up with him, brought up and playing together. Mirza Hasnu had had him appointed on his behalf as deputy superintendent of the kitchen. His custom was to put the trays of the Nawab's meal (*khasa*) in order, seal them with his seal, take them to the Nawab's palace gate and hand them over to the particular maidservants of Bahu Begum Sahiba's household, Dhaniya, Batiya and Maina. Solely so that these maidservants would allow no move against him, the Maulana had made them his sworn sisters. And so these maidservants were of use to him on very delicate occasions.

It was Nawab Shuja-ud-Daula's custom to eat inside the palace with his wife, Bahu Begum Sahiba. The maidservants would carry the trays before the Begum Sahib and open them, and the food would be laid out on the dining cloth.

Every day food came for the Nawab and the Begum from six kitchens. (First) from the principal Nawabi kitchen mentioned above, of which Mirza Hasnu was superintendent, and from which Maulvi Fazl-e-Azim himself brought the trays of the meal to the palace gate. In this kitchen two thousand rupees' worth of cooking was done daily, which means that, apart from the salaries of the cooks and other servants, sixty thousand rupees a month, or seven hundred and twenty thousand rupees a year, was spent on the cost of dishes and foods alone. (Second) from the government's small kitchen, of which the superintendent was at first Mirza Hasan Ali, superintendent of the wardrobe (*tosha-khana*), but which was afterwards made over to Ambar Ali Khan, the eunuch. In it three hundred rupees a day, that is, a hundred and eight thousand rupees a year, was spent on preparing food. (Third) the kitchen inside Bahu Begum Sahiba's own palace, of which the superintendent was Bahar Ali Khan, the eunuch. (Fourth)

By today's taste this would not be considered any merit in food; but in that age, and still among eaters of the old taste, the real standard of excellence in food is that dishes should look refined and delicate but in fact be so rich and heavy on the stomach that not every stomach can bear them.

The second accomplishment was to present one thing in various forms and make it appear that on the dining cloth there were scores of different dishes, while if you tasted and considered, they were all one and the same thing. For example, it is heard from reliable sources that one of the princes of Delhi, Mirza Asman Qadr, son of Mirza Khurram Bakht, came to Lucknow and became a Shia, and after staying here for some time went and settled at Benares. While he was staying in Lucknow, Wajid Ali Shah invited him to a meal. A preserve (*murabba*) was brought and set on the dining cloth, which looked most refined, delicate and fine. When Mirza Asman Qadr took a mouthful of it he was bewildered, for it was not a preserve but a savoury *qorma* of meat, which the *rikabdar* (master cook) had made look exactly like a preserve. He was embarrassed at being deceived like this, and Wajid Ali Shah was delighted to have deceived an honoured prince of Delhi.

A few days later Mirza Asman Qadr invited Wajid Ali Shah, and Wajid Ali Shah came thinking that he would certainly be deceived. But for all his wariness he was deceived, for Asman Qadr's cook, Shaikh Husain Ali, had performed this feat: although hundreds of dishes and every kind of food were laid out on the dining cloth, *pulao*, *zarda*, *biryani*, *qorma*, kebabs, vegetables, chutneys, pickles, breads, *paratha*s, *shirmal*s, in short every good thing, whatever one tasted was made of sugar. If there was curry, it was of sugar; if rice, of sugar; if pickle, of sugar; and if bread, of sugar. Indeed it is said that all the dishes, the dining cloth, and even the basin and ewer were of sugar. Wajid Ali Shah, in growing confusion, put his hand to one thing after another and was deceived again and again.

We have said that trays of food came to Nawab Shuja-ud-Daula Bahadur's meal from six places, but this was not confined to him. After him too the practice continued that many honoured nobles, especially royal kinsmen, were given the honour of regularly sending particular kinds of dishes for the royal meal.

So our friend Nawab Muhammad Shafi Khan Sahib Bahadur Nishapuri relates that from the house of his maternal grandfather, Nawab Agha Ali Hasan Khan Sahib, who was the most famous and distinguished of the Nishapuris, *raughani roti* (rich flatbread) and sweet *ghee* used to go to the King. The *raughani* breads were cooked so thin and so finely that they were no thicker than heavy paper, and yet it was impossible for them to be spotted with burns, nor dare they be left raw anywhere. The sweet *ghee* too was a special thing, prepared with great care.

In Delhi *biryani* is and was more common, but the refinement of Lucknow preferred *pulao*. In the eyes of the common people the two are almost the same, indeed one; but in *biryani* the excess of spices gives it the character of rice mixed with curry, while in *pulao* such delicacy, refinement and cleanness are thought necessary that beside it *biryani* seems a hotchpotch. There is no doubt that *biryani* seems better than an ordinary *pulao*. Such a *pulao* seems like plain boiled rice, a fault *biryani* does not have. But in the eyes of the refined, compared with a *pulao* of the highest class, *biryani* is a very heavy and unsightly food. It was just this difference that made *pulao* more current in Lucknow.

Here seven kinds of *pulao* are said to be famous. Of these too we now remember only the names *gulzar pulao*, *nur pulao*, *koko pulao*, *moti pulao* and *chambeli pulao*. But the fact is that on the finest dining cloths here there used to be scores of kinds of *pulao*. Muhammad Ali Shah's son, Mirza Azim-ush-Shan, gave a feast for the meeting of the two fathers-in-law (*samdhi milap*) on the occasion of a wedding, at which the ruler of the time, Wajid Ali Shah himself, was present. At this feast there were seventy kinds of rice in all, savoury and sweet, on the dining cloth.

In the time of King Ghazi-ud-Din Haidar there was a noble of the family of Nawab Salar Jang, Nawab Husain Ali Khan. He was very fond of food, especially *pulao*. Scores of kinds of *pulao* were served at his table, and they were prepared with such refinement and delicacy that he became famous throughout the city, so that none of the nobles and grandees dared compete with him. The King himself envied him. And among lovers of food he became known as "the Rice Man" (*Chaval-vale*).

In the time of Nasir-ud-Din Haidar a cook came from outside who cooked a *khichri* of pistachios and almonds. He made shapely, clean grains of rice out of almonds and prepared a *dal* (lentils) of pistachios, and cooked it so finely that it looked like a very good, fine, pure *khichri* of *mash* (black gram); but when you ate it, it had quite another savour, a taste whose flavour the tongue would never forget all its life.

In Nawab Saadat Ali Khan's time an accomplished cook cooked only a *galathi* of rice, but such a *galathi* as was the glory of the royal dining cloth, greatly liked by the ruler of the time, and every noble of the city longed to get a mouthful of it. It is said that a new cook was presented before Nawab Asaf-ud-Daula. He was asked, "What do you cook?" He said, "I cook only *mash dal*." He was asked, "What salary will you take?" He said, "Five hundred rupees." The Nawab took him into service. But he said, "I shall serve on a few conditions." He asked, "What are those conditions?" He said: "When Your Highness desires *dal* from my hand, let the order be given a day beforehand, and when I report that it is ready, let Your Highness eat it at once." The Nawab accepted these conditions too. Some months later he was ordered to cook *dal*. He prepared it and informed the Nawab. He said, "Very well, spread the cloth, I am coming." The cloth was spread, but the Nawab stayed engaged in conversation. The cook went and reported again, "The meal is ready." Again the Nawab was late in coming. He reported a third time, and when even then the Nawab Sahib did not come, he took up the pot of *dal*, emptied it at the root of a dry tree, resigned and went away. The Nawab was sorry. He had him searched for, but no trace of him was found. But a few days later it was seen that the tree under which the *dal* had been thrown had turned green.

No doubt there is exaggeration in this story, which has carried it to the point of being incredible; but it does give an idea of how highly cooks were valued at court, and with what generosity an accomplished cook was retained when one came.

Seeing this taste and enthusiasm of the nobles, the cooks too began to devise all kinds of novelties. Someone invented the *anardana* (pomegranate-seed) *pulao*. In it each grain of rice was half red and lustrous like a ruby and half white, but with a diamond-like sparkle in that too. When it was brought and set on the dining cloth it seemed as though particoloured jewels had been placed on the plate. Another cook cooked and presented a *nauratan* (nine-jewel) *pulao*, in which he mixed rice of nine colours like the famous nine jewels, and the clearness and lustre of the colours produced a wonderful refinement and delight. In the same way God knows how many inventions were made, which spread to every household and kitchen.

Among the nobles of old who loved food was Nawab Mirza Khan Nishapuri, who, it is said, received a pension of fourteen thousand a month. In his love of good food he showed such accomplishment, and gathered such fine cooks, that his table was the talk of the city. Another was Mirza Haidar. He too was a Nishapuri, and so respected a noble that all the Nishapuris regarded him as their crown and elder. His style was that to whatever feast he went, his water-cooling department, his supply of *gilauri*s (folded betel leaves) and a hundred or a hundred and fifty hookahs went with him. This style of his was a great help to many people of middling means. By flattery and entreaty one way or another they would get him to accept an invitation, and his accepting meant that the arrangements for hookahs, *gilauri*s and water at the gathering became his responsibility; and what arrangements, such as were beyond the means of even the greatest noble!

There are three groups who prepare food. First the *degshu*s (pot-washers), whose work is to wash the cauldrons and labour under the cooks. Second the *bavarchi*s (cooks). These people cook the food, and usually prepare and take down the great cauldrons. Third the *rikabdar*s. These are the masters and accomplished experts of the highest order in this art. They generally cook in small pots, and think taking down great cauldrons a task beneath their dignity and rank. Although many *bavarchi*s also cook in small pots, the work of the *rikabdar*s was confined to small pots alone. They cut flowers out of fruits and nuts, and showed taste, refinement and elegance in serving and arranging food. On the *pulao* or *zarda* served in dishes and platters they made floral designs and patterns with dried fruits and other means. They prepared very fine and delicate preserves and pickles, and with their ingenuity displayed hundreds of kinds of artistry in the food.

Ghazi-ud-Din Haidar, the first King of Awadh, liked *paratha*s. His *rikabdar* cooked six *paratha*s every day, and took thirty seers of *ghee* daily at the rate of five seers a *paratha*. One day the minister of state, Mo'tamid-ud-Daula Agha Mir, called the royal *rikabdar* and asked, "Look here, my good man, what becomes of these thirty seers of *ghee*?" He said, "Your Honour, I cook *paratha*s." He said, "Well, cook them in front of me." He said, "Very good!" He cooked the *paratha*s. As much *ghee* as was absorbed was absorbed, and what was left he threw away. Seeing this, Mo'tamid-ud-Daula Agha Mir said in astonishment and surprise, "But the whole of the *ghee* has not been used?" He said, "Now this *ghee* has become nothing but oil. It is hardly fit to be used in any other dish, is it?" The minister had no answer, but he ordered, "From now on only five seers of *ghee* is to be given; one seer a *paratha* is plenty." The *rikabdar* said, "Very well, I shall cook with that much *ghee*." But he was so angry at the minister's interference that he began to cook ordinary *paratha*s and send them to the King's table. When this went on for several days the King complained, "What sort of *paratha*s are these that come now?" The *rikabdar* said, "Your Majesty, I cook the *paratha*s as Nawab Mo'tamid-ud-Daula Bahadur orders." The King asked the truth of it, and he told the whole story. Mo'tamid-ud-Daula was sent for at once. He said, "Refuge of the World! These people plunder for nothing." In reply the King gave him a dozen slaps and punches, beat him soundly and said: "And you don't plunder? You, who are plundering the whole kingdom and the whole country, think nothing of that; and this man, who takes a little more *ghee*, and that too for my table, you cannot bear?" In any case Mo'tamid-ud-Daula repented and pulled his own ears, and was given a robe of honour, which was considered a sign that His Majesty had passed a kindly hand over him, and he came home. After that he never interfered with that *rikabdar* again, and he went on taking thirty seers of *ghee* a day as before.
