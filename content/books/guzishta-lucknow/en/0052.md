# Tobacco, Chikni Dali and the Pandan

## (51)

Before the improvement of the tobacco leaf, the credit for which goes to our honoured friend Munshi Sayyid Ahmad Husain Sahib, another successful attempt at improvement had been made. That was this: the tobacco leaf and stalks are boiled thoroughly and their liquor extracted, and by cooking and cooking it is made so thick that it takes the form of paste or fresh opium. Then, mixing musk, *kewra* and many suitable perfumes into it, it is made so delicate and fragrant that if you eat a grain's weight of this *qiwam* (tobacco paste) with your *pan*, along with the taste of tobacco there is fragrance in the mouth all day. Then refinement of temperament advanced further on this; that is, tiny pills are made of this *qiwam*, each pill the amount of one dose. Then silver or gold leaf is wrapped round the pills, making them so handsome and charming that it seems pearls have been set out. A certain Begam Sahiba of Muftiganj used to make *qiwam* and pills without equal. The true people of Lucknow liked no factory's pills but those made by her hand. But in her own time the factory of Asghar Ali Muhammad Ali prepared both these things and presented them to all India. A few days later that Begam Sahiba died, and the *qiwam* and pills of Asghar Ali's factory came into use everywhere. Afterwards many other people and several factories prepared these things under their own management, but none has yet been able to get ahead of the late Asghar Ali's factory. But there was one fault in *qiwam* and pills, which was that, although the fragrance might last long, the taste of the tobacco and its bitterness went with the very first spit. It was to remove this fault that Munshi Sayyid Ahmad Husain Sahib invented this new processed and perfumed leaf, whose bitterness and fragrance stay with the *pan* to the end. And it is the result of this merit that the world suddenly turned towards it, and *qiwam* and pills, although they are still made, have become last year's almanac; and if this rate of decline in the taste for them continues, it may be expected that in a short time they will disappear entirely.

Connected with *pan*, or in keeping with it, some other inventions were made in Lucknow: that is, such cardamoms were invented that if you eat one cardamom the mouth becomes redder than with *pan*. Although in preparing them the ingredients of *pan* itself are used, which, with colour added, are filled into the husks of the cardamom, yet, though the colour comes out strong, they cannot be a substitute for *pan*, and the purpose of eating *pan* cannot be achieved by these artificial cardamoms. In the same way another kind of cardamom was prepared in which *missi* (black tooth powder) is filled, and if women, instead of sitting a long time rubbing on *missi*, put one cardamom of this kind in their *pan* and eat it, the *missi* applies itself, and settles deep blue-black well into the gaps of the teeth. But by neither of these kinds of cardamom could the purpose for which they were invented be well achieved. For example, the red cardamoms cannot be a substitute for *pan*, and the black cardamoms do not have the fragrance of good perfumed *missi*; so they could not become generally liked and popular, and to this day no necessary use, such as is a requisite of social life, can be made of them except for jest and amusement.

In this connection we should also describe the *chikni dali* (boiled betel nut), which, if not an inseparable part of *pan*, is certainly among its accessories. Some people eat it in their *pan* instead of ordinary betel nut, and if not in *pan*, many people put it in the mouth by itself, where it gives great pleasure along with a cardamom. Especially, since Hindu friends cannot eat a *gilauri* from the hand of Muslims, their entertainment and hospitality is done with *chikni dali* and cardamom alone. So it too has become a necessary article of social life.

*Chikni dali* is in fact the same betel nut that is put in *pan*, but processed and improved. It is not made in Lucknow or Delhi or Hyderabad or other civilised cities, but comes ready-made from where it grows. It is said that the original nut is put in milk and boiled and cooked. Well, however it is made, a mucilage arises in it, its dryness goes and an oiliness comes, and the fault by which a choking (*phanda*) sometimes catches the throat from eating too much betel nut does not remain at all in the *chikni dali*. And the truth is that it becomes many times more tasty, delicate and fine than ordinary betel nut.

As far as I know, the use of *chikni dali* is much greater in Hyderabad, Delhi and other cities than in Lucknow, and it was the business of the fanciers of those places to make some kind of improvement in it or to advance it to their taste. But it is surprising that no city paid attention to this, and if anyone improved *chikni dali* too, it was the people of Lucknow. The real kernel of *chikni dali* is very delicate, tasty and tender, and the part that adjoins the husk remains somewhat astringent; especially the part on the *chandi* side [TN: apparently the crown of the nut] is very defective. With the idea of removing these faults and taking out the defective part, several kinds of ordinary *chikni dali*, cut and trimmed, began to be prepared in Lucknow. First of all are those called *do-rukhi* (two-faced). The manner of making them is that the greater part is cut from below and above, and a little from the edges all round, making handsome and well-coloured little cups, in which there remains only that soft and delicate kernel which is the best part of the *chikni dali*. *Chikni dali*s of the second grade are called *yak-rukhi* (one-faced). In them too, although there is some cutting and trimming all round, of the two defective parts below and above, the greater part of one side is left. The third kind is that handsome octagonal pieces are made of the kernel of the *chikni dali*. The dust that comes out of this cutting and trimming is sold separately, and in fact in Lucknow it has become the fifth kind of processed *chikni dali*. Then it too has come to have two or three kinds, because the dust that comes from the *do-rukhi* and *yak-rukhi* nuts is kept separate, and there is a great difference in the delicacy, softness and taste of the two, and for this reason there is also a difference as of earth and heaven in their prices. In short, although *chikni dali* is not so much a thing of the fancy of the people of Lucknow, they improved it too to a degree that had not been achieved anywhere.

Now, since the ingredients of *pan* are finished, we turn to its vessels and implements. The most important thing for keeping the equipment of *pan* is the *pandan*; say that the *pandan* is the battery of that moral and amorous attraction, like electric power, which is in *gilauri*s of *pan*. In former times, especially in Delhi, there used to be the *pitari* (box), which was of all shapes, round, square or octagonal, and probably it was from Delhi that *pitari*-like *pandan*s went to Hyderabad, an imitation of which are those square *pandan*s of tin and glass which at weddings in Hyderabad are distributed among the guests with the greatest generosity, filled with lime, *katha*, betel nut, *chikni dali*, cardamoms, cloves, *pan* and so on. In any case, the old *pandan*s were at first *pitari*s, and it was with these *pitari*s that the respected ladies came from Delhi to Lucknow two or two and a half centuries ago. Here, so long as the imitation of Delhi lasted, the same *pitari*s remained. But from the day the people of Lucknow began their own cutting and shaping in their style, social life and language, from that day the design of the *pandan* too began to change. First only round tinned copper *pitari*s were adopted for keeping *pan*. Then height and roundness began to come into their lids. In a few days their shape became like a broad silver dome, on which, in place of a finial, an elongated ring is fixed for a handle. Both ends of the ring are fitted into staples, so that instead of standing upright it lies this way or that.

Inside this *pandan* are the *kulhiya*s (small pots) for *katha* and lime, whose shape is exactly like small *patili*s (cooking pots). In line with these *kulhiya*s are three equal large boxes (*dibiya*), in some of which whole, and in some cut, betel nuts and *chikni dali* are kept. These three boxes and the two *kulhiya*s are set in an even ring, and in the space left between them, that is, at the centre, another small box is placed, in which cardamoms or cloves are generally kept. The lids of the boxes are tight; they cannot open of themselves, but need a little force to open. But the lids of the *kulhiya*s are like small plates, which are placed over their mouths. In the *katha* and lime *kulhiya*s are the little spoons for applying *katha* and lime, on whose ends sometimes a peacock is made and sometimes they are plain. Above these *kulhiya*s is a large tray the size of the whole *pandan*, in which the *pan* leaves are kept wrapped in cloth. In earlier days there used to be another separate lidded vessel in the shape of a *pan* leaf in which *pan* leaves were kept; it was called the *nagardan*. But experience proved it unnecessary and defective, because when they were shut up in it the air did not reach them and the leaves spoiled. For this reason, although the *nagardan* is still seen in some old *pandan*s, its use has in fact been entirely given up, and soon it will be as rare as the phoenix ('*anqa*).

In a few days the *pandan* began to serve women as box, treasury and cash box. And the truth is that for women in India it was the bag (*zanbil*) of 'Amr the trickster [TN: the magic bag of 'Amr 'Ayyar in the *dastan* of Amir Hamza]. Out of this need it began to grow in capacity and bulk, until *pandan*s of ten and twenty seers began to be made. And then it was absolutely necessary that it should accompany one wherever one went, because, in accordance with "*shutur ba-miqdar-e 'alam*" (the camel in proportion to its banner), the bigger the *pandan*, the greater the standing and dignity of the Begam Sahiba were considered. The result was that the *pandan* took up the whole space in the *doli*, and the Begam Sahiba found room to sit only after much squeezing and shrinking. In any case, *pandan*s kept advancing daily in weight and size, when suddenly the love of brevity invented a new kind of small, tall, domed *pandan* with a finial (*kalas*), which were at first called *aram-dan* but are now generally known by the name of *husn-dan*. Inside them are the same things as in the *pandan*, but the outer shape is like a handsome dome with a finial, and instead of by a ring it is lifted by holding that same finial or top. These *husn-dan*s were generally liked. In Lucknow and in other cities too the demand for them grew. In Lucknow they were first adopted by men, or by those people who do not like display and show. But in a few days they became general. And although *pandan*s of the former style have not disappeared, it is now mostly *husn-dan*s that are in use. And in houses where *pandan*s do remain, they are not so big but small. Now *husn-dan*s of the same Lucknow style have begun to be made in Moradabad too, but they are more spread out and not so beautiful as those made in Lucknow. The proportion of the *husn-dan*s of Lucknow is a thing peculiar to this place, and for the craftsmen of any other place it is almost impossible to maintain this proportion.

After the *pandan* comes the *khasdan*. This is the vessel in which *gilauri*s are placed and brought into a gathering or the company of friends. In Delhi this work is done by an open tray, on one side of which cut betel nut is placed, and on the other half-leaves of *pan* with lime and *katha* applied, doubled, that is, folded over. And since this same tray is still customary there, it may be supposed that in former times too this was the way of bringing *pan* into company. But in Lucknow *gilauri*s are made of at least two leaves, which at first were well compacted in the shape of a *singhara* (water chestnut), but now are generally *bira*s, and their shape is like the paper stopper made for putting in bottles. Then pins are put in to hold them. At first cloves were put in. Afterwards a cluster (*lachchha*) of chains was invented. The form of the cluster is that many chains, with pins fixed in them, are attached all round a small silver box or mango-shaped perfume holder. This whole cluster is placed in the *khasdan* together with the *pan*s. But considering this a prolixity, it became the custom to put iron pins into the *gilauri*s. But now the best method has been invented, that a sheath of *pan* leaf itself is drawn over the *gilauri*, which keeps it from opening.

In any case a tray alone was not suitable for these *gilauri*s, so a domed lid with a finial was invented for this tray, which the tray would hold. The lid also gave the *khasdan* the form of a small *husn-dan*.
