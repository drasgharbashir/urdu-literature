# Calligraphy

## (16)

Bound up with the sciences are the arts of calligraphy and writing. The old script of the Muslims was the Arabic one called *naskh*. Until the middle ages of the Baghdad Caliphate this was the script throughout the Islamic world from East to West; it had developed from the old script of the land of Hira into the *Kufic* script, and from *Kufic* into *naskh*. From the time of the Tahirid dynasty all the sciences and arts that were flourishing in Baghdad began to come towards Iran and Khurasan, and in the time of the Dailamites and the Seljuqs most of the accomplishments of Baghdad were fully gathered in Iran. Above all, through the learned taste and refined temperament of the Dailamites, Iran's western province of Azerbaijan, which lay naturally in the lap of Persian Iraq and Arab Iraq, became the cradle of every kind of excellence and advancement.

It was in this region that the script first began to take a new form. Writing passed beyond the bounds of calligraphy and entered the realm of painting, and painterly delicacies began to be brought into it. The refined Persians saw clumsiness in the old plainness of the Arab script, and its old style and form began of themselves to be abandoned. In *naskh* the pen stayed uniform in every letter and word from beginning to end. The letters had ill-proportioned curves and unbalanced irregularity. The bowls were not round but low and flat, and corners formed in them here and there. Now the delicacy of painting was joined to calligraphy, and fine points and finish began to be given to writing. The tips, necks and tails of the letters began to be made thin. The bowls began to be written beautiful and round. Keeping this new taste fully in view, Mir Ali Tabrizi, who was a native of the province of Azerbaijan itself, first made this new script principled and regular, brought it into use in the eastern lands, and named it *nasta'liq*, which was originally *naskh-ta'liq*, that is, an appendage of *naskh*.

It is not known in what period Mir Ali Tabrizi lived. Munshi Shams-ud-Din Sahib, today the famous and authoritative calligrapher of Lucknow, puts him before Timur. But books in *nasta'liq* are found so old that, never mind Timur, we think this script had been invented even before Mahmud of Ghazni. No doubt with Mahmud's day Persian calligraphers too must have begun to come to India, under whose influence the use of this script began here, and calligraphers of *nasta'liq* arose in great numbers in every province and region of India. So either Mir Ali Tabrizi's time was very ancient, or he was not the real inventor of the script. But there is no doubt that the present calligraphy of Delhi and Lucknow, indeed of all India, names Mir Ali Tabrizi as its first master. A long time after him, in Iran, the name of Mir Imad-ul-Hasani became famous as a master of *nasta'liq*; among calligraphers he is regarded as a most distinguished and renowned scribe and a consummate master. His nephew Agha Abdur-Rashid Dailami came to India at the time of Nadir Shah's invasions and settled at Lahore. In Lahore hundreds of pupils of his arose, who spread through the regions of India and proved him, if not the Adam, then certainly the Noah of Indian calligraphy.

Two of his pupils, who were from Persia, came to Lucknow. Of these two elders one was Hafiz Nurullah and the other Qazi Ni'matullah. It is said that a third accomplished pupil of Agha Abdur-Rashid named Abdullah Beg also came to Lucknow. These gentlemen probably came in the time of Nawab Asaf-ud-Daula Bahadur, when no master who came here was allowed to go back. As soon as he arrived, Qazi Ni'matullah was appointed to correct the writing of the princes, and Hafiz Nurullah too became attached to the court of Awadh, and the two of them settled here and began to teach calligraphy to the people.

Besides these elders there were also other old calligraphers here, one renowned elder among whom is said to have been Munshi Muhammad Ali. But the pupils of Agha Abdur-Rashid established their authority so firmly that all lovers of calligraphy, indeed the whole city, turned to them. Whoever had a taste for calligraphy became their pupil, and the names of all the earlier calligraphers were wiped out and sank into the shoreless ocean of oblivion. And in truth these elders deserved it by their mastery.

The esteem in which Hafiz Nurullah was held in Lucknow cannot be judged merely from the fact that he entered the government's service here. The true measure of Lucknow's appreciation is that people bought the specimens (*qit'a*) written in his hand at the price of pearls, so that even his ordinary practice sheets sold in the bazaar from hand to hand at the rate of a rupee a letter.

In those days nobles and people of taste decorated their houses with calligraphic panels instead of pictures, because of which there was in general an enormous demand for them, and wherever a panel in the hand of a good calligrapher was found, people fell on it like moths and pressed it to their eyes. Society gained from this in that moral principles and admonitory phrases or verses were always before the eyes, and a moral lesson was always to be had at home; and calligraphy gained in that the calligraphers and master penmen had confined their art to the writing of panels alone, which they prepared by writing on lustrous fine sheets (*vasli*), and by this alone they grew rich sitting at home.

But alas, the custom of panels and inscriptions is now passing away from India, and pictures have taken their place, so that with the disappearance of the former elegant, refined and religiously lawful taste in decoration, calligraphy too has left India. Now there are copyists, not calligraphers, and the one or two penmen who are famous are forced to feed themselves by copy-writing and transcription, which is in fact the enemy of calligraphy. In those days, by contrast, a body of men had arisen whose sole work was to keep calligraphy on its principles and to give it suitable advancement from time to time. So the calligraphers of old thought transcribing books beneath their dignity, and held that whoever wrote whole books could not possibly maintain the principles and rules of calligraphy fully from beginning to end. And in truth, a copyist cannot put into writing a whole book a tenth of a tenth of the labour and toil those men spent on perfecting a single sheet.

Their labour can be judged from this. Once Nawab Saadat Ali Khan asked Hafiz Nurullah, "Write me a copy of the *Gulistan*." Nawab Saadat Ali Khan was extremely fond of Sa'di's *Gulistan*, and it is said that a *Gulistan* was always by his pillow. If anyone else had made such a request Hafiz Nurullah would have taken it as an insult and given him a piece of his mind, but since it was the ruler of the time who asked, he agreed, and said: "Then send for eighty *guddi* of paper for me (in those days a ream was called a *guddi*), a hundred penknives, and God knows how many thousand reed stems for pens." Saadat Ali Khan asked in astonishment, "Will so much material be needed for just one *Gulistan*?" He said, "Yes, sir, that is how much material I use." It was no hardship for the Nawab to provide this material, and he sent for it. Now Hafiz Sahib began to write the *Gulistan*, but it was never finished: he had written only seven chapters, and the eighth remained, when he died. After his death, when his son Hafiz Ibrahim was presented at court and given the black robe of condolence, Saadat Ali Khan said: "My friend, I had Hafiz Sahib write a *Gulistan* for me; God knows what became of it." Hafiz Ibrahim said: "Seven chapters in his hand are ready. The eighth chapter remains; this humble servant will write it and match it so closely to his style that Your Highness will not be able to tell the difference. But it is true that if an expert calligrapher saw it he would certainly recognise it." The Nawab gave permission, and Hafiz Ibrahim completed that *Gulistan*.

The most distinguished of Hafiz Nurullah's pupils were, first of all, his own son Hafiz Ibrahim; second, a Hindu elder named Munshi Sarab Sukh, whom some call a Kayasth and some a Kashmiri Pandit; and third, a calligrapher of Lucknow named Muhammad Abbas. Hafiz Ibrahim too made a great name. He made hundreds of men calligraphers, and, attaining an independent rank in the art, created a style distinct from his father's. Hafiz Nurullah's bowls were perfectly round; Hafiz Ibrahim gave them a slight ovalness. Of Munshi Sarab Sukh it is said that he had so captured his master's style that he circulated hundreds of sheets under Hafiz Nurullah's name, and great calligraphers could not tell them apart at all; and in those days this was a very great feat of calligraphy.

Among Hafiz Ibrahim's distinguished pupils was, first, his son Hafiz Sa'id-ud-Din. Besides him there were Munshi Nazir Hamid and Munshi Abdul-Majid, who was employed in the royal government to write royal orders and *parcha-o-payam* (that is, the correspondence between the British government and the government of Awadh). But two pupils of Hafiz Ibrahim flourished above all, and were recognised in their time as the masters of all Lucknow. One was Munshi Mansa Ram, a Kashmiri Pandit, who was a very great master of his art, and the other Munshi Muhammad Hadi Ali, who besides *nasta'liq* had no equal in Lucknow in *naskh* and in *tughra* (ornamental monogram) writing.

On the other side, the pupils of Qazi Ni'matullah were his son Maulvi Muhammad Ashraf and Maulvi Qul Ahmad.

In short, these were the masters of *nasta'liq* through whom calligraphy reached perfection in Lucknow. Then, after printing presses were started, transcription and copy-writing flourished, and it is really through the blessing of this lineage that in Lucknow thousands of Muslims, thousands of Kayasths, with whom the quarters of Naubasta and Ashrafabad are filled, and hundreds of Kashmiri Pandits became calligraphers. But alas, the Kashmiri Pandits, in their eagerness for English education and seeing the slump in the market for calligraphy, gave up this art entirely, and now all the good writers are Muslims or Kayasths.

In the last period a Munshi Abdul-Hayy of Sandila was also a very accomplished calligrapher, whose pupils were Munshi Amirullah Taslim, his elder brother Munshi Muhammad Abdul-Latif, Munshi Ashraf Ali and others. At present Munshi Shams-ud-Din Sahib is famous in *nasta'liq* and Munshi Hamid Ali Sahib in *naskh*, and both are pupils of Munshi Hadi Ali Sahib.

Of the masters to whom the *naskh* script in India is traced, the first is famous by the title Yaqut Musta'simi, and is called Yaqut the First. We see no accomplished scribe of this name in the time of al-Musta'sim billah. It may well be that what is meant is Imad the Scribe of Juwain, entitled "Fakhr-ul-Kuttab" (Pride of Scribes), who died in 597 AH, whose book *Kharida* is famous, and who was secretary first in Syria to Sultan Atabeg Nur-ud-Din Zangi and then in Egypt to Sultan Salah-ud-Din Ayyubi, conqueror of Jerusalem; for he is regarded as the last great calligrapher of *naskh*. [TN: Sharar's conjecture is doubtful: Yaqut al-Musta'simi (d. 1298) is a well-attested calligrapher at the court of the last Abbasid caliph, al-Musta'sim; Imad al-Din al-Katib, author of the *Kharidat al-Qasr*, was of Isfahan.] After him, in the time of Sultan Aurangzeb Alamgir, there arose a great master of *naskh* named Muhammad Arif, who was given the title Yaqut-Raqam the Second. It is generally said that he invented a new style of *naskh* and made it more beautiful than before, so much so that the masters of *naskh* in Lucknow claim that the whole Islamic world acknowledged his mastery. I am not prepared to accept this. Whatever pre-eminence Yaqut-Raqam the Second may have won in India, in the countries whose national script is *naskh* and whose mother tongue is Arabic, people do not even know Yaqut-Raqam's name, nor do they follow his style.

In the time of Muhammad Arif Yaqut-Raqam there was a man named Abdul-Baqi whose trade was *haddadi*, that is, smithing. Seeing Yaqut-Raqam's general authority, he was inspired to achieve mastery in this art himself. As it happened, another calligrapher of *naskh* named Abdullah Tabbakh was famous in those days. The smith went and became his pupil, and worked so hard that he became famous as a consummate master. When the time of these two had passed, Yaqut-Raqam's place was taken by his nephew Qazi Ismatullah, and the smith's two sons, Ali Akbar and Ali Asghar, were recognised as his memorials.

After this great calligraphers arose in India, and the writing of *naskh* went on advancing steadily in India. Finally Shah Ghulam Ali Sahib, an accomplished calligrapher of *naskh*, became famous. After this, in Lucknow, on the one side Maulvi Hadi Ali Sahib became famous, whose family had come from Delhi, and who was the pupil of Mir Akbar Ali, a calligrapher of Kalpi. Maulvi Hadi Ali Sahib had great mastery in *tughra* writing.

A contemporary of Munshi Hadi Ali was a famous calligrapher of *naskh*, Mir Banda Ali Murtaish. His master was an old-time noble named Nawab Ahmad Ali, an accomplished master of *naskh*. Mir Banda Ali had a tremor in his hand, but as soon as the pen touched the paper it seemed to be a hand of iron; it never once got out of control. His eye for recognising a hand was so masterly that great men acknowledged his superiority.

Munshi Hamid Ali Sahib relates: On one occasion Munshi Hadi Ali, Munshi Muhammad Yahya (he too was a great master of *naskh*, who wrote the first Quran in Lucknow for printing), Munshi Abdul-Hayy of Sandila and Mir Banda Ali Murtaish were gathered in one company. It was a gathering of all the masters of *naskh*. Someone brought a specimen of *naskh* and offered it for sale. Although the scribe's name was not written on it, these masters unanimously recognised that it was in Yaqut's own hand, and all of them wanted to get it into their possession. But Munshi Hadi Ali Sahib said: "If it stays with me for a day, I shall be satisfied after examining it whether it is really in Yaqut's hand or not." The owner gave it to him, and he brought it home. The next day he took it and presented it, saying: "It really is in Yaqut's hand. I had another specimen of Yaqut's lying with me that is its companion. I took it and compared it with this and found them exactly the same, and I am convinced that this really is Yaqut's." And he set both specimens before everyone. All accepted without hesitation that both were written in Yaqut's hand. But Mir Banda Ali looked closely at Munshi Hadi Ali's specimen, then smiled and wrote beneath it: *In kar az tu ayad-o mardan chunin kunand* ("This is a deed that comes from you; this is how men do things"). Seeing this inscription, Munshi Abdul-Hayy Sahib grew angry and said, "Have you any doubt about it?" Mir Banda Ali said, "This specimen cannot be in Yaqut's hand." Munshi Abdul-Hayy and the other rivals of the company insisted, "It is in Yaqut's own hand." Mir Banda Ali pointed out the head of a *vav* in it and said, "This cannot be Yaqut's." Now, while everyone was in a quandary, Munshi Hadi Ali tore off one corner of the sheet and from inside the layers of paper brought out and showed his own name, and everyone was convinced that this was Munshi Hadi Ali Sahib's handiwork. All praised him enormously, and he said: "But I have become a believer in Mir Banda Ali Sahib's eye."

In keeping with the general taste of calligraphers, it was impossible for Mir Banda Ali Sahib too to do any transcription other than the writing of specimens. Never in his life did he write even a small book. When Haji Harmain Sharifain started his press, he persuaded Mir Banda Ali with a thousand entreaties to write him a *Panj Sura* (a selection of five chapters of the Quran). Mir Banda Ali wrote it with great labour and in God knows how many days, and took it to him; but when he cast a last look over it in front of Haji Sahib, something displeased him so much that, instead of handing it to Haji Sahib, he tore it up and said, "My friend, I cannot do it."

My purpose in mentioning these elders is not to say that Lucknow had attained in calligraphy some distinction unequalled in India. On the contrary, I think that these men cannot reach a tenth of a tenth of the level of the masters of *naskh* who had lived in India before the Mughal empire; indeed, mastery in *naskh* had died out by those days. Of *nasta'liq* this much can certainly be said: the specimens in the hands of Hafiz Nurullah and Hafiz Ibrahim were received throughout India with an eagerness and delight that perhaps no other calligrapher's could have been. But even so, in the art of penmanship Lucknow's rank was much the same as that of other civilised cities.

But the work that the calligraphy of Lucknow did in the advance of printing perhaps the calligraphy of no other place could have done. I have not established where the first printing press in India was started. In Calcutta much care was taken over the advancement of Urdu literature and the strengthening of Eastern learning in general, but apart from type I have not seen any old lithographed books from there.

In Lucknow, in the reign of Ghazi-ud-Din Haidar (1233 to 1256 of the Muhammadan era, corresponding to 1814 to 1827 CE), a European named Archer [?] came and gave people the idea of printing, and when the learned became eager for it he opened the first press in Lucknow. [TN: the name is uncertain in the source scan.] He had the press and all its equipment made here and began printing, and he printed and presented to the public the *Zad-ul-Ma'ad*, the *Haft Qulzum* and the *Taj-ul-Lughat* (which was in many volumes). Learning from him, others too began to start presses, the very first of which was probably that of Haji Harmain Sharifain. In those days Mustafa Khan, a wealthy dealer in glassware, took something to Haji Harmain to print, and some harsh word escaped Haji Sahib's lips, so that Mustafa Khan came home and started his own Mustafai Press, which flourished extraordinarily. A little later Ali Bakhsh Khan started the Alavi Press, and printing houses began to open in great numbers in Lucknow.

At first printing here was carried on not on commercial principles but in the manner of a hobby. The finest Arwi paper was used, which was most suitable for lithography. Great calligraphers were prevailed upon and given large salaries to do the transcription, and they were indulged without any condition as to output, and without the least thought of how much they wrote in a day, or whether they wrote at all. In the same way the pressmen were not asked how many sheets they had printed in a day. For the ink, lampblack of the first quality was prepared by burning thousands of lamps of mustard oil. Lime juice alone was used for the acid, and real sponge was used instead of cloth. In short, everything used was of the first quality. The result of this care was that, in the opinion of those who know, the Persian and Arabic textbooks and religious books printed in Lucknow in the royal period could not have been printed so well anywhere. The books printed at that time are a treasure to whoever has them, and people search for them and cannot find them.

My father's own uncle, Maulvi Ahmad Sahib, had a great love of travel and trade, and at a time when people were afraid to set foot outside their homes, he travelled from Lucknow to Rawalpindi as the agent of Haji Harmain Sharifain, riding in *rath*s (covered carts) and bullock carts and taking thousands of books with him. He used to say: "Books in those days were as rare as the phoenix. People's eyes opened wide at the sight of the books printed here, and they fell on them like moths. Such was people's eagerness that whatever town or village we reached, the news of us had arrived before us, and our entry was made with wonderful pomp and splendour. No sooner had we reached a settlement than the crowd surrounded us; a throng would gather, and whatever book we gave at whatever price, people took it without demur and pressed it to their eyes. We sold the *Karima*, the *Ma-Muqiman* and so on at 6 or 8 annas a volume, and the *Gulistan* and *Bustan* at three or four rupees a volume. And even so we could not meet the demand. On the way from one town to the next our stock of books would run out, and we would have to stop for months waiting for new goods. In those days it was hard for goods to arrive, but we had made such arrangements that goods kept coming from Lucknow regularly."

In the last period of the kingdom the Mustafai Press had no equal in the world for its printing. After the annexation of the kingdom Munshi Naval Kishore started his press. Although it could not compete with the Mustafai Press in the quality of its printing, by working on commercial principles it printed such huge and voluminous books in Persian and Arabic that no press today would dare to print them. The truth is that the former hobbyist zeal in Lucknow had gathered so complete a stock of press equipment that a press-owner of such high ambition as Munshi Naval Kishore was needed to profit from it. In the end the Naval Kishore Press rose so high that it brought the whole of Eastern literature back to life, and the pre-eminence Lucknow gained in the extent of its printing no other city can attain. And it was through its blessing that the whole learned demand of Central Asia as far as Kashgar and Bukhara, and of Afghanistan and Iran, was being met by Lucknow. So to this day the Naval Kishore Press is the key to the trade in learning, without whose help no one can set foot in the world of learning.

But alas, now in Lucknow, despite the great number of presses, the state of printing is so bad and grows worse day by day that other cities have overtaken it, and in our view, because of the bad moral state of the pressmen, printing in Lucknow is now worse than in most cities. But it is enough for our satisfaction that in Cawnpore, thanks to Munshi Rahmatullah Sahib, the presses are in good condition, and Cawnpore is really an appendix to the advances of Lucknow.

Together with printing, the art of the *muslih-e-sang* (stone corrector) was invented in Lucknow. Correcting a copy transferred to the stone by scraping it to some extent and touching it up with a pen probably began in Europe, and it may well be that this process of correction is still practised there. But to correct letters of *naskh* and *nasta'liq* in such a way that the calligrapher's style remains entirely intact and no one can tell that anyone else's pen has touched it is purely Lucknow's invention. At first this art was limited to correcting the letters and ornaments here and there that had faded or been crushed and spread. But after a while the love of novelty here went beyond this limit, and such accomplished stone correctors began to appear as write whole books in reverse on the stone, and the script remains so perfect within its bounds that no one can possibly tell it was written in reverse on stone.

At first the accomplished inventor of this was an old elder who was responsible for the fame and renown of the Mustafai Press. In his own time the great number of his pupils benefited the presses here. Many made progress, and the city began to supply stone correctors. When stone correcting had become very common, the high skill of a famous stone corrector named Munshi Jafar Husain prompted him to make the presses independent of copy-writing. He began to write in reverse on the stone. This work began with small bazaar presses, and in the end was adopted to some extent in all the presses, high and low. Now Munshi Sayyid Ali Husain Sahib has advanced so far that many famous calligraphers cannot equal his reverse writing. So an ordinary example of his reverse writing is our own *Dilgudaz*, for which no copies are written: Munshi Ali Husain Sahib writes the articles in reverse on the stone. Readers of *Dilgudaz*, by reading it and studying its script, can judge to what degree of perfection the art of stone correcting has reached in Lucknow. Although in most cities of India the stone correctors are from Lucknow, the presses of no other city have yet had the good fortune to print by having the text written in reverse on the stones instead of transferring copies. This art is to this day confined to Lucknow. But alas, because of the decline in the state of the pressmen, Lucknow cannot benefit from this excellence in stone correcting as much as it should.
