# Saadat Ali Khan and Ghazi-ud-Din Haidar

## (5)

Nawab Saadat Ali Khan, as soon as he took the throne in 1227 of the Muhammadan era (1798 CE), presented half his country to the English. The story goes that he was lying in Benares, despairing and hopeless of ever ruling, when news came that Nawab Asaf-ud-Daula Bahadur had died and Wazir Ali Khan had taken his seat as ruler. At this his last remaining hopes of the throne turned to dust. He was in this state of utter despair when a European official of Benares came and asked him: "Nawab Sahib, if you were given the government of Awadh, what would you give the English government?" What value can a man set on something already lost? Without thinking he said: "I would present half the country to the English." Hearing this promise, the English official said: "Then rejoice, for I bring you the good news that you have been chosen ruler of Lucknow." Saadat Ali Khan was certainly delighted at this unexpected tidings, but when he remembered his promise he was struck dumb; and in the end, after his accession, he had to share out half his dominions to keep it, and this thorn rankled in his heart all his life.

English histories do not mention his being made to promise, but everyone agrees that because the English had placed Nawab Saadat Ali Khan on the throne, he presented half his country to them in gratitude. Whatever the truth, at Saadat Ali Khan's accession the government of Awadh was reduced by half. The old people of Lucknow say that, smarting under this, Saadat Ali Khan practised the greatest economy and showed boundless energy and alertness in collecting revenue, and amassed two hundred and twenty or two hundred and thirty million rupees; that he corresponded with the British government in England and settled that the contract for governing India should be given to him in place of the East India Company; and that the agreement was just about to be completed when his brother-in-law, joining in some conspiracy, poisoned him, and the proverb came true: *An qadah bishikast wa an saqi namand* ("That goblet broke, and that cupbearer is gone").

This and scores of stories like it are told, for which no proof can be found but hearsay. But there is no doubt that Saadat Ali Khan was so careful with money and so good an administrator that a ruler like him would not have given away any part of his dominions without good reason. [TN: Sharar's phrase here is obscure; literally "would not have given any part of the realm from a tenant".] Besides, in his conduct and policy one sees such restless shrewdness and mysterious impatience that, though nothing can be proved, it is plain that he meant to do something great, and his bearing was full of meaning.

Because the country had been divided, his greatest difficulty was that the state's revenue had fallen by half, while the late Asaf-ud-Daula had raised expenditure beyond all bounds. So he had to cut the expenses of the court, which was a very hard thing to do. To this end he examined the accounts, looked into the smallest sums, scrutinised rent-free grants (*mu'afi*) and fiefs with great severity, and reduced the expenses of the court as far as he could. In short, by whatever means he could, bearing ill fame and treating people with great harshness, he increased the state's income and reduced its spending.

Seeing these measures, sensible and fair-minded people came to acknowledge Saadat Ali Khan's ability and good management, but among the common people there was boundless discontent. On one side the holders of rent-free grants and fiefs whose properties had been confiscated were aggrieved; on the other the idle and superannuated servants whose posts had been abolished went about lamenting. Nor was that all: there was also a very large party in the country who favoured Wazir Ali Khan, held him to be the lawful and true heir to the throne, and called Saadat Ali Khan a usurper. In short, he had thousands of enemies in the country, and there was a danger that they might make an attempt on the Nawab's life. Besides the subjects, the army too was extremely displeased with the new Nawab. The countless locust swarm of troops gathered in Nawab Shuja-ud-Daula's time had begun to be reduced in Asaf-ud-Daula's day on the advice of the Honourable English Government, but Asaf-ud-Daula's generosity and extravagance had kept people content, and the voice of complaint had not been raised much. When Saadat Ali Khan made larger reductions and was close-fisted besides, there was an outcry on every side, and everyone was cursing him.

The result was that the English government thought it necessary, to protect his life, to keep a regular English military guard inside the city itself; for to put down the mischief-makers and rebels of the city, and to keep the peace, it was very necessary that a strong outside power be present in the city at all times. It is said that Nawab Saadat Ali Khan agreed to this with great reluctance.

Before this the rulers of Awadh had shown great simplicity in their way of living. The plain houses in which the first three rulers, Nawab Burhan-ul-Mulk, Nawab Safdar Jang and Nawab Shuja-ud-Daula, spent their lives were not even their own property but were rented. They regarded either the battlefield or the whole realm, which they kept touring, as their real home, and thought of every part of the land they owned as their dwelling. Nawab Asaf-ud-Daula, though very extravagant and notorious for pleasure-seeking and waste, was content with a single plain old-fashioned building, the Panch Mahla, although he had a great taste for building. What more need be said than that he spent two million rupees on building one *imambara* and mosque, and an even larger sum on building the Chowk and various markets, grain markets, bridges and caravanserais? In short, if the first three rulers' taste for building was satisfied by building forts and strongholds and gathering military stores, Asaf-ud-Daula's was satisfied by religious buildings or works for the public good. Along with this the old taste in architecture was still kept up. Asaf-ud-Daula's *imambara*s are the most perfect examples of the old taste in building. In Delhi and Agra the Emperor Shah Jahan had found marble and red sandstone of the highest quality in nearby quarries, which gave the buildings there a special refinement and the highest grandeur. In Lucknow stone was not to be had, and bringing it from Agra and Jaipur was so difficult that no one dared send for it. Asaf-ud-Daula used brick and lime and showed the same magnificence.

Nawab Saadat Ali Khan, despite his economy, his care with money and his passion for amassing it, had a taste for houses and buildings; but alas, through living in Calcutta and elsewhere and seeing the buildings of various places, his taste had been so corrupted that the buildings of his reign lost the old characteristics, and from that time the taste in building changed altogether.

The real cause of this revolution in building in Lucknow was partly Nawab Saadat Ali Khan's exile, wandering life and association with European peoples before his accession, but mostly the fact that General Martin built one or two mansions here in his own taste and so set before the rulers a new style of building, flimsy in strength but very attractive for the needs of daily life: buildings just like the toys given into children's hands, which break every day and are bought every day. European critics object loudly that the architectural taste of the rulers of Lucknow after Asaf-ud-Daula was quite debased, and that all their buildings are boys' toys or girls' dolls' houses. But they do not stop to ask who debased this taste. It is said that the national taste here was debased because there was in fact no nation here at all; and no one considers who corrupted the nationhood of this place, and whose magic tricks made people give up their old ways. The truth is: *Ai bad-e-saba, in hama awurda-e-tust* ("O morning breeze, all this is your doing").

Saadat Ali Khan first bought the Farhat Bakhsh mansion from General Martin for fifty thousand rupees, began to live in it, and built several more houses next to it. Then, close by, he built the Terhi Kothi (Crooked Mansion) for the Resident to live in, whose ruins lie inside the Residency. After that he built the Lal Baradari (Red Pavilion) for his court, which now houses a library and was then known as the Qasr-us-Sultan (Sultan's Palace). Besides this he built a new mansion across the river called Dil Aram (Heart's Ease), and in the same line, on a high mound now in the area of the Sadr, that is, the Lucknow cantonment, from which the whole city, the surrounding plains and the river spread before the eye in a charming view, he built a beautiful mansion and named it Dilkusha (Heart's Delight). Likewise he built another mansion which he named Hayat Bakhsh (Life-Giving), but this mansion was not used by the rulers of Awadh after Nawab Saadat Ali Khan. Before the Mutiny Major Banks lived in it, and after the Mutiny the rule was that whatever European gentleman came from the English government as Chief Commissioner of Awadh stayed in this mansion.

Besides the mansions mentioned above, the Nawab also built the well-known buildings Munawwar Bakhsh and Khurshid Manzil, and the Chaupar Stables too remain as his memorial. But in building all these the old native style was abandoned and new fashions from Europe were adopted. And plainly, in this particular respect, no old house in Lucknow could compete with the splendid new buildings that have been built, or are being built every day, in various cities of India under the influence and supervision of the British power itself. In short, this is the time from which buildings in the old taste, buildings of historic value that draw travellers by some special excellence, came to an end in Lucknow.

Nawab Saadat Ali Khan built a large market town (*ganj*) in the western part of Lucknow, and took such pains over its settlement and prosperity that special laws were made for it and special rights were granted to its merchants and shopkeepers. It flourished greatly, and to this day, although it lies at a distance from the city and quite apart, it is the largest market for many goods, and the Alamnagar station keeps growing every day because of it alone.

Besides Saadatganj, the other large markets founded and settled in the Nawab's time are these: Rakabganj (today the largest market for iron and a leading market for grain and the like), Jangliganj, Maqbulganj, Maulviganj, Golaganj and the Rastogi Mohalla. The original old building in the Moti Mahal (Pearl Palace) was also built by Nawab Saadat Ali Khan. It stands on the north side of the present Moti Mahal enclosure. It had a very fine white dome, to which the craftsman had given the lustre of a pearl.

Saadat Ali Khan is considered the most alert and statesmanlike of all the rulers of Awadh, and at the same time the most economical, careful, even miserly. He administered the country with unusual shrewdness, skill and refinement, and there is no doubt at all that if he had been granted full peace of mind to the end of his reign, all the past misrule and disorder would have been removed, and he would have reformed the country completely. But the trouble was that his relations with the East India Company were not good, so much so that at times he became sick of the crown and throne and of ruling and governing. Worn out by these things, he handed over more than half his country to the glorious British government, thinking that now he would be able to govern the territory left to him without wrangling or worry. But alas, he found no peace or rest even now. In the country left in his hands too, camps of English troops were set up here and there, and a large body was stationed in Lucknow itself and its environs, which was hard to manage, and whose great numbers laid a heavy burden on the state. In return he had to reduce much of his own army. Yet despite these cares and worries, the reforms he made are very praiseworthy. But the most remarkable thing is that, along with the growth of the markets and the spread of trade, so great a company of accomplished and worthy men gathered at his court that no other court in India at that time could show such masters of their arts. Such people usually gather where the ruler shows more than usual generosity. Saadat Ali Khan, as I have said, was careful and miserly; but along with this miserliness and economy he had this quality, that his own ability compelled him to acknowledge the merit of other accomplished men, and this led him to honour able people greatly, so that Lucknow became, more than before, the resort of men of talent. Wherever an able man was, as soon as he heard of Saadat Ali Khan's appreciation of merit he bade farewell to his homeland and turned towards Lucknow, and finding such comfort here, never spoke of his homeland again.

In 1243 of the Muhammadan era (1814 CE) Nawab Saadat Ali Khan died, and his son Ghazi-ud-Din Haidar took his seat as ruler. Inside the square building of the Qaisar Bagh are the tombs of Nawab Saadat Ali Khan and his wife, Murshidzadi. Where these two tombs stand there used to be a house in which Nawab Ghazi-ud-Din Haidar lived while he was heir apparent. When, as soon as his father's eyes closed, he moved into the royal palace, he said: "I have taken my father's house, so I must give him my own house to live in." In keeping with this thought he buried the late Nawab in his own house, pulled the old house down and built these tombs.

Now in Ghazi-ud-Din Haidar's reign there was neither his father's alertness and regard for wealth nor the military zeal of the earlier rulers. There was indeed the love of ease and pleasure of Asaf-ud-Daula's time, but with this difference, that Asaf-ud-Daula's extravagance was sometimes for the good of the country and the community, while now it was pure self-indulgence.

Ghazi-ud-Din Haidar had inherited his father's cash treasury of tens of millions of rupees, which began to fly away most lavishly in satisfying royal whims. In the Moti Mahal, as I have said, Saadat Ali Khan had built a mansion on the north side. Ghazi-ud-Din Haidar built two more mansions in this enclosure, named Mubarak Manzil and Shah Manzil. Next to the Shah Manzil was a bridge of boats, and the Mubarak Manzil stood a little to the east of it. Opposite the Shah Manzil, across the river, was a game park (*ramna*) called the Hazari Bagh, in which delightful green meadows stretched for miles. In it maddened (*must*) elephants, rhinoceroses and wild beasts were often made to fight, and the King would take his seat on the roof of the Shah Manzil on this side and watch the spectacle of their fights. Lion fights also took place there, for which strong cages and a fine circus had been built. But the fights of the small, harmless animals took place in the Shah Manzil enclosure itself, on this side.

This taste for setting wild beasts and savage animals to fight had not been heard of anywhere in India before this. It seems that the King conceived the taste on hearing from the Residents and from Europeans with access to the court about the amphitheatre of the Romans. But Maulana Habib-ur-Rahman Khan Sahib Sherwani drew my attention to the fact that fights of wild beasts had been customary since the time of the Mughal dynasty. [TN: this last sentence is a later addition by Sharar, printed in smaller type in the source edition; compare his footnote in Section 18.]

Ghazi-ud-Din Haidar built the Wilayati Mahal (Foreign Palace) for one of his European wives, and named it the Wilayati Bagh. Close by he built the Qadam-e-Rasul (the Prophet's Footprint). In keeping with Ghazi-ud-Din Haidar's wish, the English government granted him the title of "King". Before this the rulers of Awadh were regarded as having the rank of Wazir, and were called by no honorific title but Nawab. Until that time the Mughal empire kept so much of its prestige in India that, although the country had been divided among independent and self-willed rulers and only the land round Delhi was left in the Emperor's hands, even in this poverty he alone was Emperor and Refuge of the World. No one in India but the occupants of the throne of Delhi had the right to be called "King", or to grant titles and honours. To break this pride of theirs, the East India Company gave the royal title to Ghazi-ud-Din Haidar, who had lent the English a great deal of money out of his father's savings. The court of Awadh regarded this honour and distinction most highly. So from that time the rulers of Awadh, who were toys in the hands of the Residents, became kings, and the title remained their pride until the death of the last ruler, Wajid Ali Shah.

To commemorate this royal title, Ghazi-ud-Din Haidar founded a new market across the river opposite Machchhi Bhawan and named it Badshahganj (King's Market). At this time Hakim Mahdi founded Mahdiganj; and because the regent (*naib-us-saltanat*) Agha Mir's palatial buildings spread so far, the quarter of Agha Mir's Deorhi grew up right in the middle of the city; and in this reign Agha Mir's Sarai was built.

The King, and still more the Queen (Badshah Begum), were deeply absorbed in religious matters. Since the time of the Safavid dynasty the religion of Iran had been Twelver Shia, but the generality of Indian Muslims were Sunni. Since Nawab Burhan-ul-Mulk had come fresh from Persia, he and all his family were Shia. Even so, until this time the same old form of government had continued in Lucknow as had obtained in the other lands of India and the whole country since the beginning of Muslim rule. But from this time, because of the religious absorption of the King and his chief consort, Shi'ism became a prominent element of the government of Lucknow. The rulers' attention turned away from the scholars of Farangi Mahal, and the family of the *mujtahid*s rose to become the real lawgivers of the state.

But if the Shia religion had stayed in its original form, there would have been little harm. The trouble was that the Queen's ignorant, aristocratic religious zeal invented new innovations (*bid'at*) in the Shia religion, so that not only did all sorts of childish whims arise among the kings and nobles, but the Shi'ism of Lucknow became new, strange and peculiar compared with the Shi'ism of the rest of the world.

First of all the Begum Sahiba instituted the ceremony of the *chhatti* (the sixth-day rite after a birth) of the Imam of the Age. If this had meant gaining merit by describing the life of the Imam at some gathering, there would have been no harm; but no, here a complete lying-in chamber was arranged after the manner of the rites of the Hindu *Janmashtami* (the festival of Krishna's birth). Next came the further development that beautiful girls of Sayyid families of pure descent were taken and declared the wives of the Twelve Imams, and were named "*Achhutiyan*" (the Untouched Ones). And since they were the Imams' wives, the Imams were born to them too, and the birth ceremonies of all twelve Imams began to be celebrated with great pomp.

Ghazi-ud-Din Haidar was a very wrathful and unstable king, and so overbearing that in his time relations with the English stayed good, but Agha Mir, who was the chief minister, so dominated the court that not even the Queen and the heir to the throne were safe from his tyranny. Ghazi-ud-Din Haidar would beat him with fists and kicks, a beating he took gladly, but he would take his revenge for it on the other notables of the court, and even on the royal kinsmen.

Out of religious devotion this first King of Awadh built in Lucknow, on the riverbank next to the Moti Mahal, a copy of Najaf-e-Ashraf, that is, the holy shrine of Hazrat Ali, and handed over a large sum to the English government for its lighting and upkeep, thanks to which it is still splendid and well kept. When he died in 1256 of the Muhammadan era (1827 CE), he was buried in it.
