# Cream, the Dining Cloth and the Abdar-khana

## (31)

We have written about the kitchen and the invention and advance of dishes to a considerable extent. But we wish to say this much more: here, and generally in Asian countries, along with producing good taste, effort was also made with great seriousness to ensure that, with refinement of taste, foods should have the finest reviving fragrances, that their colour should remain exquisite and attractive, and that their appearance should be beguiling and appetising. Although in all the cities of India where people are fond of good food all these things are attempted, it cannot be denied that Lucknow has been more successful than anywhere else. In some places a true taste for food has remained limited to a few nobles and particular people, but here a sound taste arose in almost everyone. Not only were good cooks produced, but among the women of honoured and respectable households there arose greater refinement of temperament and nicety of taste than among the *rikabdar*s. There is no honoured family in which each of the respected ladies does not have a good skill in cooking, and does not lay claim to preparing some good dish.

Milk and curds are in use everywhere. In Lucknow, besides these two things, more attention was paid to the preparation of *balai* (clotted cream), because the most delicate part of the milk goes into it. In English it is called "cream", which is very widely used in Europe. But there cream is the name for this: milk is left standing a little while, and when the white, delicate fatty part comes to the top it is skimmed off and separated.

Here this delicate part of the milk is separated by setting it on a gentle fire and letting it set, and it is laid layer upon layer with great nicety. Setting the layers of *balai* with nicety and elegance is a craft that the people of hardly any city but Lucknow will know.

In the old language it is called *malai*. Asaf-ud-Daula Bahadur, Nawab of Awadh, liked it so much that it was prepared for him with special care. He gave it the name *balai* instead of *malai*, because it is the thing on top (*bala*) of the milk. The people of Lucknow very much liked this coinage of their ruler, and the word *balai* became so current on their tongues that now in Lucknow everyone except villagers or ignorant Hindus calls it *balai*, and the word *malai* has not survived on the tongue of any cultivated person.

On this the late Maulvi Muhammad Husain Azad raised an objection in *Ab-e-Hayat*, and referred the judgement to sound taste, by which standard, in his taste, the word *malai* is more refined and eloquent than *balai*. To call a word uneloquent merely by the standard of one's own taste is in my view a rather meaningless thing, because to every community those words seem good in its taste which have become current on its tongue and familiar with its accent and idiom. To the people of the cities who say *malai* the word *balai* will no doubt sound harsh and unfamiliar to their tongue. But to the city where people say *balai*, and where this word has entered their idiom, the eloquence they see in *balai* is not possible in *malai*. To them *malai* seems a word of the ignorant and the rustic. The eloquence and refinement of a language are not judged by any particular taste or by any logic; rather, only the taste and idiom of those who are accepted as native speakers become the standard, and everyone has to follow them without any logical argument. For Urdu, Delhi and Lucknow are now both regarded as authoritative schools of native speakers, so both are recognised standards of speech, even if the word of one is unfamiliar to the other. It is another matter whether the language of Lucknow alone should be accepted as the true and authoritative standard, but we do not wish to enter that quarrel, and probably the quarrel has been settled anyway. In any case, if both cities are accepted as standards, then *malai* and *balai* are both in themselves eloquent: *malai* in the view of the people of Delhi and *balai* in the view of the people of Lucknow. There can be no reason for anyone to object to the other.

More than the cooking of food, or equal to it, there is need to show good skill in serving out dishes, and after serving them, in arranging and decorating them. The present taste of Europe is that the table is well decorated. Bouquets are placed on it here and there, and in some places, for refinement, raw rice is dyed in different colours and letters and designs are made with it on the table. The vessels too are very clean, costly, and often of silver. But English cooks or *khansama*s (butlers) give hardly any thought to decorating the food itself, except for wedding cakes, which at the wedding dinners of nobles and lords are made with wonderful elaboration in the form of towers or beautiful buildings and set on the table of the wedding feast (*walima*).

In India, on the contrary, less attention is paid to decorating the dining cloth, but the dishes themselves are served out and decorated with the highest refinement. Silver and gold leaf is laid on them. Designs and flowers of many colours are made with slivers of pistachio and almond. Leaves of coconut are cut and they are arranged with them in a most fitting order. The *rikabdar*s have special mastery in this art; indeed their work is precisely this, that with whatever excellence they prepare foods they should decorate them with still greater elegance, and make every plate a bouquet.

In Lucknow these refinements, starting from professional cooks and *rikabdar*s, reached all the houses of the gentry, and the ladies and *begam*s acquired such good skill in them that the excellence they often show in decorating plates and dishes is not possible even for the *rikabdar*s themselves, although this is their special art. The scholars of Europe have determined that women have a special affinity for the fine arts, and especially in decorating and arranging anything they naturally have superiority over men. Proof of this can be had in India from the talent of those women of Lucknow who display mastery in decorating dishes.

The Indian wedding cakes, just mentioned, are the *chaughare* (four-compartment trays of sweets), which are generally set by custom before bride and groom at weddings. The ladies of many households decorate them with such refinement, intelligence and ingenuity that one wishes to sit and gaze at them.

Along with food it will not be without interest to describe the advances of the *abdar-khana* too. The *abdar-khana* is the name of the water arrangements of kings and nobles. In earlier days there was no ice, and in some seasons it was very difficult to get cold water, so in those days special kinds of arrangements were made. Water was filled and kept in new unglazed pitchers. Delicate and fine drinking cups (*abkhora*s) were kept ready for drinking. Red cloth was drawn over the pitchers and cups and kept wet, because from the wind the wet cloth became very cold, so much so that the hotter the hot wind and the *lu* (summer hot wind), the colder it made the cloth, and the coolness of the cloth cooled the water within. Often *jhajhri*s and *surahi*s (long-necked water jugs), and even pitchers, had a cloth tied over the mouth and were hung upside down in the branches of some tree. Since the air could not get in, the water did not fall out, and it became very cold. In the rains, when even this device did not succeed, pitchers were often filled and hung down inside wells, where they became thoroughly cool.

Besides this, the greatest arrangement was that delicate zinc *surahi*s were kept ready, and they were turned round in tubs into which saltpetre and water had been put. By this device in a short time the water acquired an ice-like coolness, and its coldness was most delicate and pleasant. This device was called "fanning the *surahi*s" (*surahiyon ka jhalna*).

In later times a sensible and lasting device was also found for obtaining ice. In the forty days (*chilla*) of winter, when the cold was at its height, hot water was filled into clay saucers and bowls and set out at night in fields and open plains, and was found frozen in the morning. This ice was at once buried in deep pits in the ground that had been dug ready beforehand, and in them that ice kept its state so long as it remained covered. In any case, by this method so much ice was made and filled into the pits that it was enough for the whole year, and from it some was taken out every day. But this ice was not so clean that it could be mixed in water; rather, like saltpetre, salt and saltpetre were mixed with it and *surahi*s were fanned, or ice *qulfi*s were set.

But this arrangement remained limited to kings or nobles of equal rank. Poor people could not benefit from it. The poor and people of middling rank cooled water using the first-mentioned devices, and this care had become so general that it was found more or less in every house.

In any case, in Lucknow this care was taken for water in those days, and refinement of temperament had produced these refinements: that on clay and zinc *surahi*s, and on cups of the same kind, red *shalbaf* (*dol*) cloth was often drawn, and silver braid (*gota*) was wound beautifully over the *dol*, giving them such charm that, let alone drinking, the sight of the vessels brought coolness to the eyes.

I do not know whether this arrangement of the *abdar-khana* that I have described existed fully in Delhi or not. Probably it surely did, and all these things will have come to Lucknow from there. But I did not see in Delhi this care and equipment in the completeness and general diffusion with which I saw it among the people of Lucknow. It is possible that it was the same there too, but it cannot be doubted that on coming to Lucknow the delicacy, refinement and fineness of clay water-vessels greatly increased, because owing to the excellence of the clay here, such delicate, elegant and well-shaped earthen vessels can be made in Lucknow as can be made nowhere else. The people of Delhi will have had the same zinc *surahi*s, but such clay *surahi*s no one there could have. We shall describe these earthen vessels later in a suitable place.

Wherever kings went, the kitchen and the *abdar-khana* went with them. But here the care of the *abdar-khana* had grown so much among other nobles too that there were many nobles who kept their *abdar-khana* with them. Thus Mirza Haidar Sahib's *abdar-khana* and *huqqa-khana* (hookah establishment) were run on so generous a principle that whatever wedding gathering he went to, the arrangement for serving water and hookahs to the whole gathering was entrusted to him, and his joining a gathering became for many people an unexpected blessing and a divine mercy.
