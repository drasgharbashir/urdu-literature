# Nasir-ud-Din Haidar and Muhammad Ali Shah

## (6)

In 1256 of the Muhammadan era (1827 CE) Ghazi-ud-Din Haidar's son Nasir-ud-Din Haidar took the throne. From Ghazi-ud-Din Haidar's time, as I have said, the rulers of Awadh were kings, not nawabs. This dynasty had begun at the rank of Wazir of Delhi, and the earlier powerful and eminent rulers were all called Nawab Wazir. But now, when real government and power had departed and they had no influence left at all in the politics of India, they became kings.

One might think that when the English gave the rulers of Awadh royal honours, they would also have increased their power by backing them, and shown them to be kings not in name only but in fact. But no: we see that in this period these men not only had no influence at all outside Awadh, but were not as free even in their own dominions as their predecessors had been. Now no one could take the throne without the approval of the English. English troops were spread here and there across the whole realm. No important matter could be settled without the Resident's interference. The royal throne was a stage, on which whatever happened seemed to be done by the actors, but in reality their actions were in the power of someone else, who stood behind the curtain and did as he pleased.

But God was so kind that these later rulers of Awadh, and with them nearly everyone attached to the court, had lost all feeling, so that they could not feel their weakness and helplessness at all. Ghazi-ud-Din Haidar, as soon as he became king, gave himself up to pleasure and luxury, and Nasir-ud-Din Haidar received the royal throne as an inheritance. The money amassed by Nawab Saadat Ali Khan served both of them in their pursuit of pleasure. Some was lent to the English, some was spent on performing the newly invented religious rites that the King and his queens had devised to suit their taste with zeal and delight, and the rest began to go on extravagances and debaucheries. Ghazi-ud-Din Haidar at least did this much: he had the copy of Najaf-e-Ashraf built and so provided a resting place for his grave, and, not relying on his heirs, handed over some money to the English so that from its interest they might maintain the Najaf with full religious observance. So to this day a lamp burns on his grave, mourning assemblies (*majlis*) are held, the Quran is recited, and in Muharram it is splendidly illuminated, and by this means a few poor people are supported. But amid the press of his pleasures Nasir-ud-Din Haidar was not granted even this much grace. In the quarter of Iradat Nagar across the river he built a *karbala* (a shrine-ground in memory of Karbala) that was meant to be his own tomb, but he gave not the least thought to its care and upkeep, with the result that today it lies desolate and silent near Daliganj station, and perhaps there is not even anyone to light a lamp there. In his time the new quarters of Ganeshganj and Chandganj were settled there across the river.

Nasir-ud-Din Haidar believed in astrology, which turned his attention to astronomy, and he resolved to set up an observatory of the highest class in his city. For this purpose he built a mansion between Nawab Saadat Ali Khan's tomb and the Moti Mahal, which, being an observatory, became known in Lucknow as the "Tare-wali Kothi" (the Star House). Large telescopes and astronomical instruments of the highest class were gathered in it. The work of setting them up properly and of managing and supervising them was entrusted to Colonel Wilcox, who was a good astronomer. But this observatory of Lucknow was, as it were, an obscure episode in the life of the Colonel alone; for Nasir-ud-Din Haidar's reign began in 1256 of the Muhammadan era, and probably the observatory was set up four or five years later, and from then until 1276 of the Muhammadan era (1847 CE), in the time of the last crowned head of Awadh, Wajid Ali Shah, it remained under his management. In that year the Colonel died, and no astronomer was appointed to the post in his place. Wajid Ali Shah neglected it. I have heard from some reliable people of Lucknow that Wajid Ali Shah, thinking its largest telescope a toy, gave it to the courtesan Haidari. But the Gazetteer shows that this observatory was still standing at the time of the annexation. Probably the rebels destroyed it in the Mutiny, for Ahmadullah Shah (who was also called Danka Shah and fought the English army with great energy and ardour) had taken up residence in the Tare-wali Kothi itself, held his court in it, and the officers of the rebel armies used to gather there to take counsel.

At this time Raushan-ud-Daula, who was chief minister, built his beautiful and splendid mansion, in which the Deputy Commissioner now holds his court; for Wajid Ali Shah had confiscated this mansion when he was building the Qaisar Bagh, and when the country came into English hands the mansion was government property.

The truth is that Nasir-ud-Din Haidar's time was a very dangerous time. On the one hand the administration of the realm was in disorder. The King had no leisure from pleasure and luxury and from the rites of the piety he himself had invented; the whole administration of the state was left to the ministers, and as for the ministers, no one could be found who could manage affairs with good intentions and sound judgement. Hakim Mahdi was called in; he was an administrator of the first rank, but he wanted to make the state his own inheritance. Raushan-ud-Daula became minister; he had neither substance nor the right temperament, and could get nothing done. Such was the King's extravagance that all the money Saadat Ali Khan had amassed flew away like water, and the revenue of the country was not enough even for the expenses of the palace. On top of this, quarrels arose between the King and his mother, Ghazi-ud-Din Haidar's chief queen. She declared Munna Jan to be the King's son, and the King would not acknowledge him as his son. These things brought the country to such a state that it seemed the rulers had no capacity at all to govern and manage it.

The Resident and the Governor-General of India again and again advised, warned and threatened, told them what would come of it, and kept sounding the alarm, but here no one paid the slightest heed. From living among women Nasir-ud-Din Haidar had become so effeminate that he talked like a woman and dressed like a woman. Effeminacy combined with religious devotion produced this result: he developed still further the imaginary wives of the Twelve Imams (the *Achhutiyan*) and the ceremonies of their births that his mother had instituted, so much so that at the ceremonies of the Imams' births he himself would sit in the lying-in room as a pregnant woman, show the pains of labour in his face and movements, and then himself give birth to an imaginary child, for whom the arrangements for the birth, the *chhatti* and the ritual bath (*nahan*) were made exactly as for a real one. These ceremonies were so numerous that the King had no leisure from them all year round; who then would attend to the state?

A study of the relations between the court of Awadh and the English government shows that if the Governor-General and the Residents had not looked kindly on it, and if the Board in England that supervised the East India Company had not kept the Company in check, the annexation of the state would have taken place at this very time. But this court of childish whims still had some life left in it. The English kept meaning to take the country and stopping short.

Reliable old people of Lucknow say of Nasir-ud-Din Haidar that along with this effeminacy and these childish antics he was also extremely cruel. But since his whole life was spent among women, the victims of his cruelties too were mostly women. He had scores of women walled up alive for trifling faults and slight suspicions. They say that once, as he was passing, he saw a man with his hand on a woman's breast, and at once had the woman's breasts and the man's hands cut off.

At last, after ten years of excesses, when everyone at court, inside and out, was weary of life, the King himself fell victim to his friends and kin, and someone ended the story with poison in 1266 of the Muhammadan era (1837 CE). Nasir-ud-Din Haidar died childless. Ghazi-ud-Din Haidar's queen had always put Munna Jan forward as her grandson and the true heir to the throne, but both Ghazi-ud-Din Haidar and Nasir-ud-Din Haidar had denied that he was of royal blood. For this reason the English government had arranged beforehand for the accession of Nasir-ud-Daula Muhammad Ali Khan, son of the late Nawab Saadat Ali Khan. But the Begum Sahiba would not accept it. She took Munna Jan and came to the Lal Baradari, that is, the throne room. The Resident tried a thousand times to stop her and reason with her, but she would not listen, and forcibly seated Munna Jan on the throne. As soon as he set foot on the throne he received the offerings of homage (*nazr*) and at once began to take revenge on his enemies too. He had many houses plundered, some people arrested, some were killed, and pandemonium broke out in the city.

The Resident and his Assistant went at once to the court. They explained to the Queen Mother that Munna Jan could not be heir to the throne and that his accession would not be accepted. Then they showed the Governor-General's written order and said it was best that Munna Jan should vacate the throne and Nasir-ud-Daula's accession take place. But no one listened; instead someone attacked the Assistant Resident, so that his face was covered in blood. The Resident had already summoned English troops from Mandiaon, and they had set up guns in front of the throne room, and the soldiers stood drawn up in ranks. Left with no choice, the Honourable gentleman took his watch in his hand and said: "Ten minutes' grace is given. If Munna Jan has not come down from the throne within that time, force will be used." No one paid any heed to this either, though the Resident kept saying again and again, "Now five minutes are left", "Now only two minutes remain", "And now, look, not even a full minute".

No one heeded these warnings, and suddenly the guns began to fire grapeshot. In an instant thirty or forty men fell. The courtiers fled in panic, stumbling and falling. Several people in the troupe that was performing were wounded too. The glass chandeliers crashed and shattered and came tumbling down. When several loyal brave men who stood in the way had been killed, Munna Jan too tried to slip from the throne and flee, but he was caught. In short, the English arrested both of them, the Begum Sahiba and him. At the same time the accession of Nasir-ud-Daula took place, and he became King of Awadh with the title Muhammad Ali Shah. Munna Jan and his grandmother were sent under strict guard from Lucknow to Cawnpore and from Cawnpore to the fort of Chunar Garh, and an allowance of two thousand four hundred rupees a month was fixed for them from the Lucknow treasury.

Muhammad Ali Shah was sixty-three years old at his accession. He was old and experienced, and had watched the ups and downs of the times and the childish whims of the court. Above all, he was Nawab Saadat Ali Khan's son and had seen his ways with his own eyes. He worked very carefully, introduced principles of economy, and tried as far as he could to set the country in order. But he was advanced in years and his strength was failing. As soon as he took the throne he called Hakim Mahdi from Farrukhabad and gave him the robe of the ministry, but a few days later he died. Then Zahir-ud-Daula was given the robe of the ministry. Two or three months later he too departed this world, and Munawwar-ud-Daula became minister, who resigned after only a few months and went to Karbala the Exalted. Then Ashraf-ud-Daula Muhammad Ibrahim Khan became minister, who was sensible and steady compared with the others.

On Muhammad Ali Shah's accession a new treaty was made between the English government and the state of Awadh, under which the force kept by the English government to watch over Awadh was considerably increased, and the government of the East India Company gained the right to take under its own administration, for as long as it wished, the whole realm of Awadh or any part of it in which it saw misrule. The King signed this treaty with reluctance, and set about reforming the country as far as he could.

In the very second year after his accession he began to build his famous *imambara*, Husainabad, and near it a magnificent mosque, which care was taken to make surpass the Jama Masjid of Delhi in splendour and size.

In those days the population and splendour of Lucknow had grown so much, and so many people lived in its precincts, that it was not wrong to call it the Babylon of India. Truly this city was, in every respect, the living Babylon of its age.

Having perhaps heard this comparison from the English or some courtier, Muhammad Ali Shah resolved to make Lucknow a complete Babylon, and to set up a memorial of himself that would show his name higher than that of all the kings of Awadh. Near Husainabad, by the present clock tower, he began to build a structure like the tower of Babylon or its hanging gardens, in which a circular ring of arches was set on another ring, and a third on the second, and so on, one above another. The intention was to raise it in this way to seven storeys and make a tower so large and high that it would be unrivalled in the whole world, from the top of which all Lucknow and the country round it could be seen. Had this building been completed it would certainly have been matchless and wonderful. It was named the "Sat Khanda" (Seven Storeys), and was being built with great care, but only five storeys had been built when Muhammad Ali Shah died in 1271 of the Muhammadan era (1842 CE).

In his short time Muhammad Ali Shah made Lucknow a very beautiful city, without internal quarrels arising or complaints of misrule being raised in the country. From the gate of Husainabad to the Rumi Darwaza he made a road along the river, which was called the Chowk. Along this road, besides the splendid houses on both sides, there stood on one side the Rumi Darwaza, Asaf-ud-Daula's *imambara* and its mosque; on the other the Sat Khanda and the gate of Husainabad. Opposite this new *imambara* stood a splendid building, and beside it the Jama Masjid. Together all these buildings made so charming and enchanting a view that it rivalled all the famous and beautiful views of the world. And even now, although all the townspeople's houses that stood in between have been dug away, it is thought one of the finest views in the world.
