# Urdu Prose

## (11)

Urdu prose is young compared with verse. For a long time the way of educated people was this: although some wrote poetry in Persian too, the general tendency and inclination was towards the Urdu *ghazal*, and in India the number of Urdu poets was much greater than that of Persian poets. But in prose the whole country preferred to read and write in Persian alone. Books on the sciences and arts were written in Persian. Books of religion and faith were composed in Persian. Indeed, everyone from old men to children carried on their correspondence in Persian. In the *maktab* (elementary school) children were taught Persian model letters (*insha*) and taught to write letters in Persian alone. The result was that however sweet and eloquent the Urdu language had become in speech, when the need to write arose, everyone was struck dumb.

The first to write in Urdu was Mir Amman of Delhi, who wrote his book *Char Darvesh* (The Four Dervishes) with the encouragement and under the direction of the English. At the same time Mirza Ali Lutf composed his biographical anthology of Urdu poets (*tazkira*), which has been printed through the efforts of Abdullah Khan Sahib, resident at Hyderabad. At about the same time Maulvi Ismail Sahib Shahid wrote his book *Taqwiyat-ul-Iman* on the unity of God and following the Sunna. Whatever light these books may now be seen in, in those days they were not written to display literary excellence. The sole aim in composing them was to convey the meaning in an unaffected, plain and simple language so that ordinary people could benefit. Had the elders mentioned above wished to display excellence in Urdu literature, they would have adopted, according to the principles of the prose art of the time, the manner of Zuhuri and Ni'mat Khan Ali, Abul-Fazl and Tahir Wahid, which then ruled the literary world and without which no writing in the country was thought worthy of praise. Not only in writing but in conversation too, if particular refinement and politeness were intended, that same manner was adopted, as Sayyid Insha has shown by quoting a few words of Mirza Mazhar Jan-e-Janan's speech.

In truth, Urdu prose-writing began in Lucknow, when first Mirza Rajab Ali Beg Surur published *Fasana-e-Aja'ib* and his other books. At this time or after, *Nau Ratan* too was written in Lucknow, whose author, Muhammad Bakhsh Mahjur, a pupil of Jur'at, was formed and fashioned by the society of Lucknow.

Rajab Ali Beg Surur, in truth, displayed the highest excellence in prose composition, and when that book was published it was looked upon with wonder in Urdu circles. But unluckily he had attacked Mir Amman in his preface, because of which all his excellences were brought to dust in the eyes of the people of Delhi, so that even so cultivated an elder as Mir Muhammad Husain Azad calls him "the Lucknow scoundrel". And who knows how long revenge will go on being taken on the late Rajab Ali Beg for this impertinence? The English may have seen Mir Amman's skill in prose in those days, but none of the native speakers of India saw it, nor could they have, because the influence of English education had not yet changed the taste for the country's literature, and Eastern literature was settled in people's thoughts and minds.

I have written many times about literary style, and I write again, that it depends entirely on education and the nurture of taste. Just as the surrounding society makes foods, perfumes, colours and all other things agreeable, and there is so much difference among nations and countries that what is most delicious and beloved to one is utterly tasteless and most detestable to another, so it is with literature and literary taste: the style that is nurtured in one nation and takes hold of its minds and tongues seems absurd, insipid and tasteless to another, and no one can rightly decide which is good and which bad.

In the Arabia of the Jahiliyya (pre-Islamic age), the style of eloquence and rhetoric was to use rhymed phrases, to have balance in the sentence and current synonymous words, and to express the same meaning again and again to make it effective and attractive. The Quran, since it was in the language of the people, brought this very taste to perfection in a most miraculous manner. Then this taste became the chief element of Arabic literature. Judged by present-day standards, the most eloquent books of Arabic, the *Maqamat* of Hariri, the *Tarikh-e-Timuri* and so on, are nothing but rhyme-mongering, needless prolixity and the use of unnecessary words; yet a whole world relished them for ages and centuries. The Persian men of letters adopted the same style, and as literature advanced, that style grew firmer and deeper. And because this taste was settled in their minds, the first men of letters of Urdu adopted the same style and won the world's applause. So the idea that when *Char Darvesh* was written it was thought any literary achievement by the learned of India, apart from being liked by the English, who did not even know Urdu, is entirely baseless.

Now, under the influence of English, a time has certainly come when the ornaments and garments with which the old literature had dressed Urdu have been stripped off and new Western clothes put on it. Because *Char Darvesh* and other books like it were bare of the old literary ornaments and garments, people liked them; not because they had any special merit, but because they were bare of the style of that old, popular national literature which people today dislike.

At the same time in Lucknow Maulvi Ghulam Imam Shahid wrote his famous *Maulid Sharif* (account of the Prophet's birth), which was so steeped in the literary taste of the time that people liked it greatly, and because of its religious popularity it is much liked to this day.

But present-day Urdu prose really came out of Delhi, and will always remain indebted to Delhi. Mirza Ghalib adopted an unaffected style in Urdu prose, which is very close to present taste. Although he too sometimes indulges in rhyme, it is with such ease that the reader notices the rhyme only on reflection. Modern education had well prepared people to accept this style. It was applauded in every gathering. After him Sir Sayyid gave this simplicity gravity, but with the effort that the language should not become difficult, and should remain such that high and low alike could understand it. Maulvi Muhammad Husain Azad added charm to it along with gravity, while the people of Lucknow, being far from the influence of English, were still enamoured of the old style. Here, until the last days of Wajid Ali Shah's life, ornate and rhymed prose was written, and people had not yet been able to taste this simplicity.

Now *Tahzib-ul-Akhlaq* from Aligarh, *Terhvin Sadi* (The Thirteenth Century) from Agra, and *Awadh Punch* from Lucknow were coming out, each of which had a distinct glory in Urdu prose. *Tahzib-ul-Akhlaq* had, with gravity and scholarly dignity, the ardour and pathos of concern for the community; it had a clear, well-ordered language, and ideas, articles and addresses that were drawn from the new Western philosophy and literature and made an impression. In *Terhvin Sadi* fine prose and the old literary taste were ably preserved together with new flights of fancy and novelties, and the old Eastern literature was shown in new dress with such inventiveness that cries of "Bravo!" rose involuntarily from both the new and the old parties.

In *Awadh Punch* the language was shown in its true glory, with humour predominating. It had various writers, and each one's taste had its own particular charm and merits. The playful wit of the editor, Munshi Sajjad Husain; the language of Mirza Machchhu Beg Sahib, as if washed in the waters of Kausar [TN: a river of Paradise]; the style of Munshi Ahmad Ali of Kasmanda, showing the high literary and poetic delights of Persian diction; the Hindi poems of Pandit Tribhuvan Nath Hijr, and his articles showing their beauties with great charm, were bringing a wonderful life and freshness to Urdu prose.

Meanwhile Pandit Ratan Nath's novel *Fasana-e-Azad* began to be published with the *Awadh Akhbar*, which had a very great effect on the country; the Urdu world became acquainted with the taste for novel-writing and fell boundlessly in love with it. In *Fasana-e-Azad*, where the author shows a scene or narrates an event in his own words, he has adopted, with improvements, the old style of *Fasana-e-Aja'ib*; and where he makes others speak, he has kept a very simple and unaffected language. The language of the women especially is very pure, though there are mistakes here and there. But the truth is that the level he reached in his effort had been reached by no one before him.

This is the time when Maulvi Nazir Ahmad Sahib, at the request of the Government, translated the Indian Penal Code, and through his books presented to the country a language that is in places matchless in fluency and clarity and in places, being full of Arabic vocabulary, extremely difficult and elevated. And in this same period the literature of Maulvi Muhammad Husain Sahib Azad was presenting a most pleasing style. In particular, by writing a history of the Urdu language and an account of the Urdu poets [TN: *Ab-e-Hayat*] he won special renown in Urdu literature.

At this same time, in 1882 CE, I brought out a weekly magazine called *Mahshar* in the name of Maulvi Muhammad Abdul-Basit Sahib Mahshar, through which the style of Addison was presented in Urdu with such attractive titles and such fitting and apt words and ideas that the country was suddenly, as it were, startled. At the same time my articles began to appear in the columns of the *Awadh Akhbar*, which presented to the country an entirely new literature, so popular that cries of welcome began to be heard on every side. Suddenly it was seen that most writers were adopting this same style, and that the general tendency of the country was in this direction. In the meantime I presented to the country my novel, an interesting and moving drama, *Shahid-e-Wafa*, and encouragement came from every side.

At last, seeing the country's insistence and demand, I started the magazine *Dilgudaz* from the beginning of 1887 CE, whose literature became popular both with those who knew English and with people of the old taste. Then from 1888 CE a series of historical novels was started with it, the very first of which is *Malik-ul-Aziz Varjina*. There is no need to describe with what eagerness the country took up these novels, but it is necessary to say this much: it was because of these very novels that the foundation was laid for a growing eagerness to learn about events and to read books. Through these novels a taste arose in the country for reading history and taking an interest in the events of the world, and these novels and the pages of *Dilgudaz* created the style on which present-day Urdu literature is founded.

In any case, so far as Urdu prose is connected with the old literary style, its foundation was laid in Lucknow. The modern style, it is true, began from Delhi, but in this endeavour Lucknow kept Delhi company as far as possible. Above all, the taste for humour arose in Lucknow itself and was brought to perfection in Lucknow.
