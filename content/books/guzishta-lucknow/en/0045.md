# Wit, and Women's Celebrations

## (44)

(8) The eighth thing in the manners of social life is the manner of wit (*mazaq*). It is an old saying of the Arabs, indeed a famous *hadith* of the Prophet, that "wit in speech is like salt in food" [TN: this saying is not found in the canonical *hadith* collections; it is a proverb commonly attributed to the Prophet]. The truth is that without playfulness and wit neither does speech acquire savour nor does company come alive. But if carelessness enters into this same wit, it becomes the cause of grave strife and mischief. Wit has often caused swords to be drawn in the course of talk, and turned old bosom friends into enemies in a moment. If you look carefully you will clearly see that the cause of these evils is not wit, but carelessness in wit or going beyond moderation.

The more a language advances, the more its aspects of humour and wit increase. It is very difficult to enumerate the ways in which wit arises in speech. There are hundreds of ways from which an eloquent person profits at his own discretion, and a separate book would be needed to discuss them in detail. On this occasion we need only say this much: that the basis of wit is mostly words which have different meanings, and by some of whose meanings someone is glanced at. And sometimes in wit such words are not used at all; rather, some person or thing is compared to something which, though incongruous, is similar, and then this comparison is expressed in such a manner and from such an angle that it takes on the character of a metaphor instead of a simile. Similarly, sometimes one magnifies or diminishes oneself or someone else so much that it is far removed from the real degree. For all these things skill (*saliqa*) is needed. One with good skill makes the sharpest innuendo and gives the most disagreeable comparison, yet no one's heart is soured, or no one finds room to express displeasure. In contrast, if some unskilful person tries to do this, people take offence and become ready for enmity. Such good skill in this as the common people of Lucknow have is not seen even in the select people of other places.

A Bengali scholar, Dr Aghornath, who was a great scholar and learned man, unique in philosophy, a Doctor of Literature and a good master of Urdu, said to me by way of objecting to the Urdu language: "Sir, what excellence of a language is this, that once in a gathering I said, '*Ham aj-kal dudh piya karte hain*' (Nowadays I drink milk), at which everyone burst out laughing?" I said: "This is the very highest beauty of Urdu. Since you are deficient in this language, it appeared to you a fault of the language instead of your own fault. In every language there are words with double meanings, and it is the business of masters of the language to use words avoiding all their disparaging aspects. In English the word 'conceive' means both to think and to become pregnant. A famous Lord said three times in Parliament 'I conceive', and stopped to think further. Someone called out: 'His Lordship has said "I conceive" three times and nothing has come of it'; that is, he became pregnant three times and nothing was born. At this everyone laughed and the Lord was abashed. In the same way in Urdu there are thousands of words from which different aspects emerge. If the speaker does not have the right skill in their use, he will be laughed at at every turn."

This is the said phrase "drinking milk". In India "drinking milk" is the business of suckling infants, and to say of some sensible adult "he drinks milk", besides being a reproach, is used in the sense that he is still immature and foolish. With the idea of avoiding this aspect, the people of Lucknow will never say "*Main dudh pita hun*" (I drink milk), but will express this matter in various ways avoiding this aspect of reproach. They will say "Nowadays I make use of milk", "Nowadays my diet is milk", "I eat milk and rice". Seeing these precautions of the people of Lucknow, an able poet of Agra who was a master of the language was misled into thinking that the idiom of Lucknow is "eating milk", not "drinking milk". A gentleman of Lucknow differed with him on this, and I was asked to arbitrate. I said: "Milk is a thing to drink; how can anyone use the word 'eat' of it? But it is certainly true that, to avoid the aspect of reproach, the people of Lucknow will not use the words 'drinking milk' of themselves."

It is not limited to this one idiom; in Urdu, in hundreds of words, aspects of reproach have arisen from different idioms and meanings, and it is the business of every master of the language to avoid them. Or, if someone uses one of them in jest of someone, it is his duty to understand and reply; otherwise it will be understood that he is ignorant of the language.

There is much playfulness and wit among the people of Lucknow. They produce wit in their speech in hundreds of ways, and the more mastery one has in this art, the more he shines and proves distinguished in the gatherings of men of letters. I do not say that this faculty does not exist in the people of other places. It does, and abundantly; and now the Urdu language is advancing throughout India in such a way that wits of the highest class are arising everywhere, and the sense of appreciating and understanding speech is growing. But among the people of Lucknow this faculty, having become second nature, has become their very nature and instinct, and such easy and clean taste in wit and humour, along with delicacy of speech, as is seen in them cannot be found in others.

(9) The ninth thing in the manners of social life is the gatherings of joy and mourning. The former wealth and rule of the Muslims have greatly increased the longings (*arman*) of their women here as compared with most places. From birth to marriage every joy and success of a boy becomes an occasion of ceremony. Right after birth come the *chhathi* (sixth-day rite), the *chilla* (fortieth-day rite) and the bathings in between, the *'aqiqa* (sacrifice for the newborn), the *khir-chata'i* (first taste of rice pudding), the *dudh-barha'i* (weaning), the *Bismillah* (beginning of reading), circumcision, and above all the marriage contract (*'aqd-e nikah*). These are all in themselves occasions of rejoicing. Children's birthdays (*salgirah*) are often celebrated. Besides the said ceremonies, extraordinary occasions of rejoicing also take place on the *ghusl-e sihhat* (bath of recovery from illness) or on the fulfilment of some particular wish.

At all these ceremonies the ladies of the family and many acquainted women of the neighbourhood gather. Women's gatherings are arranged, in which a bright floor-covering of *dari* and *chandni* is spread on platforms of planks, or on the ground if there are many guests. In wealthy houses costly and elaborate carpets are spread over the *chandni* on three sides, or only at the head. *Kanwal*s (lotus-shaped lamps) and *mirdang*s (glass lamp-shades) are lit. And a troupe of *domni*s sits in front and performs *mujra*. The dancing *domni* ties on ankle bells (*ghunghru*), dances and shows *bhao* (expressive gesture). In the course of the *mujra* the *domni*s from time to time perform comic mimicries (*naql*). In any case there are surges of joy and chirrupings of happiness. And although the *domni*s often begin to commit excesses in the *mujra* and increase the shamelessness and immodesty of the company, no difference is allowed to come into the skill of deportment, the ladies' mutual relations and, with that, the observance of rank. For every ceremony there are hundreds of customs (*rasm*) whose performance is considered necessary. The guardians and preservers of these customs are the old women, and with them the *domni*s, who get a good deal on the pretext of these customs.

At most ceremonies there is always a *ratjaga* (night vigil), and this is the one thing which in the belief of Indian women is purely for the sake of God, and in which the *domni*s sing the song of "*Allah Miyan ki salamati*" (God's well-being). There is a keeping awake all night, but not for worship; rather for singing and playing, romping all night, and at daybreak going to the mosque to fill God's niche (*Allah Miyan ka taq bharna*), for whose offering *gulgule* (sweet fritters) and, God have mercy, particular things are reserved [TN: the phrase *khuda rahm* is obscure here; it may be the author's aside or the name of a votive sweet]. At these ceremonies the same proceedings take place in the villages too, but whereas there is ill-breeding and lack of skill there, among the city women there is refinement, neatness, good order and polish.
