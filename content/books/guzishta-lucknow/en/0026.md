# Courtesans, the Rahas and the Stage

## (25)

Although mastery of dancing is peculiar to men, the breadth and universality with which the singing courtesans in general developed this art would not have been possible for men. Dancing is also more particularly suited to women. This is to be seen to some extent in every city of India, but such accomplished dancing and expressive courtesans as arose in Lucknow perhaps arose in no other city. Forty years ago a famous courtesan of Lucknow, "Munsarim-wali Gauhar", went to Calcutta and won distinction there. I saw her at a gathering show a single piece for three full hours with such beauty that those present (among whom were all the accomplished *dhari*s and respected people of Matiya Burj) were lost in wonder and silence from beginning to end, and there was not even a child who was not wholly absorbed. Zohra and Mushtari were not only poets and accomplished singers but also matchless dancers. Jaddan for a long time kept the age captivated by her dancing and singing.

The courtesans here were generally of three castes. First the *Kanchani*s, who were the true courtesans, and whose profession was generally selling their virtue. Delhi and the Punjab were their original homes, from which their coming began as early as Shuja-ud-Daula's time. Most of the famous courtesans of the city are of this caste. Second the *Chunewali*s. Their original work was selling lime (*chuna*), but later they joined the ranks of the women of the bazaar, and in the end they won great prominence. Chunewali Haidar, whose voice was renowned, and of whom it was thought that no one had ever had such a voice, was of this caste, and kept a large company of courtesans of her own community. Third the *Nagarni*s. These three are the beauties of the bazaar who have formed their own groups and keep their communities; otherwise women of many other castes too join this group after falling into a wayward life.

After the singers and dancers there is here another group of the same kind, which flourished greatly in Lucknow, and which it would perhaps not be wrong to call peculiar to Lucknow. They are the people of the *rahas*. The *rahas* is the special art of Mathura and Braj, and it was the *rahas-dhari*s of those places who came and gave Lucknow a taste for it.

When Wajid Ali Shah took a liking to the *rahas*, he prepared a new *rahas* to his own taste and on a plot of his own imagining. As soon as the people saw it, a particular desire arose among them to see love stories, which in those days were mostly concerned with the beauty and love of fairies, shown in action. Seeing this inclination of the public, Mian Amanat, a famous poet with mastery in wordplay, composed his *Indar Sabha*, in which the first example was seen of the mingling of the Muslims' Persian taste with the mythology of the Hindus.

As soon as this *Indar Sabha* was shown in the city, everyone was enraptured. Suddenly scores of *sabha*s were set up in the city, and in no time they became so popular that the market for singers and dancing courtesans was slack for a while. Now besides Amanat many others began to make new *sabha*s, in which Urdu poetry might be spoiled, but the language was being polished and was spreading into the rustic population of the East and among Hindu craftsmen. This taste had laid a firm foundation for drama and theatre, and if the royal era had lasted a few more days, a purely Indian *natak* (drama) would have taken a distinct form on very good principles, entirely original and steeped in Indian taste.

But suddenly cultivated society, in which the old music had made its home, saw vulgarity in these plays. The taste for the art of music turned the gentry back towards the singers and the troupes that performed the *mujra*, and these things that had the character of drama remained confined to the common people and the bazaar folk. But the earlier taste had produced in the city a particular group who showed this taste in action, whom it would be more fitting to call by the present-day term "actors". These actors of ours at first went on advancing in the Urdu language through the patronage of cultivated society, but since they came to be counted among the lowly bazaar people, that cultivated language was lost. Even today these people present scores of kinds of performances in the language of the bazaar.

The greatest reason for the degradation of these actors of ours was that the Parsis of Bombay set up theatres in the English taste, in which, to tell the truth, there was neither the art of music nor proper acting. But their polish, their organisation, their magical spectacle and their glittering curtains strangled our national drama, which was still like an infant in the cradle. People of high society, captivated by the splendour of the *natak*s, forgot true taste.

The truth is that the theatres of Bombay have done India immense harm as regards the arts of dance and song. First of all they ruined music, adopting and spreading through the bazaars unprincipled tunes of a kind than which nothing could be more absurd. After this they sought to wipe out our dance, which was an art of a very high order, and under the name of dance made current on their stage the "drill" of Europe, in which a few boys create interest by changing their order and attitude. But the music and acting of the *rahas* people, though both are defective, are steeped in the colour of the homeland and have a national taste. What is needed is not to abandon them but to reform them.
