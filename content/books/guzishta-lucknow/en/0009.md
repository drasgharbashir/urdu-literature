# Wajid Ali Shah at Matiya Burj

## (8)

It was Wajid Ali Shah's great good fortune that, as soon as he was parted from his crown and throne, at the end of 1285 of the Muhammadan era (1856 CE), he left Lucknow for Calcutta, to pursue his case formally and, if he did not succeed at the Governor-General's court, to go to London and lay it before Parliament and the Queen of England. So when nothing came of it in Calcutta, he resolved to go to England; but the physicians thought a sea voyage harmful for the King, and his advisers dissuaded him. The result was that the King himself stayed in Calcutta, but sent the heir apparent to England with his mother and brother. On this journey my maternal grandfather, the late Munshi Qamar-ud-Din, went with this ruined royal party. The King refused to accept the allowance proposed by the English government and insisted: "We will take nothing but our crown and throne, which were seized from us without fault."

The King was in Calcutta, his family in London, and the case under consideration, when suddenly the quarrels over the cartridges and the government's obstinacy brought about the Mutiny in 1286 of the Muhammadan era (1857 CE), and such a fire broke out from Meerut to Bengal that the houses of friend and stranger alike went up in flames, and so great a turmoil arose that the very foundation of British government in India seemed to be shaken. Just as the rebels of Meerut and other places had gathered from every side in Delhi and made Zafar Shah Emperor of India, so the rebels of Allahabad and Faizabad reached Lucknow in 1857 with great fervour and tumult. As soon as they came, many idle men here too rose up, and, finding no other member of the royal family of Awadh, they set on the throne a ten-year-old child of Wajid Ali Shah's, Mirza Birjis Qadr, and his mother, Nawab Hazrat Mahal, became the regent with full powers over the state. The small English force that was here, and with it all the European officials of the realm who escaped the rebels with their lives, shut themselves up in the Baillie Guard, round which earthworks had been raised before the rebels arrived and ample arrangements made for defence and supplies. It was a mercy, or rather good fortune, that Wajid Ali Shah had already left Lucknow; otherwise he would have been made king willy-nilly, his fate would have been worse even than Zafar Shah's, and the stricken people of Awadh would not have had even that temporary support, the court of Matiya Burj, to help them recover a little.

Now in Lucknow, besides the rebel troops of the English army, large numbers of the *zamindar*s and *taluqdar*s of Awadh and the disbanded soldiers of the royal period had gathered, and with them a reckless mob of the city's ruffians and people of every class had joined in. It seemed as if a whole world were besieging a handful of Englishmen. But the difference was that among the besiegers, apart from the city's ruffians and lawless, self-willed pretenders to valour, there was not a single man who knew the principles of war and could unite all the scattered forces into a regular army. The English, on the other hand, defended themselves with their lives at stake, held off the attackers with their heads in their hands, and were thoroughly versed in the modern principles of war.

Now in Lucknow it was the time of Birjis Qadr and the rule of Hazrat Mahal. Coins were struck in Birjis Qadr's name, officers of state were appointed, revenue began to be collected from the country, and the siege too was kept up, merely as a pastime. People praise Hazrat Mahal's energy and goodness. She greatly valued the soldiers and rewarded them beyond their work and their courage. But there was no help for the fact that it was impossible for her to come out of *purdah* herself and command the army. Her advisers were not good and the soldiers were useless. Everyone served his own interest and no one would obey anyone else. The rebels of the English army were proud in the belief that all this existed only by their breath, that they were the real rulers, and that whoever they set a shoe on the head of would become king. A holy man named Ahmadullah Shah, who had come with the Faizabad rebels and fought in several battles, was establishing his own authority separately, and in fact wanted to set up his own rule. In opposition to Birjis Qadr he held a separate court in Lucknow itself, and between the two courts, along with political differences, Shia-Sunni quarrels and bigotry began to show. In short, the rivalry between the King and the Shah kept growing. At last, in November of the same year, when Birjis Qadr had been on the throne only six or seven months, the English army came to take Lucknow, and with it were Sikhs of the Punjab and hillmen of Bhutan, who are said to have committed the most atrocities. In two or three days of bombardment the outline of the new state that had been drawn broke like a spider's web. With thousands of fugitives, Hazrat Mahal and Birjis Qadr fled towards Nepal. Though the Shah, by fighting for two or three days, gave Birjis Qadr the chance to escape freely, he could not save his own life. Defeated, he fled by way of Bari and Muhammadi to Powayan. There someone shot him. The Raja of Powayan cut off his head and sent it to the English, and received a reward and a fief in return.

To clear the town of rebels, the English bombarded the city heavily. All the people were terrified. Men and women abandoned their homes and fled, and such a doomsday broke out that those who saw it still tremble to remember it. Ladies of the palaces, whose faces even the sun had never seen, wandered barefoot through the dust of the jungles. In their helplessness they clung to the skirt of anyone they met, and whoever they met proved an enemy, and Saadi's line came wholly true: *Yaran faramosh kardand ishq* ("Friends forgot their love"). In this state the victorious army plundered the city, and only after Basra had been ruined, and with God's help at last, were people allowed to come back to their homes. [TN: "after Basra had been ruined" is a proverbial phrase for help that comes too late.] Now, after one cataclysm, the peace that was established has by God's grace lasted to this day and grows every day. But the dependants of the old state and the royal kinsmen, who became utterly idle after the revolution and had no ability to profit from the new government, kept on dying out. So the ruin and decline of great, wealthy and honoured families went on steadily for a long time. Quarter after quarter was laid waste, family after family was being wiped out, and most people became convinced that in a few days not a trace of Lucknow would be left. But in the end the policies of the English government, which have established English colonies all over the world, prevailed, and Lucknow survived the ravages of time and recovered. Those who were to perish perished, and those who were left became able to recover. And if Lucknow gets a few more officials like Mr Butler, it is to be hoped that it will progress greatly in future.

It seems necessary at this point in the course of events to set before my readers the rest of Wajid Ali Shah's life and the story of his stay in Calcutta, for without it this history cannot be complete. My own childhood was spent in Calcutta under the King's protection, and if I have so far described past events from what I heard from people and read in the pages of history, from now on I shall mostly describe what I saw with my own eyes.

Three or four miles south of Calcutta, on the bank of the river Bhagirathi (the Hooghly), is a quiet quarter called "Garden Reach", and since there was a sort of mound of earth there, ordinary people called it Matiya Burj (the Earthen Tower). There were several splendid mansions here, whose grounds stretch along the riverbank for about two or two and a half miles. When Wajid Ali Shah reached Calcutta, the Government of India gave him these mansions: two for the King himself, one for the Nawab Khas Mahal, and one for Ali Naqi Khan, who was with the King, to live in. And a large tract of land round them, stretching about a mile or a mile and a half inland from the riverbank and not less than six or seven miles round, was given to the King for himself and his servants to live on. The municipal road cut through this area lengthwise. The two mansions given to the King he named Sultan Khana and Asad Manzil, and when the King took over the Nawab Khas Mahal's mansion as well, he named it Murassa Manzil (the Jewelled Mansion). Ali Naqi Khan's mansion remained in his possession to the end, and after him in the possession of his children, especially Nawab Akhtar Mahal, who was Ali Naqi Khan's daughter and the King's favoured wife, indeed the mother of his second heir, Mirza Khush Bakht Bahadur.

During the Mutiny the rebel officers of the English army planned to raise a mutiny in Calcutta too, if the King would be their ruler. But the King had not adopted this course towards the Government of India when he was parted from his crown and throne, nor did he favour it now; instead he informed the Governor-General of these men's intentions, for which he was thanked. But a few days later it was thought proper to keep the King in Fort William, so that the rebels could never reach him again. The case pending in London on his behalf was postponed on the ground that the country it concerned was no longer even in British hands; when the British state held it again, the matter would be looked into.

The King was still in this custody when the rising in Lucknow was put down, and Masih-ud-Din Khan, who was the King's general agent (*mukhtar-e-'am*) in London, presented his claim again. On the face of it he had every hope of success and of the restoration of the kingdom; but unfortunately a conspiracy arose among those who were the King's advisers and companions in the fort, whether at some outside instigation or out of their own interest. They thought that if Masih-ud-Din Khan won the case, their market would go slack and he alone would count. So they all began to persuade the King: "Your Majesty, has anyone ever taken a country and given it back? Masih-ud-Din Khan has deceived Your Majesty. Nothing is going to come of it, and Your Majesty is suffering for nothing. You have taken no allowance for a year and a half or two. There is want of everything, and we, the servants of the state, are in need of every pice. It is best that Your Majesty accept the English government's proposals, draw the allowance, and live in peace and comfort with the ladies of the palace and the nobles of the court." The King was short of money, and his companions were in even greater straits than he. When the courtiers put this proposal again and again, he wrote to the Viceroy without more ado: "I agree to accept the monthly allowance proposed by the English government. So let my allowance up to this time be paid, and let the case pending in London be dismissed." The reply came: "First, you will not now be paid the allowance for the past; it will be paid only from this time. Second, only twelve hundred thousand rupees a year will be given, and the three hundred thousand rupees a year that had been proposed for your servants are no longer thought necessary."

In all probability the King would not have accepted this loss, but the courtiers persuaded him to agree even to this, and the Government of India informed England that Wajid Ali Shah had accepted the government's proposal, so his case should be dismissed. I heard these events myself from my grandfather, Munshi Qamar-ud-Din, who was chief secretary (*mir munshi*) of the office attending Her Highness (the Queen Mother) and the special deputy of Maulvi Masih-ud-Din Khan, and all the proceedings passed through his hands. As soon as the news reached London that the King had agreed to the allowance, Masih-ud-Din Khan was thrown into confusion. The King's mother, his brother and the heir apparent beat their heads, and were bewildered at what calamity had happened. Alas, everything done until then was turning to dust. At last Masih-ud-Din Khan, after much thought, hit upon a point, and put this legal objection before Parliament: "The King is at present in the custody of the Government of India, and in such a state no writing of his can be held reliable." The objection was reasonable and was accepted, and the Government of India was informed of the King's agent's plea. At the same time Masih-ud-Din Khan and all the members of the royal family wrote to the King: "What calamity are you bringing about? We have every hope of getting the country of Awadh back." By now the Mutiny had been put down. The government released the King, and he came happily out of the fort to Matiya Burj. He had hardly gained his freedom when the courtiers said: "Your Majesty, Masih-ud-Din Khan is saying in London that Your Majesty agreed to take the allowance only because you were a prisoner." At this the King flared up and wrote at once: "I accepted the government's proposal freely and of my own will and wish, and Masih-ud-Din Khan's statement that I agreed while a prisoner or under any compulsion or duress is entirely false. Accordingly I hereby revoke for the future the very power of attorney by which he was made my general agent."

Now what was left? The whole proceeding was at an end. The King began to hold his revels in Matiya Burj, money rained into the courtiers' houses, and the broken-down party of the royal family lying in England was very nearly ruined there. Most of the attendants deserted them. The King's mother, Her Highness (*Janab-e-Aliya*), fell ill from the shock, and in this illness set out to go by way of France to the holy places and, having had the honour of visiting them, to reach Calcutta. But death did not let her go beyond Paris. She died there and was buried in a Muslim cemetery next to the mosque of the Ottoman embassy in France. Mirza Sikandar Hashmat was so grieved at his mother's death that he fell ill as soon as she died, and fourteen or fifteen days after her he too was laid beside his mother to await the Day of Judgement. The heir apparent alone came back to Calcutta to rejoin his parents.

They say that at first the King's life in Matiya Burj too was one of great alertness and good sense. Seeing this, the people about him procured a few musical instruments. At once the whole sense of "the song reminded him of the garden" came true, and a company of musicians and dancers began to gather there too. The finest singers of India came and took service, and such a gathering of musicians formed at Matiya Burj as was to be found nowhere else.

There was the same taste there for gathering beautiful women and being caught up in the wiles of beauty and love as is heard of in Lucknow. But in Matiya Burj full regard was paid to religious scruple in this taste. The King was a Shia, and in Shia law *mut'a* (temporary marriage) is permitted without any limit or restriction. Taking advantage of this religious freedom, the King satisfied his taste to his heart's content, and his rule was that he would not bear even to look at the face of a woman to whom he was not married by *mut'a*. This scruple went so far that he contracted *mut'a* with a young water-carrier woman who brought water to the women's quarters in his presence, and gave her the title Nawab Ab-rasan Begum (Water-Bringing Begum). A young sweeper woman who came and went in his presence also joined the *mut'a* wives and was honoured with the title Nawab Musaffa Begum (Begum of Purity). In the same way his taste for music was confined to his *mut'a* wives. It can only rarely have happened that the King watched a performance by a public courtesan. Various troupes had been formed of the *mut'a* wives themselves, who were taught dance and song in various styles: the Radha Manzil troupe, the Jhumar troupe, the Latkan troupe, the Sardha Manzil troupe, the Nath (Nose-ring) troupe, the Ghunghat (Veil) troupe, the Rahas troupe, the Naqal (Mime) troupe, and scores of groups like these, who had been given the highest training in dance and song, and it was in their dancing and singing that he found his amusement. All of them had been married by *mut'a* and were called begums. If in one or two groups there were young, under-age girls not yet married, it was because they would be taken in among the *mut'a* wives after they came of age. Most of them lived near the King himself in the Sultan Khana, and some had been given separate women's quarters in the other mansions. Those of the *mut'a* wives who bore children were given the title of Mahal (Palace), were given separate women's quarters to live in, and had their allowance and rank increased.

From this it is clear that in every respect except music the King was very devout, abstinent and observant of the religious law. He never missed a prayer. He kept all thirty fasts. All his life he abstained from opium, wine, *falak-sair* (a hemp preparation) and every other kind of intoxicant. And he observed the mourning of Muharram with the most sincere devotion.

His third passion was building. Scores of women's quarters were built round the Sultan Khana, and many new mansions with women's quarters in them. From the government he had received only the Sultan Khana, Asad Manzil and Murassa Manzil. But in a few days the King's passion had scores of mansions built, surrounded by the airiest gardens and the most delightful flower-beds. At the time I saw it the King held the following splendid mansions, which ran in order from south to north: Sultan Khana, Qasr-ul-Baiza (the White Palace), Gosha-e-Sultani (the Royal Retreat), Shahanshah Manzil, Murassa Manzil, Asad Manzil, Shah Manzil, Nur Manzil, Tafrih Bakhsh, Badami (the Almond-coloured), Asmani (the Sky-blue), Tahniyat Manzil, Sad Sultani, Sadd Sultani and Adalat Manzil. Besides these there were several other mansions whose names I no longer remember.

Inside the gardens attached to them, on the edges of ponds, were many rooms, bungalows and small mansions. In all these mansions, separate rooms, bungalows and pavilions, clean and costly carpets were always spread. Silver beds stood fully made with bedding and pillows. They were furnished with pictures and all kinds of furniture. And, simply out of the wish to provide for people, more housekeepers (*makandar*s) than were needed were employed, who dusted every day and kept everything clean and in order. In short, each mansion in itself looked so well appointed and adorned that one was lost in admiration. The gardens and flower-beds round the mansions were laid out in such geometrical patterns and Euclidean figures that onlookers marvelled at the King's natural sense of proportion.

In Lucknow the King had built only the Qaisar Bagh and a few buildings near it, or his late father's *imambara* and tomb; but in Matiya Burj he had founded a beautiful city of fine and splendid buildings. Across the river, right opposite Matiya Burj, is the famous Botanical Garden of Calcutta, but it was eclipsed by the earthly paradise of Matiya Burj and its charming wonders. All these buildings, flower-beds, bowers and wide, delightful meadows were enclosed by high walls; but along the municipal highway, for about a mile, there were fine shops, and only those servants of the lower grade lived in them whose duties required them to live there. But no way in was left through any shop; the only entrances were the gates, which were guarded. Over the gate of the Sultan Khana itself was a most splendid drum-house. The drummers sounded the *naubat*, and day and night the gong was struck by the reckoning of the old watches and hours.

Thousands of kings with a passion for building have passed through the world, but probably no crowned head has had built by his own efforts as many buildings and gardens as Wajid Ali Shah built in his frustrated life and in the brief period of his nominal kingship. If after Shah Jahan any name can be mentioned in this particular respect, it is the name of this ill-used King of Awadh. It is another matter that one man's particular building survived for hundreds or thousands of years, while time wiped out another's hundreds of buildings in a few days.

Besides building, the King had a passion for animals, and he carried this passion too to such a pitch that the world cannot show its equal, and perhaps no private effort has ever reached even half its level.

In front of the Nur Manzil a wide park (*ramna*) had been made, enclosed by an elegant iron railing, in which hundreds of *chital*, deer and wild quadrupeds roamed free. In the middle of it was a solid marble tank, always full to the brim, in which ostriches, cassowaries, *fil-murgh* (turkeys), cranes, geese, egrets, demoiselle cranes, swans, peacocks, partridges and hundreds of kinds of birds, and tortoises too, had been let loose. Such care was taken over cleanliness that not a dropping or even a feather of any animal could be seen anywhere. On one side, on the edge of the tank, there were lions in cages, and right beside this park began a series of large wooden cages with bars, in which scores of kinds of monkeys, brought from God knows where, had been collected, who performed strange antics and would not let anyone pass without showing him their tricks.

Fish were kept in tanks in various places, which gathered at a signal and, if someone threw in something to eat, made a fine show with their leaping and splashing. To crown all, in front of the Shahanshah Manzil a large, long, deep tank had been made, its edges made very smooth all round and sloping inwards, and in the middle of it an artificial mountain had been built, through which hundreds of channels had been run, and at one or two places at the top a spring of water had been made to flow. Thousands of large snakes, two or three yards long, had been let loose on this mountain, and they ran and crawled about continually. They would climb to the top of the mountain and come down again. Frogs were let loose, and the snakes would dart after them and catch them. Round the mountain ran a channel in the manner of a canal, in which the snakes glided swaying along chasing the frogs, and people stood nearby without fear watching the sight. At the foot of the mountain were also two cages in which two large leopards were kept. Usually they lay quiet, but when a cock was brought and let in, they would pounce on it and swallow it whole. Probably no arrangement for keeping snakes had ever been made anywhere before, and this was Wajid Ali Shah's own invention, which European travellers watched in astonishment, making pictures of it and writing descriptions.

Besides the animals mentioned, there were thousands of brass cages of chirping birds inside the Sultan Khana itself. There were scores of great halls protected with iron netting, which were called *ganj*es (aviaries). Birds of every kind had been brought in great numbers and let loose in them, and everything needed for them to live and thrive had been provided. The King's effort was to collect every kind of beast and bird that could be obtained, and truly perhaps no such complete living museum existed anywhere on the face of the earth. Money was spent without restraint in obtaining these animals, and whoever brought a new animal got whatever price he asked. They say that the King bought a pair of silk-feathered pigeons for twenty-four thousand rupees and a pair of white peacocks for eleven thousand. There was a pair of giraffes too, a very large and most strange animal of Africa. Two-humped Baghdadi camels are not to be seen anywhere in India, but the King had them. Elephants are never kept in Calcutta, but in the King's living natural history museum there was an elephant too. Simply so that no animal should be missing, two donkeys too had been brought and let loose in the park. Of the wild beasts, African lions, Indian lions, cheetahs, leopards, bears, caracals, *charkh*s (hyenas), wolves were all kept in cages and looked after with great care.

The arrangements for the pigeons were separate from those for the other animals. In the King's various mansions there were in all twenty-four or twenty-five thousand pigeons, in flying which the pigeon-fanciers had shown great skill.

Some idea of what was being spent on the animals may be had from the fact that there were more than eight hundred animal keepers, about three hundred pigeon keepers, about eighty fish keepers, and thirty or forty snake keepers, who were paid from ten rupees a month down to six rupees a month. The officers' salaries were from thirty down to twenty rupees. And besides the pigeons, snakes and fish, a little under nine thousand rupees a month was spent on feeding the other animals.

The building work was mostly in the charge of Munis-ud-Daula and Raihan-ud-Daula, who received about twenty-five thousand a month under the head of buildings.

There were about a thousand guards, whose pay was usually six rupees a month, some getting eight or ten. The same pay went to the housekeepers, who numbered more than five hundred. The gardeners got the same pay too, and they also numbered more than five hundred. There were about eighty men of the pen, that is, clerks, who were paid from thirty down to ten rupees a month. The honoured companions and high officials, who were paid eighty-eight rupees a month, will not have numbered fewer than forty or fifty. There were more than a hundred palanquin-bearers (*kahar*s). Besides these there were scores of small departments: the kitchen, the water-cooling room (*abdar-khana*), the prison, the *khas* room (cooled with screens of scented grass), and God knows what else. Then there was a head for the dependants of the begums, that is, the relatives and kinsfolk of the *mut'a* wives, who received allowances according to their standing.

All these people had built houses outside the area of the mansions, mostly on the land given to the King and many on other land nearby, and a city had grown up with a population of more than forty thousand. The lives of all of them depended on the King's allowance of a hundred thousand rupees a month, and no one could understand how so vast a multitude managed to live on so small a sum. Among the common people of Bengal it was said that the King had the philosopher's stone (*paras*), and that when he needed money he would rub iron or copper on it and turn it into gold.

The fact is that through the King's residence a second Lucknow had grown up next door to Calcutta. The real Lucknow had been wiped out and its choicest society had gone to Matiya Burj; indeed, the truth is that in those days Lucknow was no longer Lucknow; Matiya Burj was Lucknow. There was the same bustle, the same language, the same poetry, the same gatherings and witticisms. The scholars and learned men were from there, the nobles and grandees were from there, and the common people were from there. No one could see that he was in Bengal. There were the same kite-flying matches, the same cockfights, the same quail fights, the same opium-eaters, the same storytelling (*dastan-go'i*), the same *ta'ziya* rites, the same recitation of *marsiya*s (elegies) and *nauha*s (dirges), the same *imambara*s and the same *karbala*. Indeed, the procession and magnificence with which the King's *zarih* (replica shrine) was carried out could perhaps have been matched in Lucknow only in royal times; after the Mutiny no *ta'ziya* ever went out like it. Thousands of people from Calcutta, even Englishmen, came to Matiya Burj to see it.

Although the King was a Shia, there was not the least bigotry in his temperament. His old saying was: "Of my two eyes, one is Shia and one is Sunni." Once two men came to blows over a religious difference. The King ordered both to be dismissed, indeed barred them from ever serving him again, and said: "There is no place for such people with me." Towards the very end some offensive words were printed in one of the King's books, at which there was a great uproar among the Sunnis of Calcutta. But people do not know that those words were not in the book itself but in someone else's chronogram or commendation (*taqriz*), and that as soon as the King was told, he was ready to apologise without anyone urging him. What greater proof of freedom from bigotry could there be than that the whole business of administration was in Sunni hands? The chief minister, Munsir-ud-Daula Bahadur, was a Sunni. The Munshi-us-Sultan, who was at one time the most favoured of all and the chief officer of the whole animal house, all the clerks and several other departments, was a Sunni. The paymaster (*bakhshi*) Amanat-ud-Daula Bahadur, through whose hands all the servants, even the ladies of the palace and the princes, received their allowances, was a Sunni. Utarid-ud-Daula and the steward (*daroga*) Taqi Ali Khan, who at the end were the highest officials and in charge of the whole business, were both Sunnis. What more could there be than that the management of the *imambara* Sibtainabad and of the palace's own *imambara*, Bait-ul-Buka (the House of Weeping), and the arrangements for holding mourning assemblies and religious ceremonies, were also in Sunni hands? No one there ever even noticed who was Sunni and who was Shia.

Even the shopkeepers and moneylenders of Matiya Burj were from Lucknow, and there was nothing of Lucknow that was not to be found there in its most perfect form. Wherever you went you saw a wonderful splendour and bustle, and people were so absorbed, intoxicated and carried away by these delights that no one gave a thought to the end. The people of Lucknow, all the servants and indeed all the residents of Matiya Burj, had general freedom to enter the royal buildings, the park and so on. If you strolled in the gardens, you could find no airier place anywhere. If you stood on the riverbank you saw a wonderful sight. Ships coming to and going from Calcutta passed in front, beginning from just here to lower their flags in salute to Fort William, and people thought they were saluting the King. If you stood at the gateways (*deorhi*s) of the palaces and the doors of the women's quarters, amid the wonderful bustle you would sometimes catch sight of such faces, and hear such eloquent and charming speech and such delightful, lovely talk, that a man would relish it for a long time, indeed for the rest of his life.

Ah, this beautiful and enchanting picture did not deserve to be erased, but alas, time erased it all the same, and so erased it that it was as if it had never been. In 1314 of the Muhammadan era (1887 CE) the King's eyes suddenly closed, and it became clear that "What I saw was a dream; what I heard was a tale." Everything was a dream and a fancy. It was an enchantment that suddenly broke, and that beautiful spot, which the sovereigns of Europe and the rulers of India longed to visit, is today a wilderness of extinction and a house of warning, where there is nothing at all. What can one who once saw its former colour do now, seeing the desolation there, but heave a cold sigh with the utmost regret and grief and say, "God's name alone endures!"?
