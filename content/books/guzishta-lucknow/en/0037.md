# Shawls, Shoes and Women's Dress

## (36)

Over the *angarkha* or *chapkan* and the like, the use of the *doshala* (double shawl) is seen more in earlier days, and this was what used to be granted from royal courts as a robe of honour (*khil'at*). Along with this the wearing of a shawl *rumal* (square scarf) was also customary to an ordinary extent. Both these things came from Delhi to Lucknow, but in Lucknow wearing the *rumal* was more customary. In winter mostly the shawl *rumal*, and in times of cold the *doshala*, was worn. After the court was established in Lucknow, when refinement, delicacy and lightness began to advance in dress for the summer, *rumal*s of *barlet* and *chikan* were invented, and this became the dress of all the gentlemen in white: on the head a *chikan* *chaugoshiya* cap stretched on the mould, on the body an *angarkha*, on the legs wide-legged trousers, and on the shoulder a light *rumal* of *chikan* or net. This was the first general style of the gentry of Lucknow, which the family of the late Mir Anis is keeping up to this day with all those former refinements.

The last and very important thing in dress is the shoe. Before the coming of the Muslims there was no custom of shoes at all in India, because the Hindus on religious grounds avoided the use of leather; instead of shoes, wooden *kharaon* (sandals with a toe-knob) were worn here, which besides some of the *faqir*s and ascetic *rishi*s of today were also current among the ancient Rajas. The Muslims brought with them here, along with sewn clothing, leather shoes too.

The first shoe of the Muslims among the Arabs was only a leather sole, which was fastened to the foot by straps or ties. The leather sock (*mauza*) of the Persians and Romans had reached Arabia before the shoe. Then when the Arab courts were established in the lap of Syria and Iraq, that is, of Rome, the use of leather shoes began. But those first shoes were apparently plain simple slippers (*zer-pa'i*). Wearing these the Muslims came to India.

The nobles and kings of Delhi in earlier days are seen in their pictures wearing high-heeled slipper-like shoes. In the last period of Delhi the *charhwan* (upturned) shoe was invented, whose original style was that half the toes and the foot down to below the ankle were hidden in it. At its tip a broad point was bent over and set down on the toes. This was the first *Dilliwal* shoe, which was much in use fifty years ago. After this came the *Salim-shahi* shoe, which was probably invented in the time of Jahangir. Its point stood out in front and was raised, and the small fine end of the point was turned upwards. After its invention strong work in *kalabattun* began to be made on it, which was entirely genuine and costly. Although this work was made on shoes of both styles, *Dilliwal* and *Salim-shahi*, the *Salim-shahi* shoe came very much more into use, and in a few days it wiped out the old *Dilliwal*; and its greatest merit is that now, when English style and cut have wiped out all our dress and all our things, it survives and is generally popular to this day, and most men of style who prefer the Indian manner wear it with the heaviest dress, and at present in Lucknow too many people wear it.

But in Lucknow in the royal period a *khurd-noka* (small-pointed) shoe of a new cut was invented, which the men of style here liked very much at first. It had no point at all; rather, the point that in the *Dilliwal* and *Salim-shahi* was brought out on top was, after sewing, turned back inside. Near the tip there remained only a slight swelling. These shoes were made of red *nari* (goatskin) extremely light and neat, and the old taste for refinement and lightness made it so light that a pair from the hands of some shoemakers weighed no more than four or five *paisa*s. Although for the common people and villagers the *chamraudha* (rough leather) shoes of this same style were so heavy that they were not less than a seer or a seer and a half each, and then were made still heavier by soaking them again and again in mustard oil.

A little later attention was turned in Lucknow to the adornment and beauty of this *khurd-noka* shoe. First, for the dry weather of winter and summer, they began to be made of Kashani velvet, and for the rains of *kimukht* (shagreen); and there is no doubt that the broadcloth (*banat*) shoe was most refined, simple, light and handsome. The *kimukht* was of a verdigris-green colour, made from the hide of a horse or ass, in which grains were raised like the prickles of a jackfruit, and its praise was that however wet it got in the rains its colour and look did not change. Although the art of making *kimukht* itself had come from outside, many workshops for it were started in Lucknow, and it began to be made better than anywhere else.

A few days later the adornment of shoes advanced further, and shoes of *karchob* work with *salma* and *sitara* (gold wire and spangles) began to be made, on which tassels of *muqaish* were fixed, producing a wonderful glitter and lustre. After this, when imitation *salma* and *kalabattun* came, upturned shoes of imitation work began to be made, which at very low prices made a wonderful show.

But along with the *charhwan* there was current here a *ghetla* shoe, which in fact was derived from the old slipper-like shoes and was generally worn among nobles of high rank and most gentlemen of the upper class. This in truth was the old national shoe of India, and the *chappal* of Hyderabad and the country shoes of other places are memorials of it, and it is this that is seen on the feet of the courtiers of former days and the native elders of the past. The *ghetla* advanced so far that its point, instead of remaining short, was greatly lengthened and spread like an elephant's trunk and wound in the form of a large ring over the toes. This shoe was the ornament of the feet of all the former kings of Awadh and the ministers and nobles. After its invention the *charhwan* shoe began to take its place, until by the time of the Mutiny the *ghetla* remained only on the feet of women, for whose delicate feet it was the general wear, and it passed entirely out of men's dress. But the *kafsh* survives to this day in its original form, being reserved for the pious and the *'ulama* of the Shi'as of Ali, especially the *mujtahid*s.

*Ghetla* shoes, *kafsh*es and the *karchob* work made on them produced two particular trades among the Muslims of Lucknow, on which the livelihood of many people came to depend. First, the Muslim shoemakers (*mochi*), who form here a separate community and brotherhood. These people consider it against their respectability to make any kind of shoe except the *ghetla*. In Lucknow there were many households of these people, all true Muslims, decently dressed, and distinguished in comparison with other people of the lower class, and in former days they lived in great comfort. But now the result of the change of the old style and dress has been that after the men the women too have entirely given up the *ghetla* shoe, and in the market which used to be full of *ghetla* shoes of the highest quality, if now a pair or two of this style is to be found at some shop, it is very mean and wretched, old, dull and dirty. The result was that the group of Muslim shoemakers was entirely ruined. Scores of their households were laid waste, and those who remain are right on the edge of the pit of extinction. But one must praise these people's constancy, that they were plundered and ruined, yet did not consent to make slippers or boots instead of *ghetla* shoes and, keeping pace with the march of the times, to advance further than before.

The second group of craftsmen that came into being by the bounty of these same shoes is that of the makers of imitation *aukhi*s for shoes. *Aukhi* is the name for those pieces of various shapes of *karchob* work that are fixed on women's or men's shoes. *Aukhi*s were made here very fine, glittering, of the highest quality, such as could be made nowhere else. And the demand for them was so great that a considerable part of the population lived by making them.

In any case, both these groups suffered loss from the passing of the *ghetla* shoe. Now instead of the *ghetla*, among women slippers are generally in use, and for particular households or particular occasions high-class pump shoes among all gentlewomen. In wealthy households, giving up the *ghetla* shoe, they had begun to wear *tat-bafi* (that is, *karchob*-worked) boots. A few days after them, leather boots that could be taken off the foot without unfastening began to be worn. And now pump shoes are general, and the ladies of those who have adopted the full English style have begun to wear ladies' shoes of every kind.

It seems fitting in this same connection to describe women's general dress too, and so finish the discussion of style and dress.

The ancient dress of women in India was only an unsewn long sheet, half of which was wrapped round and tied at the waist, and half thrown over the shoulder or head as a covering. Along with this, a garment for the breast also comes down from the ancient times of the Hindus, which in upper India is called the "*angiya*" and in southern India the "*choli*".

It appears that this garment existed even in the time of Shri Krishna. In later times the distinction between the *choli* and the *angiya* came about in this way: in the Deccan a pocketed band is brought from behind to the front and tightened between the two breasts with a knot or a button, and both breasts remain pressed and held in this pocket with some uplift. This is the *choli* of the Deccan. In contrast, in upper India the *angiya* is made thus: two cups of cloth are made to the measure of the breasts, which are sewn together for two or three fingers' breadth, and on their upper corners two small sleeves of net are fixed, and below these sleeves two ties are fixed on each side. Prepared in this way, with both arms put into the sleeves, this *angiya* is put on. The sleeves are very short, less than half the upper arm. And putting the breasts in the cups, the ties are drawn across the back and two fastenings are made, below and above. Unlike the *choli*, the *angiya* makes the breasts prominent by lifting them more than their natural shape.

In any case this is an old Hindu garment, and we do not know what reforms or advances took place in it with the passing of time. At first glance the *angiya* appears more advanced and a later reform.

Apart from this, no other garment of women is known in the Hindu period. Sewn clothes and *kurta* and trousers the Muslims brought with them. The women of the Muslims came here from the Persian lands wearing wide loose-legged trousers, which were tied at the ankles with pleats. A few days later those trousers became narrow-cuffed *ghutna*s, whose flare above was loose. Gradually the liking for tightness kept increasing in them, until the upper flare too was reduced, and the cuffs of the legs became so narrow that after putting them on they had to be sewn tight, and when taking them off it became necessary to break the stitches of the cuff. Such trousers are still current today in many cities.

In Lucknow the style of the Muslim *begam*s at first was this same tight narrow-cuffed trouser; on the breast a tight *angiya* with short narrow sleeves; and to cover the stomach and back a strange *kurti* (short shirt), which was cut away in front as far as the *angiya* occupied the body. It had no sleeves and no part of it was on the breast. By means of two long ties that came over the shoulders, it hung on the stomach and back. Over it was a fine pleated *dupatta* of three yards, which was worn over the head, but in the end came to lie only on the shoulders.

The climate of India and the delicacy of temperaments began to make the *mahram* (bodice), the *kurti* and the *dupatta* lighter day by day, until *angiya*s of *lahi* (gold tissue) and *dupatta*s of crêpe entered the fashion of stylish noble young ladies. From the time of King Nasir-ud-Din Haidar the *ghutna*s took their leave, and in their place gusseted trousers with very full flared legs, which were very tight at the waist and in which the *chor-kali*, that is, the crotch-piece (*miyani*), was kept well drawn, came into general use and became the special style of women. These legs were tucked in front below the navel with a refined and graceful manner, so that in walking about they should not trail on the ground and be spoiled and soiled. Near the Mutiny, in the last period of the monarchy, close-fitting *shaluka*s of fine cloth with half sleeves came into use, which instead of the *kurti* were at first worn over the *mahram*, and a few days later did away with the need for the *mahram* too. But even so, owing to the use of very fine cloth, this dress looked naked, especially since the arms remained entirely bare. The result was that instead of *shaluka*s somewhat looser *kurta*s began to come into use. But now all of a sudden, in place of *kurta*s, the English jacket and bodice began to be worn.

Now a comparison of the styles of every province and every city, and with it mutual mingling, has begun, and to some Muslims, or to the ladies themselves, the *sari* has begun to seem more handsome, for which reason nearly half the women of Lucknow, leaving the old style, have begun to tie *sari*s, and it is said that there is more simplicity in it. Although I am not against women wearing different garments to bring novelty and freshness to their beauty, and, in accordance with the line "*Har lahza ba-waz'-e digar an yar bar-amad*" (Every moment that beloved appeared in a different guise), charming their husbands with ever new airs, I am strongly against our national style being given up entirely and our social characteristics being entirely wiped out. The *sari* is an unsewn cloth and a relic of the very earliest and uncivilised period of human civilisation. Simplicity is no doubt an attractive thing, but with many restrictions and qualifications; otherwise complete simplicity lies in nakedness. Dress itself clothes human nature in the garment of its own fancy. So I do not understand what special excellence and beauty there is in the *sari*.

Just as it is the nature of man's temperament that, wearying of his most beautiful wedded wife, he inclines towards other young women, so our young men, sated with the style of their own wives, become infatuated with the dress of the women of other peoples. But remember well that just as you are infatuated with their dress, so the men of other peoples find more attraction and grace in the advanced dress of your women. It is a fallacy of the carnal desires that at present, by proving your own women's dress faulty in your eyes, again and again raises the debate in the country as to what is suitable for the wives of Indian Muslims.

We would discuss this question thoroughly if we were certain that it had arisen purely for the moral and social reform of women. In fact this question has arisen from the same urge of temperament that made the young men wear coat and trousers, adorned their heads with the hat, and left nothing of their own in them but their complexion. So we are certain that this question has arisen only from the eagerness that, like the men, the women too should adopt English dress. We know well that all writing and reading, speaking and listening on this subject is useless, because until it is decided to wear English *saya*s (petticoats), skirts and bonnets (*bant*, the hat of the English women), our social reformers and imitative inventors of fashion will have no rest. Apart from this, however good a reform and modification may be made, they will not be satisfied.

In short, considering this outcome, there is no result in blackening the pages of newspapers and journals on this subject.
