# Wedding Ceremonies: Mehndi and the Barat

## (46)

On the very next night after the *sachaq*, the *mehndi* (henna) goes from the bride's house with a great procession and illumination. It is thought that this is probably a rite of Arab origin. In it in fact the suit goes from the bride's family for the bridegroom, which he will wear when he comes to be married. In this suit there is generally a *khil'at* (robe of honour) in the court style of the old Mughal period, a *shamla*, a *jigha*, a *sarpech* and a jewelled *kalghi*. If fortune allows, a necklace of pearls is also sent with it. Besides the said things there are also ordinary things such as silk trousers and shoes. Often a gold ring also goes. With this suit, ready ground henna is also sent for the bridegroom to apply, which they spread out on many dishes, and in it they set up green and red candles and light them. Many dishes of henna are lit in this way, which give a special splendour and pomp to the *mehndi* procession. With these lighted dishes of henna there is *malida* on fifty or a hundred dishes, which is made by pounding *khurma*s (fried sweets), and is sent in abundance suitable to one's standing. On this occasion a gold *sehra* is also sent with the suit for the bridegroom.

On the day after the *mehndi* the *barat* goes from the bridegroom's side. The former necessary time for the *barat* to go was with a *pahar* of the night remaining, that is, three o'clock at night; but now this time is mostly being given up, and instead of with a *pahar* of night remaining, *barat*s have begun to go a *pahar* after daybreak, that is, at nine or ten in the morning. This delay began from the time of Wajid Ali Shah, the last King of Awadh. The going of his *barat* happened to be delayed, and day had broken. People, with the idea of convenience and of reducing the expense of illumination, began to adopt that same time. Thus now the *barat* generally goes at the beginning of the day and the marriage contract takes place at noon.

In the *barat* as full a procession as possible is assembled. The three customary bands, that is, the old *dhol*, *tasha* and cymbals (*jhanjh*), the *raushan-chauki*, and the organ band (*argan baja*), are always present. If there is advance beyond this, then the *naubat* on horses, kettledrums (*naqqara*), flags, spear-bearers, elephants, camels, horses. And if there is still more ambition, several groups of these same bands are added. The bridegroom, wearing the same suit that came with the *mehndi* and with the *sehra* tied on, mounts generally on a horse, and in the houses of nobles of the upper class on an elephant, and sets out slowly with dignity and gravity behind the whole procession and bands. The bridegroom is called "*naushah*", that is, the new king, and the idea too is that the bridegroom is made a king for one day. But the question worth considering is this: when they make the bridegroom a king, why is there a *shamla* on his head? Why do they not put a crown on him? This gives proof that in India the Muslim occupants of the throne did not wear crowns, but there were plumed *shamla*s on all their heads. The English, from the time of Ghazi-ud-Din Haidar, put crowns on the Kings of Awadh, but native society did not accept that crown, and kept for its kings the same style as of old, and makes its bridegrooms (*naushah*s) kings on that same model. Behind the bridegroom, riding in *pinas*es and *doli*s, are the bridegroom's mother and sisters and related women and *domni*s. The hundreds of customs and charms (*totka*) performed in the house at the time of setting out are many and various, and being absurd are not much worth noticing.

When the *barat* reaches the bride's house in this state, the bride has usually been bathed by then, and the water of her bath is brought out and thrown under the feet of the horse or elephant on which the bridegroom is riding. The bride is given this bath with cold water kept stale for seven days, which is called the water of the *kalas* (pitcher). And in the winter season bathing in this water is nothing less than doomsday for the poor bride. She is bathed with *pan* leaves spread on the *chauki*, and these same leaves go into the *bira* (folded *pan*) that is the very first one given to eat at the in-laws' house.

Now the bridegroom gets down from his mount and goes into the women's quarters. There the *rassi-nagha'i* (stepping over the cord) is performed, and scores of other customs of all kinds take place, which in every group and every family are different and strange. This time is generally when the bride has bathed but has not yet been dressed. She is wrapped in a sheet, and sugar candy is placed on her hand and fed to the bridegroom, in which the bride's sisters (*sali*s), lively women and *domni*s, adding condition upon condition, make every act difficult for the bridegroom.

Having passed this first stage of the wedding, the bridegroom comes out to the men's quarters, where a gathering of merriment is arranged. Relatives and friends, wearing elaborate clothes, sit in order on a floor-covering of clean *dari*, *chandni* and carpets, and in front a male or female troupe stands performing *mujra*. In the very middle of the gathering, at the place of honour, there is a gold-worked *masnad-takiya* for the bridegroom, on which boys of his own age bring and seat the bridegroom, and sit down themselves on both sides of him, so that the bridegroom may talk with them freely.

It is necessary for the bridegroom to show bashfulness in his every manner and every movement. He can neither talk informally, nor can anyone hear his voice, nor can he mix informally with anyone. The *sehra* is over his face, and then, a *sehra* of flowers being tied over the gold *sehra*, he is not left fit for anyone to see his face without effort and long labour. On sitting down in the gathering of merriment, or more often after the marriage contract has taken place, the *sehra* is raised and wound into the *shamla* so that the face may be uncovered. But even now it is necessary for him to keep a *rumal* over his face with one hand, which is a sign of showing bashfulness. And now, even after the face is uncovered, because of this *rumal*, those eager to see his face cannot succeed without being long engaged in the effort.

After the bridegroom has come out and sat a little while, the arrangements are made for the marriage contract, for which all this fuss has been made. If it is a wedding of Shi'a families, two *mujtahid* Sahibs come, one as the deputy and representative (*wakil*) of the boy, and the other as the deputy and representative of the girl. The girl's people, going themselves to the curtain or having it confirmed by trustworthy witnesses, obtain the girl's lawful authorisation, and after this both sit before the bridegroom and pronounce the formulas of offer and acceptance (*ijab-o-qabul*) on behalf of the bridegroom and bride with correct recitation and points of articulation. And if the family is Sunni, some respected Maulvi Sahib, or if it is a village the appointed hereditary Qazi Sahib of that place, comes and reads the *nikah*, the method of which is this: someone among the girl's relatives comes as her *wakil* and attorney, and presents witnesses that such-and-such a girl appointed me her *wakil* before these two witnesses and gave me authority for her marriage. The Qazi Sahib, satisfying himself with these witnesses and enquiring of the *wakil* Sahib the amount of the *mahr* (dower), makes the bridegroom recite the *kalima-e shahadat* (profession of faith), makes him affirm in Arabic all the things which a Muslim must believe in, and after this, saying to him three times, "We have married you to such-and-such a girl for so much *mahr*", makes the bridegroom affirm, "I have accepted." After this he reads a *khutba* (sermon) of prayer and says to the people "*Mubarak*" (Congratulations), and at once there is an uproar of "*Mubarak salamat*". The *nuql* and dried dates (*chhuhare*) that are set in front heaped in trays are scattered among those present to scramble for.

At the time of the *mujtahid*'s or Maulvi Sahib's coming the singing and playing stops, and after the contract, when the Maulvi Sahib leaves, the gathering of dance and song is in full swing again. And after this the bridegroom is again called inside into the women's quarters. In the world of women this is the special time for performing the real requisites of the customs and conditions of marriage. In the women's quarters on this occasion every kind of ridicule is practised on the bridegroom in the course of the marriage customs, and no [illegible] is left unturned in harassing him. Those who perform all these customs are the bride's sisters and the *domni*s. The truth is that for young men about to marry, the wedding is a mysterious lodge (*faramosh-khana*, a Masonic lodge), in which scores of such stages occur as never entered their imagination. The bride, covered and wrapped up, is brought and set down before him like a motionless bundle. She has not yet been dressed in the suit of departure. While bringing her, an effort is made that at the first [illegible] the bride should give the bridegroom a kick. Then *tone* (charm songs) are sung. The bridegroom is made to confess, and a promise is taken from him, that he will be his wife's slave, the most abject slave, and perform God knows what services. After this the rite of *arsi-mushaf* (mirror and Quran) is performed, for which a Quran on a *rahl* (book-rest) is placed between the bridegroom and bride, and a mirror on it, and in this mirror the bridegroom is shown the first glimpse of the bride. But it is necessary that before seeing her face the bridegroom should recite the *Sura-e Ikhlas* [TN: Quran 112]. In this glimpse the bride keeps her eyes closed. The women make the bridegroom plead in all sorts of ways to get her to open her eyes, and in the course of this make him confess every kind of obedience and slavery. After great difficulties and coaxing the bride opens her eyes, takes one look and then closes her eyes again, and with this the customs come to an end.

Now the bridegroom is sent away outside, so that the bride may be dressed in her clothes and jewellery, adorned and made ready to go to her in-laws' house. At this time the *domni*s sing the *babul*, that is, the heart-melting song of departure, and the house of joy becomes a house of mourning. When the bride has been adorned and made ready, then all the relatives and friends of her parents' home and all acquaintances come, weeping bid farewell to the bride, and give her money or jewellery as much as they can afford.
