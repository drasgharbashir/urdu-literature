# The First Cause: Misunderstanding, and Interference in Religion

## The First Cause

The misunderstanding of the subjects, that is, their taking the measures of the Government in the opposite sense.

In describing the things we set out here, we do not mean that these things were really present in our Government. We mean that people misunderstood them in this way, and that this became a cause of rebellion. Had Hindustanis also had a share in the Legislative Council, this misunderstanding would not have arisen.

## Interference in Religion

There is no doubt that all people, ignorant and learned, high and low, firmly believed that it was the Government's heartfelt intention to interfere in religion and in custom, and to bring everyone, Hindu and Muslim alike, to the Christian religion and to the customs of its own country. This was the greatest cause of the rebellion.

Everyone knew in his heart that our Government's orders come into effect very slowly, and that what it means to do it does little by little. So, they thought, it does not order people all at once and by force to change their religion, as the Muslims did, but the more power it gains, the more it will interfere. And the things that came about little by little, which will be described further on, made this mistaken suspicion of theirs ever firmer and stronger. Everyone was sure that our Government would not openly force people to change their religion, but that by secret schemes it would make them irreligious: by doing away with the learning of Arabic and Sanskrit; by impoverishing the country and its people; by keeping people ignorant of the teachings of their own religion; by spreading the books, tracts and preaching of its own religion; and by holding out the lure of employment. The orphan boys of tender age who were made Christians in the famine of 1837 were taken throughout the districts of the North-Western Provinces as a sample of the Government's intention: that it would bring Hindustan into its religion by reducing it to poverty and want in this way. I tell the truth when I say that whenever the Honourable East India Company conquered a country, the subjects of Hindustan were deeply grieved. And I tell the truth when I say that this grief had no other source than this: people knew that as our Government's power grew, and it no longer had to fear opposition or trouble from any enemy or neighbouring ruler, so it would interfere more and more in our religion, customs and usages.

At the beginning of our Government's rule in Hindustan there was very little talk of religion. It grew day by day, and in these times it reached its height. No doubt our Government had no hand in these matters, but everyone thought that all this was being done on the Government's orders, at its hint and with its approval. Everyone knew that the Government had appointed the *padris* (Christian missionaries) in Hindustan, that the padris drew salaries from the Government, and that the Government and the English officers from home serving in this country gave the padris much money for their expenses and for distributing books, and helped and supported them in every way. Many officers of the covenanted service and officers of the army had begun to talk about religion with those under them. Some officers ordered their servants to come to their bungalows and listen to the padri's sermon, and so it was done. In short, this had gone so far that no one knew whether his religion, or his children's, would survive under the Government's rule.

The preaching of the padris had taken a new form. Books of religious controversy, in question-and-answer form, began to be printed and distributed. These books contained words and matter about the holy men of other religions that gave offence. In Hindustan the custom of preaching and of *katha* (religious recitation) is that people sit in their own temple or house and speak there, and whoever wishes and is inclined goes there to listen. The padris' way was the opposite. They went themselves to the gatherings, places of pilgrimage and fairs of other religions to preach, and no one stopped them, only out of fear of the officers. In some districts it became the practice for a *chaprasi* (orderly) from the police station to go with the padris. In their sermons the padris did not confine themselves to expounding the Holy Gospel, but spoke of the holy men and holy places of other religions with great contempt and insult, which caused the listeners great grief and heartache, and sowed the seed of displeasure with our Government in people's hearts.

Many mission schools were opened, and religious teaching began in them. Everyone said they were the Government's. In some districts very high-ranking covenanted officers used to visit these schools and urge people to enter and join them. Examinations were held in religious books, and the pupils, who were young boys, were asked, "Who is your God? Who is your Saviour?" They answered according to the Christian religion, and for this they were given prizes. By all these things the hearts of the subjects were turning away from our Government.

A large objection arises here: if people were displeased with this teaching, why did they enrol their boys? This should not be taken as a sign that they were not displeased. It is rather strong proof of the extreme distress, poverty, hardship and ruin into which Hindustan had fallen. It was only the want and poverty of Hindustan that made people put up with so hard a thing, which certainly caused them heartache and grief of soul, in the hope that by entering these schools their children would gain some means of livelihood and employment. They bore it, but not willingly.

When village schools were set up, everyone firmly believed that they had been started only to make Christians. The *pargana* visitors and deputy inspectors, who went about every village and town urging people to send their boys to the schools, were called the *kala padri* (black padri) in every village. Whenever a pargana visitor or deputy inspector reached a village, the villagers told one another, "The black padri has come." The common people thought that these were Christian schools set up to make *kristans* (Christians). Thoughtful men, though they did not believe that, did think that only Urdu was taught in these schools, that their boys, studying there, would become wholly ignorant of the commandments, teachings, beliefs and observances of their own religion and would turn Christian, and that the Government too intended to wipe out the religious learning of Hindustan so that it could spread the Christian religion in future. In many districts of eastern Hindustan the opening of these schools and the enrolment of boys were done by plain order, and people were told that it was the Government's order that boys be enrolled.

There was much talk in Hindustan of the education of girls, and everyone firmly believed that the Government's aim was that girls should come to schools, be educated and give up *purdah* (the seclusion of women). Hindustanis found this extremely distasteful. In some districts an example of it had been set up. The pargana visitors and deputy inspectors thought that if they worked to set up girls' schools they would win a great name with the Government. So they pressed people by every means, fair and foul, to set up girls' schools, and for this reason people's hearts grew more displeased, and they became surer of their mistaken ideas.

At first people felt some unease about the great colleges established in the cities too. At that time Shah Abdul Aziz, the most famous *maulvi* in all Hindustan, was alive. The Muslims asked him for a fatwa, and he answered plainly that going to the English college, studying there and learning the English language were all lawful according to religion. On this, hundreds of Muslims entered the colleges. But the colleges then were not as they are now; their system of teaching was very good. Every kind of learning was taught: Persian, Arabic, Sanskrit and English. The teaching of *fiqh* (Islamic law), *hadith* (the Prophet's traditions) and literature was allowed. Examinations were held in fiqh and certificates were awarded. There was no religious inducement of any kind. The teachers appointed were men of great standing, trustworthy, well known, learned and pious. But in the end this did not last. The value of Arabic fell greatly, and the teaching of fiqh and hadith ceased altogether. Persian too was no longer given much weight. The form of teaching and the books in use changed entirely. Urdu and English came into much wider use, and this fixed the very suspicion that the Government meant to wipe out the religious learning of Hindustan. The teachers were no longer trusted or learned men. The very students of the college, who had not yet won people's trust, began to be made teachers. So these colleges came to the same state.

While the village schools and the colleges were in this state, and everyone suspected them of spreading the Christian religion, suddenly the Government issued a notification that whoever had been educated at a school and had passed examinations in such-and-such subjects and in English, and held a certificate, would be preferred above all for employment. Even small posts came to depend on the certificates of the deputy inspectors, whom everyone still took for "black padris". Because of these mistaken ideas, a weight of grief fell on people's hearts, everyone grew displeased with our Government, and people thought that Hindustan was being deprived of livelihood and reduced to want in every way, so that, helpless and forced, they would little by little change their religious ways.

At about the same time it was proposed in some districts that prisoners in the jails should eat food cooked by one man, by which the religion of the Hindus would be wholly lost. Though the religion of the Muslims suffered no harm, everyone was grieved that the Government was bent on taking away everyone's religion and was scheming to do so in every way.

All these troubles were working in people's hearts when suddenly, in 1855, the padri E. Edmond sent letters from the seat of government at Calcutta to people in general, and in particular to respectable Government servants. Their purport was that now all Hindustan had come under one rule, the telegraph had made the news of all places one, the railway had made travel to all places one, so there should be one religion too, and it was therefore fitting that they too should become Christians, of one religion. I tell the truth: after these letters arrived, everyone's eyes went dark with fear and the ground slipped from under their feet. Everyone was sure that the time Hindustanis had been dreading had now come: all Government servants would have to become Christians first, and then all the subjects. Everyone certainly believed that these letters had come on the Government's orders. Hindustanis would ask Government clerks among themselves, "Has a letter come to you too?" meaning, "Have you too become a Christian out of greed for your job?" These letters brought Hindustani officials under such reproach that those who had received them hid them for shame and fear of disgrace, and denied it: "None has come to me." People answered, "It will come now. Are you not a servant of the Government?" If the truth be told, these letters were the very thing to confirm and fix the mistaken suspicions of all Hindustanis. And so they did, and no measure succeeded in removing them.

It would not have been strange if some unrest and a certain amount of disorder had begun in the country at that very time, and the circumstances of that time suggest as much. But His Honour the Lieutenant-Governor of Bengal took notice very quickly and issued a proclamation, which brought some comfort to people's hearts, and the agitation that had arisen died down. But it was not uprooted as it should have been. People thought that the matter had been put off for now, and would be taken up again at some convenient time. The letter of the padri E. In all these matters the Muslims were far more displeased than the Hindus. The reason is that the Hindus perform the commands of their religion as custom and usage, not as commandments of religion. They do not know at all the commandments and doctrines of their religion, or the inward articles of faith on which, according to their religion, salvation in the hereafter depends, nor do these enter their conduct. So they are very lax in their religion, and, apart from those customary matters and the restrictions on food and drink, they are not firm or zealous in any religious belief. If things contrary to the beliefs they ought to hold in their hearts are said in front of them, they feel no anger or grief. The Muslims are the opposite. They know well, according to the doctrines of their religion, the things that in their religion bring salvation and the things that bring punishment, and they act on these commands as commandments of religion and commands from God. So they are firm and zealous in their religion. For these reasons the Muslims were more displeased, and it was likely that they would take part in the disorder more than the Hindus, and so it happened. No doubt, just as it is against the principles of statecraft for a government to interfere in religion, so it is wrong and improper for it to hinder the teaching of any religion, above all of the religion it holds to be true. Our point is only this: although our Government was exactly so, things were done in such a way that this mistaken suspicion of the subjects was not removed.
