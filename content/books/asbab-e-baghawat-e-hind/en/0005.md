# The Second Cause: Unsuitable Laws and Methods of Rule

## The Second Cause

The introduction of laws, regulations and methods of rule that did not suit the government of Hindustan and the habits of Hindustanis.

Through the Legislative Council too there was interference in religious matters. Act XXI of 1850 plainly disturbed religious law. Moreover people suspected that this Act had been passed specially to encourage the acceptance of the Christian religion. For it was plain that no one of another religion can join the Hindus, so the Hindus had no benefit from this law. And if someone of another religion becomes a Muslim, the religion he has adopted forbids him to take the inheritance of his forebears who were of another religion. So no convert to Islam could profit from this Act; only one who had accepted the Christian religion could gain from it. For this reason people thought that, besides interfering with religion, this Act was a plain inducement. [TN: Act XXI of 1850, the Caste Disabilities Removal Act, protected the inheritance rights of those who changed religion.]

Act XV of 1856, concerning Hindu widows, disturbed the religious customs of the Hindus. Although there were great debates over it and reports were taken, the Hindus, who are bound more by custom than by religion, greatly disliked this Act, and indeed considered it a cause of dishonour and of the ruin of their families. They suspected that the Act had been passed so that Hindu widows might become their own mistresses and do whatever they pleased. [TN: Act XV of 1856 is the Hindu Widows' Remarriage Act.]

How much harm the rule on the free agency of women, applied in the criminal courts, did to the honour, good name, customs and usages of Hindustanis! Even married women became free agents in the criminal courts. The authority of guardians over women was lifted, and these things plainly harmed religion. The remedy that was left to the civil courts was certainly insufficient and useless. What ought, by religion and custom, to have been put right at once was thrown into such delay and wrangling that it caused still more disorder. Decrees of the civil courts for the restitution of a wife can very seldom have been carried out. Many cases will be found in which the woman has borne two or three children in the abductor's house while the plaintiff is still wandering about trying to trace her.

There are some Acts and laws under which, even when both parties to a suit were of the same religion, civil suits were decided against their religion. We do not mean that our Government should favour any religion. Where the parties are of different religions, justice must certainly be kept in view, provided it does not go against both religions or against an agreement between the two parties. But when both sides are of the same religion, civil suits concerning rights must be decided according to their own religion or their own custom.

The laws for the resumption of rent-free lands, the last of which is Regulation II of 1819, were very harmful to the government of Hindustan. Nothing made the subjects of Hindustan so displeased with our Government and so ill-disposed towards it as the resumption of lands. Lord Munro and the Duke of Wellington were right when they said that to resume the *muafis* (rent-free grants) was to make enemies of Hindustanis and to reduce them to want. I cannot describe how much displeasure, heartache and ill-will towards our Government, and how much distress and hardship in their livelihood, this caused Hindustanis. Many grants had come down for hundreds of years and were resumed on the slightest pretext. Hindustanis thought plainly that the Government had not provided for them itself, and had even taken away the *jagirs* (land grants) that earlier kings had given to them and their ancestors; what then could they hope for from the Government? Even if, in the matter of resuming lands, the Government's excuse is accepted as sound and true, namely that if rent-free lands had not been resumed then some other tax would have had to be laid on Hindustanis to meet the Government's expenses, which must be admitted to have been very economical, this could in no way console the subjects or remove the misery that fell on them. See how, at this time, wherever the rebels issued proclamations to mislead and stir up the subjects, there was no mention in any of them of anything but two things, that is, interference in religion and the resumption of grants. This proves clearly that these two things were the real source and the very great cause of the displeasure of the people of Hindustan, above all of the Muslims, who had suffered this loss far more than the Hindus.

In earlier reigns there was no doubt a custom of the private sale, mortgage and gift of the proprietary right in a *zamindari* (landholding), but it happened very rarely, and when it did, it was by consent and goodwill. The forced, compulsory auction of proprietary rights for arrears of revenue or for debt was never the custom. In Hindustan the *zamindars* (landholders) hold their hereditary landholdings very dear, and their loss causes them the deepest grief. If one considers it, each estate in Hindustan looks like a little kingdom. From old times one man has been headman by everyone's consent; he would propose a thing, and every sharer had the right to speak and take part according to his share in the holding. The *chaudhris* (headmen) of the tenants living in the village would also attend and say something. If a dispute dragged on too long, it was settled by order of the headman and chief of some larger village. In every village of Hindustan a very fair form of a little kingdom and parliament existed. Truly, as much as a king grieved at losing his kingdom, so much did a zamindar grieve at losing his landholding. Our Government took no account of this at all. From the beginning of its rule until today there is perhaps hardly a village left in which some land has not changed hands. In the early days these auctions multiplied so wildly that the whole country was turned upside down. Then our Government passed Regulation I of 1821 to remedy this, and a commission was appointed. This produced hundreds of other troubles, until the work could not be done as intended, and in the end the department was closed.

We do not wish to argue here about what else the Government could have done if it had not fixed this rule for collecting the revenue, and why land, which is held to be pledged for and answerable for the Government's revenue, should not be auctioned. For here we only state that these were causes of the rebellion, whether they arose from necessity or from ignorance. Whoever wishes to see this question argued should see our other opinion, on the method of administering Hindustan. But we will say this much here: to hold land as wholly pledged for the revenue is very open to debate. In truth the Government's claim is on the produce, not on the land.

The practice of auctioning proprietary rights in payment of debts caused much disorder. Moneylenders and men of wealth lent money to zamindars with coaxing words and used many deceits on purpose to seize their landholdings. They brought every kind of suit, false and true, in the civil courts, ousted the old zamindars and became owners themselves. These calamities shook the zamindars of the whole country.

The revenue settlement made by our Government is highly praiseworthy, but it is heavy compared with earlier settlements. In earlier reigns the revenue was collected in kind. Sher Shah fixed the Government's share at one third of the produce. No doubt there were many difficulties in this method, and the Government could expect losses, but the cultivators all prospered and no one had to suffer a shortfall. Akbar the First approved of this settlement, that is, of taking a third share of the produce, and kept it in force, but made the settlement firm, as is recorded in Lord Elphinstone's excellent history and also described in the *Ain-e-Akbari* (Akbar's Institutes). Akbar fixed the classes of land:

First: the class of land called *polaj*, which was sown every year. The revenue share was taken from it every year.

Second: the class of land called *parauti*, which was not always cultivated but was left fallow for a while to recover its strength. Revenue was taken from this land only for the years in which it was cultivated.

Third: the class of land called *chachar*, land that had lain untilled for four years and needed money spent on it to bring it back. In the first year of cultivation very little was taken, and then it rose, until it was taken in full in the fifth year.

Fourth: the class of land called *banjar*, which had lain untilled for more than five years.

There were other lenient terms as well. This settlement in kind was converted into cash in this way: the produce of every *bigha* (a measure of land) of every class of land was worked out by weight of grain on an average reckoning. For example, the average produce per bigha was worked out at nine *maunds* (a measure of weight) of grain, and three maunds of grain from that bigha was fixed as the Government's share from the cultivator. Then the price of grain was set from average price lists, and that became the cash rate for that bigha. And there was this great relief in it: if the cultivator, finding prices high, wished to give the three maunds of grain instead of cash, he was free to do so. In the Government's settlement many of these things were not kept in view. The same tax was laid on fallow land, and no allowance was made for land that had to be left fallow for a while to recover its strength. Ploughed year after year, the land lost its strength, the produce fell, and the reckoning made at the time of the settlement no longer held. In many districts every settlement became harsh. Losses fell on the zamindars and cultivators. Little by little they lost their means, the stock for cultivation grew very scanty, and so the land they tilled was not worked as it should be. This too reduced the produce. To pay the revenue they fell into debt, and the interest on their debts mounted. Many zamindars who paid the revenue and had good means and a decent living became poor. Villages with much fallow land were ruined still further. The Honourable Mr Thomason writes in section 64 of his manual that in the settlement under Regulation IX of 1833 it generally appears that the assessment of good villages was fixed somewhat lightly and that of poor villages heavily. The illegitimate profits of the zamindars were done away with. Though this was very good, allowance should have been made for it at the time of the settlement, and it was not. In short, through these causes poverty had closed in on the zamindars and cultivators, so that, in spite of the peace and security the zamindars enjoyed, the memory of past reigns did not fade from their hearts.

The breaking up of the *taluqdari* settlement (the holdings of great revenue-farmers), though we do not say there was any injustice in it, was a great cause of disorder, especially in the country of Oudh. These *taluqdars* had become rajas, ruling in the villages of their taluqas and taking the profit. Their lordship and profit were suddenly gone. Here too we will not argue whether, had the Government not done this, it could have freed the real zamindars from the hands of these oppressors; that is argued in our other opinion. Here we need only say that the breaking up of the taluqdari settlement was also a cause of the rebellion.

The introduction of stamp duty is entirely a rule of a foreign country, where in effect no revenue is taken from the land. To introduce it in Hindustan and then to raise its price little by little, until it reached its height in Regulation X of 1829, was certainly against the nature of the people of Hindustan, and unsuitable in view of their poverty. Earlier writers have argued much about stamp duty, and many arguments have been put forward that it is beneficial, and stronger arguments that the truth is the opposite. But here we set all these debates aside and think it enough to write this much: such debates are needed in countries whose subjects are educated, prosperous, honest in their dealings and understanding of business. The subjects of Hindustan, who grow poorer day by day, are in no way fit to bear this burden. All wise men have disapproved of this tax. They hold that, however blameworthy and groundless it is to tax deeds, the tax taken on papers in order to do justice is worse still. Besides the burden of expense, in many cases it keeps people from going to law. Mr Mill's book *Political Economy* and Lord Brougham's *Political Philosophy* are full of its disapproval. And however much it is objected to at home, it is blamed far more in Hindustan.

The administration of civil justice in the Presidency of Bengal and at Agra is very good. I find little to object to in it. I know that most officers will be of a contrary opinion and will prefer the system of the Punjab, but this question is very debatable. The law of the Punjab is a summary of the very laws in force in this country. No rules have been laid down for applying them in detail. Every officer is his own master in it. Not every officer's judgement can be sound. Then how many evils are bound to follow! The civil department is more important than any other and needs the greatest care. It is this department on which the prosperity of the country, the carrying on of trade, the growth of commerce and the security of rights depend. In the Punjab this department is held in very low regard. The officers pay it no attention at all; indeed, we would say they have no time to attend to it. The cases needing careful thought that arise in the courts of these provinces, because of the many transfers and transactions and the long period of the Government's rule here, have not yet arisen in the Punjab. When they do, there is no doubt that the laws of the Punjab will not be sufficient to decide them properly. Whatever effect of the civil courts is seen in this mutiny comes to this much: first, the transfers of proprietary rights; second, people falling into debt or under decrees as debtors. Both these things caused quarrels among the people themselves, not resistance to the Government. They caused bitterness among people, and it is the rule that when rule grows slack, disorders break out from people's quarrels. And the chief reason for the bitterness people felt over these two things was that improper transfers and debts had fallen on people wrongly, and they had become subject to false decrees, and for this reason the civil courts are blamed. It should be considered that the slackness, disorder, cursory inquiry and arbitrariness of the officers who decide civil suits in the Punjab will produce far more evils than these. The effect of a civil court does not show itself in ten years. Fifty years from now the Punjab should be compared with the administration and the working of the civil courts of the North-Western Provinces. Still, we now accept that the law on civil suits in the Presidency of Bengal and at Agra needs reform. There is great delay in deciding cases. The high cost of stamps and the many stages of appeal in every case lay a burden on people. Not giving civil officers certain powers held up the decision of cases. Act XIX of 1853 removed some of this, and what remains needs reform. Whoever wishes to see more discussion of this should look at our other opinion, on the administration of Hindustan.
