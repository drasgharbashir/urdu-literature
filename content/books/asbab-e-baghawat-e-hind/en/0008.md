# The Fifth Cause: The Army

## The Fifth Cause

The bad management and neglect of the army.

Our Government's management of the army was always open to objection. The small number of English troops was always a ground for objection. When Nadir Shah conquered Khurasan and two different countries, Iran and Afghanistan, came under his rule, he raised two equal armies, one Iranian, the *Qizilbash*, and the other Afghan. When the Iranian army meant to disobey orders, the Afghan army was there to put it down, and when the Afghan army rebelled, the Qizilbash were there to deal with it. Our Government did not do this in Hindustan. We grant that the Hindustani army was very obedient, loyal and devoted to the Government, but where had it been promised that no order against its liking would ever be given to this army and that it would never be offended by any order? And if this army were displeased, as it was, what provision had our Government made by which such defiance could be put down at once?

It is true that our Government enlisted both Hindus and Muslims, two communities opposed to each other, but because the two communities were mixed together in every regiment, this division did not remain. Plainly, all the men of one regiment, living in one place and drawn up in one line, came to feel a brotherly union and bond among themselves. The sepoys of a regiment thought of themselves as one brotherhood, and so there was no distinction of Hindu and Muslim. Both communities thought of one another as brothers. Whatever the men of a regiment did, they all joined in. Each became the supporter and helper of the other. If regiments of these two communities had been raised so that one regiment was wholly Hindu with no Muslim in it, and another wholly Muslim with no Hindu in it, this union and brotherhood would not have arisen, the old division would have remained, and I think the Muslim regiments might even have made no objection to biting the new cartridges.

Because there were so few English troops, whatever fear the subjects had was only of the Hindustani army. Besides, the Hindustani army was very proud. They thought no one counted but themselves. They thought the English troops of no account. They believed all the conquests in Hindustan had been won by their swords alone. Their saying was, "From Burma to Kabul we have conquered it for the Government." Especially after the conquest of the Punjab, the pride of the Hindustani army grew very great. Their pride had now reached the point that they were ready to dispute over the smallest thing. I think the pride and arrogance of the army had reached such a pitch that it would not have been strange if it had begun to dispute even over marches and halts.

At such a time, when the army was in this state, their heads full of pride and arrogance, and believing in their hearts that whatever they stood out and argued for the Government would have to grant, they were given the new cartridges, which they firmly believed to contain fat, and by using which, they thought, their *dharam* would be lost. They refused to bite them. When the Barrackpore regiment was disbanded for this offence and the order was read out, the whole army was deeply grieved, for they believed that the Barrackpore regiment had done no wrong, since it was a matter of interference with religion, and that it had been disbanded, wholly innocent, only through the Government's injustice. The whole army was deeply grieved: "We have stood by the Government, we have given our heads, we have conquered country after country for it, and the Government sets out to take away our religion and disbands men over a just cause." At that time there was no disorder, because the army had suffered nothing but the disbandment. But in the heart of the whole army, partly from the belief that the cartridges contained fat, partly from grief at the disbanding of the Barrackpore regiment, and most of all from pride and self-regard and the thought that "we alone are everything", a firm resolve was formed that not one of them would bite the cartridges, come what may. No doubt after the Barrackpore incident there was correspondence among the regiments, and messages came that no one should bite the new cartridges. Until then, though there was displeasure and anger in the heart of the whole army, in my opinion there was as yet no intention of making trouble.

Suddenly, by fate, the ill-starred May of 1857 arrived. At Meerut the sepoys were given a very harsh punishment, which every wise man thinks very bad and wrong. The grief this punishment caused the army is beyond words. They remembered their medals and wept to see fetters and handcuffs worn in their place. They thought of their loyal service and looked at the reward they had received for it. Besides this, the extreme pride in their heads, by which they thought themselves very great, grieved them all the more. Then the whole army stationed at Meerut was sure that either they would have to bite the cartridges or this same day would be their lot. In this state of grief and anger, on the tenth of May, the army did that deed for which perhaps no parallel will be found in any history. What choice had that army after this deed, but to carry its mischief as far as it could?

Wherever this news reached the army, the whole army was grieved still more. From what the Meerut army had done, the whole Hindustani army was sure that the Government no longer trusted the Hindustani army, that when it found its chance it would punish them all, and for this reason the whole army no longer trusted or relied on the words and deeds of its officers. They all said among themselves, "These are the things they say now; when the moment has passed, they will all change their looks." I state on very good authority that among the rebel army gathered at Delhi, thousands of men grieved over this senseless deed and this useless revolt. They wept and said, "Our fate made us do this." Then they would say with great regret, "If we had not done it, what could we have done? Sooner or later the Government would have destroyed us, because the Government no longer trusted the Hindustani army; whenever they found a chance they would have destroyed us." At the beginning of the mutiny, when the march on Delhi was decided and the force had not yet set out, some men were plainly of the opinion that the moment fighting began at Delhi, the whole Hindustani army would certainly rise, and so it happened. The reason was that once fighting with the army had begun, it was impossible for the rest of the army to feel secure with the Government. They were sure that when it had killed their brothers it would turn on them, and so they all girded themselves for mischief and went on rising. Even those who had no mischief in their hearts could not separate themselves from the body, because they were part of the army. The subjects of Hindustan knew that all the Government had was the Hindustani army. When the rising of the whole army became known, everyone raised his head. The fear of the Government's rule left people's hearts, and disorder broke out everywhere.

Now weigh this opinion of ours against the circumstances of the Punjab. The Muslims of the Punjab had suffered much oppression at the hands of the Sikhs. They had suffered little loss from the Government's rule. In the Punjab the Government was very severe at the start of its rule and grew more beneficent day by day, the opposite of Hindustan. At the beginning of the rule the weapons of the whole country were taken away, and no one had the means to make trouble. Though the Sikhs no longer had the wealth they once had, the money they had earned and stored had not yet been spent, and the poverty that existed in Hindustan had not yet come there. Besides this, there were three other very strong reasons why the Punjab did not rise:

First, the English troops were present there.

Second, through the alertness of the officers there, the Hindustani army was suddenly disarmed without warning. Because the rivers were in flood and many, and the ferries were closed, the Hindustani army was helpless and could not raise a mutiny.

Third, all the Sikhs, Punjabis and Pathans, from whom trouble might have been feared, were taken into the Government's service, and the lure of plunder was added to it. What the subjects and the job-seekers of Hindustan could get from the rebels only with difficulty and disgrace, the people of the Punjab got from the Government with honour and without trouble. So the circumstances of the Punjab were the very opposite of those of Hindustan.
