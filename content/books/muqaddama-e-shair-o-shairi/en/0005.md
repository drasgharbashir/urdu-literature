# The Conditions of Poetry: Imagination, Study of the World, Choice of Words

## What Conditions Are Necessary for Poetry

We hope these examples have made clear the difference between the words of a poet and of one who is not a poet, and also between poetry and painting. Now we must say what conditions are necessary for attaining perfection in poetry, and what quality it is in the poet that distinguishes him from the man who is not a poet.

## Imagination

The first and most necessary thing that distinguishes the poet from the non-poet is the imaginative faculty, or *takhayyul*, which in English is called imagination. The higher this faculty in a poet, the higher his poetry will be, and the lower it is, the lower his poetry. It is a gift the poet brings with him from his mother's womb, and it cannot be acquired. If a poet has this gift and falls short in the other conditions needed for perfect poetry, he can make up the shortfall through it. But if someone lacks this natural gift, then however great a collection of the other necessary conditions he may command, he does not deserve to be called a poet. It is the power that frees the poet from the bonds of time and age, and draws past and future into the present for him. He tells the story of Adam and Paradise, and of the Resurrection and the Judgement, as if he had seen it all with his own eyes, and everyone is moved by it as one should be moved by a true account. He has the power to give imaginary and non-existent things like jinn and fairies, the *anqa* (the fabulous bird) and the water of life, such reasonable qualities that their picture passes before our eyes. The conclusions he draws do not fit the rules of logic, but when the heart rises a little above its ordinary state, they seem exactly right. For instance, Faizi says:

The blackness of my night is fierce; my star is a piece of the night.

By the rules of logic one could object that the darkness of night is the same for everyone, so how can one particular man's night be darker than all others; and all stars are bodies whose existence cannot be conceived without light, so how can one particular star be so dark and black that it can be called a piece of black night? But in the world in which the poet wishes to show himself, all these impossible things appear possible, indeed actual. It is this gift by which a single word of the poet sometimes sets an army of magic before us, and by which he sometimes expresses in one word a thought that could fill several volumes.

## The Definition of Imagination

To define imagination is as hard as to define poetry, but in a way an idea of its nature can be given in these words: it is a power that takes the store of knowledge already gathered in the mind through experience or observation, arranges it anew and gives it a new form, and then displays it in a charming dress of words that is wholly or partly different from ordinary dress. This shows that imagination works and acts on words just as it does on thoughts. We see that at times a poet's way of putting things is so novel and strange that the mind of a non-poet could never reach it. This makes it plain that it is one and the same thing that acts now on ideas and thoughts, now on words and phrases. Although this power must be present in every poet, in our view it does not work equally in all of a poet's verse; it works more in some and less in others, in some purely on thoughts and in some purely on words. It seems fitting to give a few examples here.

(1) Ghalib of Delhi:

If it breaks, we'll bring another from the market; this clay cup of mine is better than Jamshed's cup.

These facts were already present in the poet's mind, each in its place and in order: that a clay cup is a very cheap thing of little value that can be had in the market at any time, and that the cup of Jamshed was a thing that had no equal in the world. He also knew that in the eyes of the whole world a clay cup has no merit that would make it superior to a thing like Jamshed's cup. He also knew that wine was drunk from Jamshed's cup, and that wine can be drunk from a clay cup too. Now the imaginative faculty arranged all this knowledge in a new way and displayed it in such a form that Jamshed's cup became nothing beside the clay cup; and then it gave this form, existing in the mind, a captivating dress of expression, so that the tongue delights to recite it, the ear is pleased to hear it and the heart is moved to understand it. In this example the power that rearranged the poet's earlier knowledge and gave it a new form is imagination; and when this new form in the mind put on the dress of words and stepped into the world of the senses, it is called poetry. And in this example imagination has worked at the very highest level, both in thought and in words, for with all its perfect simplicity and spontaneity it is very lofty and very astonishing.

(2) Another verse of Ghalib's in the same rhyme and metre is this:

The glow that comes to my face when she comes makes her think the sick man is doing well.

The poet already knew that meeting a friend brings joy and restores a troubled spirit. He also knew that until the lover shows the beloved his wretched state and the pain of separation, the beloved cannot be fully sure of his love and passion. He also knew that some joys can suddenly bring such cheer that no trace of grief, sorrow or pain is left on the face. Now imagination acted on all this knowledge and produced a new arrangement: that the lover can in no way show the beloved the pains of his time of separation, because when the time of pain comes the beloved is not there, and when the beloved is there the pain is gone. In this example too imagination has worked with the utmost subtlety and wonder in both meaning and words, as is plain to every person of sound taste.

(3) Khwaja Hafiz says:

O breeze, tell that graceful gazelle, gently: it is you who have sent us wandering headlong over mountain and desert.

The gist of this verse is no more than this: that only because of the beloved do we wander lost over mountains and through forests. Clearly, if imagination has worked here on the thought at all, it is very slight and brief; but in the words it has shown a wonder that has raised the verse to the highest level of eloquence. Of such speech it has been said: "A phrase that is worth the meaning itself." First, addressing the breeze, which hints that no means is seen of carrying a message to the friend; in despair he has made the breeze his messenger, thinking that since it goes from place to place it may perhaps pass the friend too. It is as if longing has made him so beside himself that he sends a message by a thing that cannot be a messenger and hopes for an answer. Then, calling the true Beloved, whose being has no sign or trace, by the metaphor of a graceful gazelle, than which no better metaphor is possible; and then, in keeping with the gazelle, calling his search wandering over mountain and desert; and then, although the attached pronoun was already present in *dada'i* ("you have given"), adding the separate pronoun of address, the word *tu* ("you"), which shows that nothing but you is the cause of our wandering; and since the message was a complaint, asking the breeze to "tell it gently", that is, to give the message softly and politely so that the complaint does not offend. All these things have raised an ordinary thought so high that even the finest and subtlest thoughts cannot be shown at a greater height.

## The Second Condition: The Study of Creation

Even when the circle of a poet's knowledge is very narrow and limited, the imaginative faculty can draw some results or other from that ordinary store; but to attain perfection in poetry it is also necessary to study the book of creation, and above all the book of human nature, with great care: to look deeply into the different states that man meets with in life; to form the habit of arranging what one observes; to observe with a penetrating eye the properties and qualities in creation that are hidden from ordinary eyes; and, through practice and skill in thinking, to gain the power of drawing at once a common quality from different things and different qualities from like things, and to keep this capital safe in the treasury of memory.

An example of drawing a common quality from different things is what Mirza Ghalib says:

The scent of the rose, the cry of the heart, the smoke of the lamp of the gathering: whatever left your gathering left it scattered.

Another example:

Leave aside good fortune and ill, for Venus has slain me with her glance and Mars with his wrath.

Nahid, that is Venus, is held to be a lucky star and Mars an unlucky one, so the two differ in essence and qualities. But the poet says: leave aside their difference in good and ill fortune; on me their effect is the same: Mars kills with wrath, Venus with her glance.

An example of drawing different qualities from like things is this verse of Mir Mamnun:

What is the difference, Mamnun, between the beloved's stature and Doomsday? It is the same tumult; only here it is cast in a mould.

That is, the beloved's *qamat* (stature) and *qiyamat* (Doomsday) are alike in being a tumult, but the difference is that the tumult of Doomsday is not cast in a mould, while the beloved's stature is shapely, as if cast in a mould.

In short, all the things mentioned above are so necessary that no poet can claim to do without them; for without them the imaginative faculty does not get its true food, on which it grows, and its strength is left at less than half.

The imaginative faculty cannot create anything without matter; it works on the material it receives from outside and carves out of it a new shape. All the great and famous poets the world has known were deeply absorbed in the study of creation or of human nature. When the habit of this study is gradually formed, one gains the gift of looking closely at everything, and treasures of observation begin to gather of themselves in the storehouse of the mind.

## The Poetry of Sir Walter Scott

Of Sir Walter Scott, a famous poet of Britain, it is written that "his chief poems have two qualities that all admit: one, that they do not go beyond reality; the other, that they express each thought in new ways. Wherever he has described the scene of a garden, forest or mountain, it is clear that Sir Walter had picked out all the qualities that were in the spirit of that place. Reading Sir Walter's verse, the very scene rises before our eyes that we once knew by seeing the place itself and that had since slipped from our minds. In these descriptions he evidently did not rely on the imaginative faculty so far as to leave reality aside and be content with fancy alone." It is said that when he was writing the tale of *Rokeby*, someone saw him noting in his pocket-book the little wild flowers, leaves and fruits that grew there. A friend said to him, "What is the use of this trouble? Would ordinary flowers not have done, that you need to examine little ones?" Sir Walter said, "In all creation there are not two things exactly alike." So whoever relies on his imagination alone and ignores or neglects the study described above will very soon find that his brain holds a very limited store of a few ordinary comparisons and images, which he himself will tire of using and his hearers will come to loathe hearing. The man who does not let go of reality in composing verse, and does not build on air alone, has the power to express a thought in as many ways as he likes. His imagination will be as wide as his study.

## The Third Condition: The Search for Words

After forming the habit of studying creation, the second most necessary study or search is of the words through which one must set one's thoughts before the person one addresses. This second study is just as necessary and important as the first. In composing a verse, first to choose fitting words, and then to arrange them so that the person addressed is left in no doubt about the meaning intended, and the picture of the thought passes exactly before his eyes, and yet a hidden magic lies in the arrangement that captivates him: this stage is as necessary as it is hard to pass, for if a verse lacks this, it is better not to compose it at all. Although the poet's imagination has as much part in arranging words as in arranging thoughts, if the poet has not mastered the necessary part of the language and does not seek and search for words with patience and perseverance when composing a verse, the imaginative faculty alone can do nothing.

Those who have the power to move the hearts of their fellow men through poetry know the worth and value of every word. They know well what effect a given word has on the feelings of the people, and what quality its use or omission gives to a statement. If even a hair's breadth is lacking in the order of words, they understand at once what is missing in their verse. Just as a thing cast in a faulty mould at once betrays itself, so if even a slight flaw remains in their verse it immediately catches their eye. The fetters of metre and rhyme often force both imperfect and perfect poets to use a word that cannot properly express the thought; the only difference is that the imperfect poet is content with that word after a little search, while the perfect one is not content with it until he has looked into every well of the language. Unless a poet has complete command of words and great patience and perseverance in searching for them, he cannot rule the hearts of the people for long. A philosopher-poet has said: "A poem does not leap fully armed from the poet's brain; from the first roughness of the thought to its final polish and refinement many stages must be passed, which the hearers may not now perceive but which the poet certainly goes through."

There are some points connected with this subject that should always be kept in mind when composing verse. First, to clothe the thoughts in words with patience and forbearance; then to test and weigh the words, and to remove whatever falls short in expressing the meaning; to arrange the words in such an order that, though in form they are distinct from prose, they express the meaning as fully as it could be expressed in prose. A poet, if he is a poet, will first of all certainly attend to these things at the time; and if for some reason he has no chance to think much about them at the moment, then whenever he looks at his verse at leisure he is sure to have to cut and prune it. That is why the verse of many great poets is found with different words in different manuscripts.

## The Difference Between *Amad* and *Award*

Many people think that a verse that drops at once, spontaneously, from a poet's tongue or pen is finer and more pleasing than one composed after long thought and reflection. The first they call *amad* (what comes of itself) and the second *award* (what is brought by effort). Some give this example: the juice that drips of itself from the grape is finer and sweeter than the juice squeezed out of it. But we cannot accept this view. In the first place, the very example given on this occasion proves the opposite, for the juice that drips of itself from the grape after it ripens certainly takes much longer to be ready than the juice squeezed from unripe or half-ripe grapes. Except in exceptional cases, the verse that is most acceptable, finest, most pleasing, weightiest and most moving is always the one composed after the utmost thought and reflection. It is possible that on some occasion a poet may express fine thoughts, already stored in order in his memory, in fitting words that by happy chance come into his mind at once; but first, such chances happen rarely, and the rare is as the non-existent. Second, how can thoughts that had been ripening in his mind for a long time, like the juice of the grape, be said to have been accomplished in a moment, without thought or reflection? A verse has two things: thought and words. The thought may be arranged in the poet's mind at once, but preparing a dress of fitting words for it will certainly take time. A master builder may conceive a very fine and novel plan of a house in his mind at once, but the house cannot be built on that plan in the twinkling of an eye. To come safely through the rough pass of metre and rhyme and to succeed in the search for fitting words is no easy task. If a day's work is done in an hour, it will not be work but forced labour.

Of the famous Roman poet Virgil it is written that he would dictate his verses in the morning and spend the whole day thinking about them and pruning them, and he used to say, "The she-bear too licks her ugly cubs into beauty." The poet Ariosto, whose verse is famed for its perfect spontaneity and ease, has left manuscripts that are still kept at Ferrara in Italy. Those who have seen them say that the verses of his that seem most clear and simple were written after being cut and pruned eight times over. Milton too admits that verse is written with very hard toil and labour, and that every line of a poem must undergo many successive changes before it is shapely. A poet writing in Persian also describes the state of composing verse thus:

For the purity of a single word he turns night into day, when bird and fish are asleep and he awake.

The truth is that there is no poem, long or short, that has lastingly moved the hearts of the people and that was dashed off without effort. The more spontaneity and ease a poem seems to have, the more labour, thought and revision, one should know, have gone into it.

Ibn Rashiq writes in his book *al-Umda*: "When a poem is finished, one should look at it again and again and refine and polish it as far as possible. If even then the poem does not become excellent and good, one should not hesitate to throw it out, as many poets do. A man is infatuated and enamoured with his own verse because it is his figurative offspring; so if one is reluctant to throw it out, the whole poem will fall from the level of eloquence because of one bad verse."

## The Art of Writing Depends More on Words Than on Meanings

On this question of words Ibn Khaldun says that the art of writing, whether in verse or in prose, lies wholly in words and never in meanings. Meanings merely follow words; the words are the essence. Meanings are present in everyone's mind, so no art need be acquired for them; if anything is needed, it is only how to express those meanings in words. He says: "Think of words as a cup and meanings as water. You may fill the water into a gold cup or a silver one, a cup of glass, crystal or shell, or a clay cup; the water itself does not change. But in a cup of gold or silver its value rises, and in a clay cup it falls. In the same way the value of meanings rises in the expression of an eloquent master and falls in the expression of one who is not eloquent." But we humbly say to him: Sir, if the water is brackish, muddy, heavy or half-boiled, or if it is served when one is not thirsty at all, then serve it in a cup of gold or silver, or of crystal and quartz: it can never be pleasant and its value can never rise.

We accept that poetry depends more on words than on meanings. However lofty and subtle the meanings, if they are not expressed in fine words they can never find a home in hearts; and a trite subject, expressed in pure words, may deserve praise. But to set meanings wholly aside on the ground that they are present in everyone's mind and need no art does not seem right. If the poet's mind holds only the few limited thoughts that the earlier poets set down, or he knows only the ordinary things that ordinary people know, and he has not widened his knowledge to perfect his poetry, nor formed the habit of studying the book of nature, nor gathered more material for his imagination, then however great his command of language and mastery of words, he is sure to meet one of two difficulties: either he must set down again and again the same thoughts the earlier poets set down, with slight changes and in their own manner, or he must seek new ways of expressing each trite and trodden subject, whose acceptance is very doubtful and whose rejection likely.

## What Kinds of Things Should Be Said in Poetry

Besides this, there is another excellence connected with meaning that must be acquired, which has nothing to do with words. The poet's task is not merely to study nature and gather a store of knowledge; it is his task to pick out the qualities that lie in the spirit of each thing and to paint them. The poet, for instance, does not look at plants, flowers and fruits as a student of botany looks at them; nor does he view a historical event as a historian views it. From each thing he picks only those qualities on which the imaginative faculty can work and which are hidden from ordinary eyes. Just as a *niyariya* (a washer of jewellers' sweepings) extracts from sand the specks of silver that no one else notices, so the poet takes from every thing and every event only those matters of taste in which no one else has a share, and leaves the rest. For instance, whatever the historians may have written about the death of Alexander and the events of his last hours, a moralist poet draws from it only this lesson:

Alexander, who held command over a world, at the moment he passed away and left the world,
could not have this granted him: that they should take a world from him and give him a moment's respite.

Or, seeing the extraordinary warbling of the nightingale of a thousand songs in spring, a student of the properties of animals may assign it whatever causes he likes, but a Sufi poet gives it this meaning:

A nightingale held in its beak a rose-petal of lovely colour, and with that petal and that store it was making sweet and piteous laments.
I said to it, "In the very midst of union, what is this lament and outcry?" It said, "The splendour of the beloved has set us to this work."

So to say that the excellence of poetry lies wholly in words and never in meanings cannot be thought right in any way.

## The Verse of the Poets of the Highest Rank Should Be Learned by Heart

Ibn Rashiq says: "The poet should know by heart the verse of the poets of the highest rank, so that he may found his own verse on the same pattern. Whoever's mind is empty of the verse of the masters, even if he writes something from the mere invention of his nature, it will be called not poetry but discredited verse, or coin not of the mint. So when his memory is filled with the verse of the eloquent and their manner is engraved on the tablet of his mind, then he should turn to composing verse. Then the more he practises, the firmer his poetic gift will become."

Ibn Rashiq gave this advice with the Arabic language in particular in mind. Perhaps it suits Arabic, for there poetry had reigned for a very long time. More than a thousand years had passed, in every age and class of which one poet finer and greater than another was to be seen; the language had grown immensely wide; hundreds of ways and forms existed in the literature for expressing every thought; perhaps there it is possible to adopt the manner of the ancients for expressing every thought, without any need to create new manners. But in an unfinished language like Urdu, whose poetry is still in mere infancy; whose literature, fairly judged, is no more than fifty or sixty years old; whose dictionary has not been compiled to this day; whose grammar has not yet been made satisfactory; whose able authors and poets can be counted on the fingers: in such a language, if one relies wholly on following the masters, then just as the swallow's nest has stayed the same since the beginning of creation and will stay so, Urdu poetry will rock for ever in the same cradle in which it opened its eyes.

Ibn Rashiq then says: "Some hold that one should look over the verse of the masters in detail once and then erase it from the tablet of the mind, because keeping it exactly in memory will always prevent one from using phrases and manners like it. But when that verse is erased from the mind, then, because of the colour that has of itself come over one's nature from roaming through the verse of the eloquent, a gift will arise by which phrases and manners like those in the masters' verse will form of themselves, in other words, as need arises, without the thought that this phrase is based on that phrase and this manner is a copy of that manner."

In our view this opinion deserves more weight than the first. Besides the benefit its author mentions, it has the great benefit that until the masters' verse is erased from the mind, one's nature stays bound and confined within the very manners and forms that, through reading and memorising their verse again and again, have become a second nature; because of them no gift arises for inventing new manners and new forms in the course of expression, and so the art of poetry makes no progress.

## Imagination Should Be Kept Subject to the Faculty of Discernment

In short, as said above, three qualities must be present in a poet: one God-given, imagination; and two acquired, the habit of studying the book of nature and command of words.

About imagination it is also necessary to know this: it should be kept as far as possible in balance and not allowed to dominate one's nature. For when it gains too much hold over one's nature and escapes the control of the faculty of discernment, which checks and restrains it, its state is very dangerous for the poet. The imaginative faculty always leans towards creation and lofty flight, but the faculty of discernment limits its flight, resists its creativeness and does not let it take a single step out of order. However bold and high-flying the imaginative faculty may be, so long as it is subject to discernment, poetry suffers no harm from it; indeed the higher its flight, the higher poetry will rise. In all the great poets of the world the lofty flight of imagination and the rule of discernment are found together; their imagination is allowed neither excess in thought nor crookedness in words. But in the other case, when imagination overpowers discernment, its flight is as dangerous for the poet as a very spirited horse with no bit in its mouth is for the rider. The freedom and unbridled licence of this faculty have led thousands of promising poets astray, and those who went astray and came back to the right path did not come back until they had made discernment its ruler. The boldness and lofty flight of imagination grow most when it does not find its food in the poet's mind, that is, a store of facts and events on which it can work. Just as a man in severe hunger, when he cannot find ordinary food, is forced to fill his belly with grass and leaves, ruins his health and often dies, so when the imaginative faculty does not get its accustomed food, it lays hands on unaccustomed food. It carves out far-fetched thoughts with no trace of reality in them and forces the dress of verse on them; it thinks discernment a hindrance to its work and throws off its obedience; and in the end it makes the poet a "talker of nonsense" and an instance of "digging a mountain to bring out a straw".

The treasury of nature is always open to the poet, and there is no lack of true food for the imaginative faculty. So instead of sitting at home making paper flowers and petals, he should go and watch the spectacle of God's power in the mountains and forests and in his own self, where there are unending treasuries of real flowers and petals of every kind; otherwise it will be said of him:

You think that Nature is a game; what can we show you of Nature's games?
