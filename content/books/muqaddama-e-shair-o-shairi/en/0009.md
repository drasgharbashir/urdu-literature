# The Ghazal

## The Ghazal

In the *ghazal*, as is known, no particular subject is stated continuously, save as God wills; separate thoughts are expressed in separate couplets. This form, in its present shape, became current first in Iran, and in India some hundred and fifty years ago. Although the original purpose of the *ghazal*, as the word *ghazal* [1] itself shows, was love subjects alone, after a time it did not stay true to its origin. In Iran many poets, and in India a few, have also brought Sufism, ethics and moral counsel into the *ghazal* along with love subjects.

Considering how wretched the state of the *ghazal* is today, it seems a merely useless and far-fetched form; but since the poet cannot always find occasion to write extended, long, continuous poems, and his imaginative faculty cannot stay idle either, there can be no better instrument than the *ghazal*, the *ruba'i* or the *qit'a* for expressing the simple thoughts that really pass through the poet's mind from time to time, or the fresh feelings by which his heart is truly moved each day on hearing of some event or seeing some state. Some thoughts that cannot be expressed in two hemistichs at all, or not so well, can be shown in the dress of the *qit'a* or the *ruba'i*; and a few simple thoughts that have no connection with one another can be strung together in the chain of the *ghazal*, provided the unbearable restrictions of *radif* (refrain) and *qafiya* (rhyme) are lightened somewhat. If time allows we shall give our opinion on *radif* and *qafiya* on some other occasion; here we state a few things about the *ghazal* itself.

The reform of the *ghazal* is the most important and necessary of all the forms of verse. The educated and the uneducated of the nation are all familiar with the *ghazal*. Children, the young and the old all have more or less of a taste for it. It is sung alike at wedding gatherings, at assemblies of ecstasy and *sama'*, in companies of amusement and play, at the shrines of *faqirs* and among dancing-girls. Its verses are recited on every occasion and in every place as authority or support for what one says. Those who are put off by reading books and have no head for long subjects in prose or verse also read *diwans* of *ghazals* with pleasure. No verse can be learned by heart as easily as the verses of the *ghazal* can by everyone, for in it every subject ends in two hemistichs and the thread of the statement is broken. It is plain that a form so widespread in the nation and so loved by select and common alike must have an effect on the nation's taste and morals that can hardly be overstated. That is why, in our view, poets should first of all turn to the reform of the *ghazal*. But the reform of the *ghazal* is as difficult as it is necessary. It is very hard for the general charm of the *ghazal* to survive reform. Ears that have grown used to *tappa* and *thumri* cannot enjoy *dhrupad* and *khayal*. The thirst of those who listen to romances can never be quenched by historical events. The pleasure there is in talk of lust and self-gratification cannot be had by everyone in pure love and affection. The relish there is in the banter and jibes of rakes and libertines can be found in serious talk only by someone without feeling. On tastes that have taken on the colour of ribaldry and jest, the charm of wisdom and morals does not work. How can those who are infatuated with collyrium, lamp-black, comb and braid reach the reality of beauty itself? But the age is saying aloud: either the building will be repaired, or the building itself will be no more.

Those who made the *ghazal* shine and made it accepted by select and common alike were men who to this day are thought to be people of God and of inward vision, or at least singers of the song of divine love, such as Sa'di, Rumi, Khusrau, Hafiz, Iraqi, Maghribi, Ahmad-e-Jam and Jami. Before these worthies no great attention to the *ghazal* is found. We have said somewhere in the *Hayat-e-Sa'di* that the theme of his *ghazal* was not earthly love, as the outward words suggest; he revealed, or let us say concealed, reality under the veil of the figurative. Every word of theirs shows that they were drenched in the colour of love and affection. There is certainly something in their verse that can be called spirituality. Hearing their *ghazal*, a sense of the instability and untrustworthiness of the world spreads over the heart. They speak of the mole and the down on the cheek in a way that rouses not the worship of beauties but disgust for the worship of the world. They declare the drunkenness of wine better than the cleverness of worldly hypocrites. They prefer libertinism, disgrace and infamy to the gilded patched robe of the Sufis and the hypocritical asceticism of the ascetics. They hold no sin greater than deceit and hypocrisy, no folly greater than pride of wealth and rank, no idolatry greater than self-worship and the worship of appetite, and no deception greater than the world. None of their verse is without effect, and this shows that what they said came from their hearts.

The *ghazal* of these men, though in some respects not suited to the present state of the nation, was wholly suited to the state in which the nation had made the world its prey, or the world the nation. Their verses were as a whip to those absorbed in love of the world and love of rank, heedless of God and drunk with the wine of arrogance. The tyrant, the greedy, the covetous and the miser took warning from them. They exposed hypocritical ascetics, preachers and Sufis. They saved simple-minded nobles from the snares of cunning *faqirs*. They warned and alerted the people of God and the people of sincerity and purity against the thefts and treacheries of the commanding self.

In Urdu this colour, apart from one or two, has generally never appeared in anyone's *ghazal*; but poets who express love thoughts in a natural and simple way have appeared more or less in the ranks of Urdu *ghazal*-writers; yet alas, now this colour too is fading day by day. Artifice in words and baseness and feebleness in thought grow day after day. Instead of finding fault with the present way of writing the *ghazal*, we think it more fitting to offer our countrymen a few pieces of advice on its reform in general.

(1) It has become almost a necessity for the *ghazal* that it should be founded on love subjects. And the truth is that if the *ghazal* is not given the flavour of love and affection, it is as hard in the present state for it to flourish and be accepted as for wine to keep its intoxication after it has turned to vinegar. But there is a difference of heaven and earth between the real and the copy. The feeling there is in love can never arise in feigned love. *Ghazals* written as love poems merely by imitation can have only as much effect as the mimicry of a *bhand* (mimic) who comes into the gathering got up as Majnun or Farhad. Effect follows the state of the speaker and the hearers. If some feeling really exists in speaker and hearer, or at least in the speaker's heart alone, its expression will certainly be moving. When a man who is really wronged or afflicted tells his story, his telling will certainly strike people's hearts; but if the same telling comes from the mouth of someone whose very state belies it, no effect can follow except that people laugh. So for a pious young man who has never felt even a breath of lust, or an old man of seventy in whom the capacity for lust is gone, it is not at all becoming to compose subjects of the pursuit of beauties and the worship of desire in the *ghazal*, so that the one pointlessly slanders himself and the other disgraces and defames himself.

Love does not depend on lust and desire, the pursuit of beauties and self-gratification. A servant of God may have attachment and devotion to God, children to parents, parents to children, brother and sister to brother and sister, husband to wife, wife to husband, servant to master, subject to king, friends to friends, man to animal, the dweller to his dwelling, to his homeland, to his country, to his nation, to his family; in short, to everything. So since love and affection have such range and comprehensiveness, and since to proclaim one's love is a mark of a small vessel and to reveal the beloved's identity a lack of honour, what need is there to confine love to the desires of the flesh and animal appetite, and to show one's own small capacity and lack of restraint by divulging such a hidden secret?

That is why it is our opinion that love subjects composed in the *ghazal* should be expressed in such comprehensive words as cover every kind and sort of friendship and love and every bodily and spiritual tie, and that, as far as possible, no word should be allowed in from which it openly appears whether the one sought is a man or a woman: for instance, cap, turban-band, turban, robe, gown, the green down on the cheek, the moustache coming, goldsmith's boy, minstrel boy, wine-seller's boy, Christian boy and so on; or bodice, chemise, henna, bangles, braid, hair-ribbon, thumb-ring mirror, forehead pendant and so on.

Although (as we have set out in detail at the end of the *Hayat-e-Sa'di*) making a man the object of a man's love, which is current in the poetry of Iran and India, rests merely on a misunderstanding and on a notion of national honour, and not on facts and events, it is nonetheless so ugly and unworthy a custom that it stains the nation's morals; so it should be given up as soon as possible. And one should wholly give up the thought that all the famous poets of Iran and India have gone on writing the *ghazal* in this way. The demand of every age is different. If we today imitated the obscene and shameless things found in the verse of the great old masters of Iran and India, we would be held criminals under the law. So where we have given up many of their absurdities for fear of being called to account in court, we should give up one or two of them at the command of reason and morals alone.

In the same way, to use words in the *ghazal* that point to the accessories and particulars of women is wholly unsuited to the state of a nation that keeps the rule of *purdah* (the seclusion of women); for if the beloved is a wife or a betrothed, then to praise her beauty and draw a picture of her charm, coquetry and grace is as good as introducing one's own honour to kin and strangers; and if she is a woman of the bazaar, it is to beat the drum of one's own unworthiness or ill intent. On this ground, in the *ghazals* of all the distinguished, choice and highest-ranking *ghazal*-writers of Iran the particulars of women are found so rarely that they are as good as absent; and this much survives in India to this day, that though in the *ghazal* the one sought is made now a man and now a woman, and the particulars now of a man and now of a woman are mentioned, the verbs and adjectives used for the one sought are never feminine but always masculine. For instance, one never says "she used to peep through the chink in the wall", or "that fairy took our heart", or "she was looking at her face in the thumb-ring mirror", or "she was doing her hair", or "she is enamoured of her own face", or "she is one who burns the lover's heart"; even in such cases the verbs and adjectives are always masculine, although the context demands the feminine. For instance, Zauq says:

"The chink in the wall through which he used to peep at us: alas for fate, a hornets' nest is in that very chink!"

Or Amanat of Lucknow says:

"That fairy would open his tresses with combs; he would make the hair-splitters prisoners of calamity."

In short, no Urdu *ghazal*-writer, so far as we know, has used a feminine verb or adjective for the beloved. [TN: the printed text says "masculine" here (*muzakkar*), which contradicts the argument; "feminine" is clearly meant.]

If the beloved is left unspecified and no particular of men or women is mentioned in the *ghazal*, then using masculine verbs and adjectives will be wholly in keeping with the rule. In all the languages of the world this rule seems general: when a statement is made about human beings in general and no distinction of man or woman is intended, then, although mankind includes both male and female, the subject of that statement is always taken to be the complete individual, that is, the masculine, and not the feminine. In religion, philosophy, medicine, ethics and all the sciences and arts this rule generally holds. But to mention the beloved now with turban-band, gown or green down, and now with braid, hair-ribbon, thumb-ring mirror and bangles, and yet always to use masculine verbs and adjectives for her, will mean that the beloved is neither man nor woman but effeminate, or a eunuch.

Verses in which love is expressed in words that cover the general sense of affection, or that can be taken as purely spiritual or divine love, and from which it does not appear at all whether the one sought is man or woman, are found in abundance in the *ghazal* of both languages, Persian and Urdu alike; especially in the verse of the Sufi poets most verses are of this kind. So to wish that such a restriction be kept in the *ghazal* for good is not something to be thought an impossible burden.

(2) Just as love subjects belong to the nature of the *ghazal*, so *khamriyat*, that is, mention of wine and its accompaniments, as well as taunts and jibes at the jurists, the ascetics and all the people of outward observance, pride in one's own wine-drinking, breaking of vows of repentance and sitting in taverns, finding fault with the deeds and words of the people of the law and of piety, and other such things contrary to reason and religious law, have also become inseparable parts of the *ghazal*. This way was first adopted in the *ghazal* by the Sufi poets, who are held to be people of God and of inward vision, such as Sa'di, Rumi, Hafiz, Khusrau and others. Since their *ghazal* gained more currency and acceptance in Iran and India, and above all the *ghazal* of Khwaja Hafiz, in which these subjects abound more than anywhere, became famous and accepted beyond measure, the later poets too took up the same manner in imitation of them. But we must see what the purpose was behind these worthies' going to such lengths in composing such subjects.

The jurists and the people of outward observance have always been bitter opponents of two groups: the people of inward vision, and the people of independent opinion. Both these groups have always suffered severe harm from the *fatwas* of the jurists; they have been killed, hanged on the gallows, bound hand and foot, flogged, imprisoned, exiled, had their books burned, and who knows what else. Since the jurists' hostility to these people was such, they in turn vented the vapours of their hearts freely in their writings, in prose or verse; as someone has said, "One man's hand moves, another's tongue." The jurists and preachers took them to task for their words and deeds; they began to expose the jurists' morals. The jurists said that these people do things contrary to the law; they said that wine-drinking and gambling, which are the greatest of great sins, are better than selling barley while showing wheat. The jurists said that these people say things contrary to the law; they said that to speak unbelief openly is better than to have unbelief in the heart and Islam on the tongue. The jurists enjoined the good and forbade the evil; they said that there is no greater sin than guiding others and staying astray oneself. The jurists said, "You do not fulfil the rights of God"; they said, "You betray the rights of His servants." In short, the cavils that the Sufi poets have made against the people of outward observance are taunts and retorts of this kind.

Besides this, in the *ghazal* of these people wine, cupbearer, cup and flask and their accompaniments, and words contrary to religious law, have mostly been used figuratively and as metaphors. These men described spiritual states in the dress of wine and the beloved, either so that the Friend's secret should not be revealed to strangers; or with a view to keeping themselves safe from people's good opinion, which is a highway robber on the mystic path; or because the pent-up feelings of love and affection come out far better in free and libertine talk than in sober and respectful talk; or for the purpose of teasing their opponents and inflaming them further, and of relishing their rebuke and reproach, which to the innocent accused is sweeter than praise and applause. Proof of the last of these comes from this quatrain of Maulana Rumi:

Yesterday at the head of the lane I plundered the leavings, and turned the pure ones towards your friend;
in thanks for this, I broke the fast in Ramazan, and at the Id said my prayers without ablution.

Shah Waliullah has written a commentary on this quatrain. He says that breaking the fast in Ramazan means that when striving had led to vision, the discipline was given up; and prayer without ablution means that when the Id of union was attained and the pain of separation was gone, now a presence beyond description, which is the reality of prayer, remained at all times, so that in outward purity and impurity, waking and sleeping, in short in every state, the treasure of presence was there. This verse of Khwaja Hafiz too is of the same kind:

Our Master said: "No error passed over the Pen of Creation." Bravo for his pure, error-covering sight!

In the second hemistich the word "error-covering" brings to mind the idea of covering the errors of the Pen of Creation, but in truth this is not the meaning; what is intended is covering the faults of man.

For the meaning of there never being error in the Pen of Creation is that whatever it has written is unalterable; and from this it follows that man is under compulsion, and therefore without fault.

From the verses above it is plain that these worthies used such words on purpose, so that the people of outward observance might find occasion for cavil. That is why Maulana Rumi says:

"It is sweeter that the secret of the beloved be told in the tales of others."

Besides these worthies, there have also been some poets who were really in the habit of drinking wine, and who described in verse the feeling that passed through their hearts in the state of intoxication or hangover, or the effect it had on their nature or morals. Since the greatest element of poetry (as has been said above) is that the thought composed in it should be founded on reality, according to the principles of poetry the right to compose subjects of wine and roast meat should belong only to those who are either themselves men of that field, or can express their real thoughts in the dress of wine-poetry by way of figure and metaphor; otherwise they will be thought imitators of the ancients in the way the monkey imitates man. Again, trampling on and finding fault with the preacher, the ascetic and the rest befits only those who really have some ground of opposition to them. But even where there is no opposition of any kind, such subjects can properly be composed in one way only: that the fault-finding be done in such a manner as shows that the purpose is merely to state the evil of hypocrisy, deceit and sanctimony, and not to attack the persons of ascetics and preachers; for the evil of vices and the goodness of virtues cannot be impressed on the mind without supposing some person or group to be their subject, and showing abstract things in the dress of sensible ones. Tyranny and justice can be clearly stated only by blaming or praising a tyrannical or a just king; and cowardice or bravery can be pictured only by casting them in the mould of some coward or hero. But in that case it is necessary to point in some way to some quality of the preacher, ascetic and the rest that is blameworthy by reason or by religious law; otherwise it will be said that the good are attacked not because they are blameworthy but because they are good. Here, as an example, we write two verses of Shaikh Ibrahim Zauq:

Ascetic, do not tease the ruined libertine; what is another's business to you? Settle your own.

In this verse there is some pointing to the trait found in most ascetics and devotees, that they reproach others for the slightest fault, and, proud of keeping the outward commands, neglect the reform of their inner selves. So no objection can be made to this manner of expression. But elsewhere he says:

It would suit the Shaikh's white beard to be dyed with indigo from hemp-water and with henna from rose-coloured wine.

In this verse no sin or fault of the Shaikh is pointed out except that the Shaikh is a Shaikh; and no merit is put into the verse except that two jibes are flung at a holy man to regale the hemp-smokers and the drunkards. Such verses are found in abundance in the verse of our poets, and even if we show our poets more respect than is due, we cannot give such verses more weight than the ribaldry of Sa'di and Suzani.

(3) Apart from the subjects mentioned above, whatever thing raises true passion and ardour in the heart, whether its source be joy or grief, regret or remorse, gratitude or complaint, patience or contentment with God's will, contentment with little or trust in God, desire or aversion, pity or justice, anger or wonder, hope or hopelessness, longing or waiting, love of the homeland, sympathy for the nation, turning to God, defence of faith and religion, the instability of the world and the thought of death, or any other of the human feelings, that too can be stated in the *ghazal*.

Although in its original purpose the theme of the *ghazal* is nothing other than love and affection, our poets have made it general for every subject, and now this form is called *ghazal* only figuratively. So every kind of thought that arises in the poet's heart from time to time can be stated in the *ghazal*, the *ruba'i* or the *qit'a*. But it is not right that we should keep singing the same tune and keep repeating the very thoughts that earlier poets expressed in response to the demand of their age or in the passion of their feelings. No; we should make our *ghazal* the organ of our own thoughts and our own feelings. It may be that someone among the earlier poets called struggling and striving for the world vain and useless, but our heart holds this thought in contempt; or they, on the contrary, may have thought it cowardly and shameless to sit down with broken legs, while on someone among us the opposite state has come. In either case the cry that comes from our mouth should be the one that has risen from our heart. It is also possible that at one time we ourselves may find, for instance, effort and planning merely useless and fruitless, and at another time such passion may arise in that same heart of ours that we resolve to move a mountain from its place. We should draw a picture of both states, each on its own occasion, without addition or omission. Not only will this reveal the subtleties and mysteries of human nature and the revolutions that arise in it from moment to moment; it will also have an excellent effect on the nation's morals; for until both the good and the bad side of everything are shown, the excellence of moderation does not shine forth. For instance, Sa'ib says in one place:

Be content with dry bread, lest you lose your honour; for the many-coloured desires belong to the many-coloured delicacies.

Elsewhere the same Sa'ib says:

Do not spend your days in idleness; do not make trust in God a veil over your own work.

It is plain that until both these different thoughts are kept in view, that degree of contentment which lies midway between ease and greed cannot be attained.

Perhaps someone may think that ethical subjects cannot bring into the *ghazal* the warmth that love subjects have; the effect there is in describing longing and desire, the pain of separation, the wasting of waiting and the jealousy of rivals can never be found in preacherly counsel and advice. Certainly it is very hard work to state ethical subjects in a moving form, and without doubt a *ghazal* that has no burning and melting in it, and nothing but restlessness and liveliness, has no attraction or grip in either case; but for our contemporaries there is so much material for burning and melting that it cannot be used up for centuries. A great revolution is taking place in the world and goes on taking place. The state of the world today plainly looks like that of a tree on which new shoots are constantly sprouting and old branches are constantly falling; the mighty trees are sucking up all the strength of the earth, and all the small plants around them are withering; old nations keep vacating their places and new nations keep taking their place. And this is no flood of the Ganges and Jumna that will drown the villages round about and stop there; it is the flood of the ocean, by which water seems to be passing over the whole globe. If one sees and understands, hundreds of spectacles appear from morning to evening so full of warning that a poet's whole life would not suffice to describe their particulars. At the sight of one event one wonders, "What is this that has happened!"; at the sight of another one grieves, "Why did this happen?" Sometimes fear is felt of what will be, and sometimes despair overcomes the heart: "That is all; now there is nothing." What more interesting material could there be for the *ghazal*? Everything has its place and every work its time. The fancies of love and lovers suited the days of prosperity; that time has gone; the night of pleasure and revelry has passed and morning has appeared; now is not the time for *Kalingra* and *Bihag*, now is the time for the strain of *Jogiya*. [TN: *Kalingra* and *Bihag* are *ragas* of the night; *Jogiya* is a sombre *raga* of early morning associated with renunciation.]

Besides, great masters have often written continuous *ghazals*, in which the subject of one verse is not separate from the next, but the subject of the whole *ghazal* from first to last is one. If anyone wishes to write such *ghazals*, somewhat longer subjects too can be composed in them: for instance, the character of each season, the scene of morning and evening, the delight of a moonlit night, the spring of forest or garden, the bustle of fairs and shows, the silence of the graveyard, the account of a journey, attachment to the homeland, and many other things of this kind can be stated very well in continuous *ghazals*.

In short, the *ghazal* should be widened as far as possible in subjects and thoughts. People do not need verse as they need food in hunger. If a man cannot always have dishes of every kind, he can be content with a single dish all his life; but in verse or music, unless there is variety and diversity, the heart wearies of it. The singer who sings nothing but *Bhairavi* morning and evening, night and day, makes his singing unbearable. In the same way, hearing the same kind of subjects in verse again and again brings disgust with verse.

"A thing repeated, even though it be mixed with magic, brings weariness to the nature."

There is no doubt, of course, that just as producing novelty in verse and always trying one's nature on new and untouched subjects is the poet's mastery, so stating one subject in different forms and many styles also belongs to poetic mastery; but when the same subject is always shown in a new form, no freshness is left in it. Every subject has a few limited aspects; when all those aspects have been done, there is no room left for variety in that subject. If one still goes on harping on it, then instead of variety there will be repetition and reiteration. A *bahrupiya* (quick-change mimic) can deceive people by taking on two or four disguises, but then he is exposed; everyone recognises him from afar as a *bahrupiya*. When we write a *ghazal* and go to the *mushaira*, we think in our hearts that we are taking along subjects distinct from everyone else's and untouched; but look at the *ghazal* and it is like English confectionery: the sweets have different shapes, but they all taste the same. Suppose several moulds of different shapes are ready: one round, one oblong, one triangular, one square, one hexagonal and one octagonal. Now melt wax and pour it into each mould; plainly the wax will come out of each mould cast in a new shape. Exactly so it is with the *ghazal*: the subjects are the same ordinary ones, but by differences of metre, *radif* and *qafiya* different shapes are produced.

The *diwan* of *ghazals* of a famous poet is before us now. In it the subject of the torn collar is composed in the following forms:

(1) O Madness, we have already torn the collar; what shall we do now? Show us some other occupation.

(2) People have started tearing their clothes again, and our hand has started going to the collar again.

(3) The days of spring have come near, when the collar tears of itself.

(4) If my clothes had not been snatched away in spring, neither hem nor collar would be seen on my body.

(5) If there were no restraint of reason, we would tear hem and collar and all.

(6) She pulled her hand free and went away; now I too will not stop till I have torn my collar.

(7) O Madness, we tear our collar in separation; you go on counting its threads all night.

(8) Her writing made me so mad that I tore my shirt to pieces.

(9) Seeing the hem of her close-fitting gown, collars tear.

(10) O Madness, make rags of the collar as you did of the hem.

(11) Let us see how long we go on tearing clothes, and how long Madness keeps us naked like a needle.

(12) O Madness, do not tear clothes now; how long shall we tear the hem and use it to darn the collar?

(13) How idle the hands are in spring! Come, let us just tear the collar.

(14) O Madness, the collar chokes me more than a noose; tear it to shreds.

(15) O Madness, this year in spring tear the collar so that no one can darn it.

(16) You pulled your hem from my hand and went off; I tore my collar and went off.

(17) When madness passed all bounds, the collars tore and ran clean out of the hems.

(18) I envy the torn collars: how they run off towards the skirts of the desert!

(19) Thanks to madness our hands are in full vigour, for new collars are torn all the time.

(20) O Madness, how harassed I am by your hands! Where am I to get new collars every day?

(21) Her lovers always keep their collars torn; is there any darning in the collar of the rose?

(22) Spring came and madness began tearing clothes again; how many collars flew off in rags!

(23) O Madness, I adjure you by your passion for her tresses: let not a single thread of the collar go to waste.

The *diwan* from which we have copied these twenty-three styles of expressing one subject is a *diwan* of a little over two hundred pages. When such is the state of one short *diwan*, consider in how many shapes this one subject must have been composed in all the *diwans* of Urdu; and if the *diwans* of Persian are added to them, I think several bulky volumes could be filled with the verses on this one subject alone. Yet the subject is so narrow that it seems to have room for no more than one or two styles. From this one may judge how far things must have gone with the ordinary subjects of the *ghazal* in which more aspects can be found than in this one, such as the cruelty of the beloved, jealousy of rivals, longing for union, the pain of separation, dishevelled tresses, the bewitching eye, idol-worship, the breaking of vows of repentance, libertinism and wine-drinking and so on. It does not seem any exaggeration that if all the *ghazals* of Persian and Urdu were summarised, and only the original subjects picked out, leaving aside repetitions, the whole quantity of subjects would not come to more than a hundred or a hundred and twenty-five pages; and if one made it a rule to select all the fine aspects from which each subject has been composed, the quantity would no doubt grow somewhat, but most of the fine aspects would be found in the verse of the ancients and their leavings in the verse of the later poets. This same subject of the torn collar, which one of the later poets has composed in twenty-three ways, is composed by Mir Taqi thus:

"This time, in madness, perhaps no distance will remain between the rent in the hem and the rent in the collar."

I have no hope at all that any of the later poets has composed the subject of the torn collar better than this.

By the argument above we do not mean that the later poets should take nothing from the verse of the ancients, and should not now compose from any aspect a subject the ancients composed, or should never again repeat the subjects they composed; for without this, not only in poetry but in every art and every craft, work cannot go on at all. Ka'b ibn Zuhair, a poet who lived both before and after Islam and a eulogist of the Prophet (God bless him and his family and give them peace), says:

"I do not see us say anything but what is borrowed, or what is said again, repeated, from our own speech."

(That is, whatever we say we either borrow from the speech of others or repeat our own speech again and again.) So when poets thought so thirteen and a half centuries ago, how can we say that we have no need to glean from the ancients, or that we have the power to compose a subject once and never repeat it?

Two contradictory proverbs are well known in Arabic. One is "How much the first left for the last!" (that is, the earlier ones left much for the later ones), and the other is "The first left nothing for the last" (that is, the earlier ones left nothing for the later ones). These two proverbs can be reconciled thus: the earlier ones left many unfinished things so that the later ones might complete them, but they left the later ones nothing of which no model exists.

The whole nation agrees that a later poet who takes a subject from the verse of an earlier poet and makes in it some subtle addition or change that increases its beauty, its weight or its clarity in truth snatches that subject from the earlier poet. For instance, Sa'di of Shiraz says:

"He knows nothing of our whirlpool, who rests at ease on the shore of the sea."

Khwaja Hafiz has expressed the same subject thus:

"A dark night, the fear of the waves, and a whirlpool so terrible: how can those who walk lightly on the shores know our state?"

It is plain that in this subject Hafiz has, as it were, made up the shortfall left in the Shaikh's statement; so it can be said that Hafiz snatched this subject from the Shaikh. Naziri has expressed the same meaning thus:

"Under the rose-branch a viper has bitten the nightingale; what do singers who were never bitten know of the wound?"

Although Naziri has made no addition to the original subject on account of which it could be said that he snatched it from Khwaja Hafiz, he has expressed the subject in so novel a style that it seems a wholly new subject.

One day this same verse of Khwaja Hafiz was recited on some occasion. A gentleman who had a true taste for verse, hearing it, said: "If only the second hemistich too had described difficulties and hardships of the same kind as the first, and nothing had been said about how the unfeeling know nothing of our state, it would have been a stronger proof of being caught up in one's own state and oblivious of the thought of others." [TN: the sentence is given as printed; its wording is somewhat tangled in the original.] I recited this verse of the late Ghalib:

"The wind against us, the night pitch-dark, the sea raising storms; the anchor of the boat broken, and the boatman asleep."

Hearing this verse he was thrilled and said, "Yes, that is just what I meant." From these examples it is quite plain that sometimes some shortfall is left in the verse of the ancients which the later poets make up; sometimes the ancients take a subject to be confined to a particular style, and the later poets find an original style for it; and sometimes the later poets take away one excellence from the ancients' style and add another; and through this poetry progresses without limit. So how can a poet, relying on his own limited thought and imagination, give up gleaning from the ancients?

Shifa'i of Isfahan, or some other of the later poets of Iran, says in a *ghazal*:

"Tell the tire-woman to add something more to the Friend's apparatus of beauty, for the turn to gaze has come to us."

The speaker's meaning seems to be that the beloved's ordinary adornment is not enough to please us; so the tire-woman should add something more to it, because now the turn to see her has come to us. The poet did bring novelty into the subject, but a limp one. First, it seems that the one he has called "Friend" has not yet stamped the image of his love on his heart; then how can it be right to call her "Friend"? Second, his statement shows that he has no attachment to the beloved's own beauty but is infatuated with passing adornment. Third, he wishes to produce by intention and will a love that always arises without intention or will.

Mirza Ghalib says in praise:

In his reign the age is absorbed in adornment; now more stars will be made for the sky.

Apparently this thought arose, intentionally or not, from that very Persian verse, but the Mirza has wholly snatched the subject from the one who first composed it. The flaws it had in the setting of love poetry have entirely disappeared in the setting of praise. The Mirza has credited the patron with a perfection that is the root of all perfections, namely that he wishes to see everything in a more perfect and more excellent state; so everything wishes to show itself to him in a more perfect state; and from this he has drawn the conclusion that if this is so, perhaps more stars will be created to adorn the sky. Apart from some logical objection, no other kind of fault can be found with this; unlike the Persian verse, whose very foundation is contrary to the principles of poetry and the manners of love and affection.

Urfi of Shiraz says:

"Not everyone knows the secret; otherwise, all these things that are known to the common people are the secret."

The late Ghalib has displayed the same subject in another dress thus:

"It is only you who are not privy to the songs of the secret; otherwise, whatever is a veil here is the fret of an instrument." [TN: *parda* means both "veil" and the "fret" of a stringed instrument.]

Although the most likely guess is that Urfi was led to this thought by this verse of the Holy Quran: "And there is nothing that does not glorify Him with His praise, but you do not understand their glorification" [TN: Quran 17:44]; in any case this verse of Urfi's deserves to be written in letters of gold, and it is hard now to find a better style than the one in which he has expressed this thought. Even so, the Mirza's novelty and search deserve no less praise, for in a subject that had no room at all for addition he has made so subtle an addition that, along with the charm of its words, it is not without subtlety of meaning either. Urfi's meaning is that the things known to the common people are in truth the secrets; the Mirza says that the things that seem to prevent the revealing of the secret are in truth what reveal it.

In any case the later poets have always made borrowings of this kind from the verse of the ancients, and lamp has gone on lighting lamp. When the poets of Arabia composed some untouched subject and people, amazed, asked them by what occasion their minds had reached so far, they would plainly state the source of their thought. Abu Nuwas composed this verse in praise of al-Fadl ibn al-Rabi':

"It is not beyond God to gather the whole world in one man."

(That is, it is not far from God to gather the whole world in the person of one man.) At this someone asked him how this subject occurred to him. Abu Nuwas said plainly that the thought arose from this verse of Jarir which he composed in praise of the Banu Tamim:

"When the Banu Tamim are angry with you, you would think all people angry."

(That is, when the Banu Tamim are displeased with you, you should understand that all mankind is displeased with you.)

It is not only verse; in all the sciences and arts man has progressed in the same way: the earlier ones kept leaving unfinished models, and the later ones kept working on them little by little, until every science and every art reached perfection. The progress of poetry too can be conceived only thus, that reasonable changes go on being made, little by little, in the thoughts of the ancients. But to make changes of this kind the poet must have full ability; otherwise, with inventions of the kind most of the poets of our country make, poetry grows instead more base, low and contemptible day by day.

In Persian less, in Arabic more, and in English far more, and not only in verse but in prose even more than in verse, there is a store of lofty, subtle and pure thoughts of every kind. So those of our countrymen who have minds able to take new thoughts from foreign languages and work excellent changes on them should, by making changes according to the reach of their thought, and those whose imaginative faculty is of a lower degree should, by translating those same thoughts exactly into their own language with clarity and simplicity, enrich Urdu poetry. In Sanskrit and *bhasha* there is another world of thought, and the Urdu language has more affinity with the thoughts of Sanskrit and *bhasha* than other languages have; so let them not fall short in taking thoughts from these languages too, and let them show them in the dress of verse as far as their power of expressing them in their own language allows. And in this way let them breathe the spirit of progress into Urdu poetry.

We see that where our poets have here and there translated Persian verses into Urdu verses, people have objected to it; but in our view this is no ground for objection. To make a fine translation of the verse of one language into the verse of another is no easy thing. A certain gentleman has translated the whole *Sikandarnama-e-Bahri* into Urdu, and we have heard that he was both a poet and a *maulvi* (religious scholar). Consider a few of his verses: [TN: the following lines are a deliberately clumsy specimen, Persian half-rendered into Urdu; the English gives their sense.]

When fruit adorns the branch, so that it makes one forget the earth that gives,
when the pleasant garden was adorned with every fruit, sweet and sour,
the pistachio's lip laughed with joy; the fresh date too grew sharp of tooth at it;
the pomegranate's face was lit up, like rubies sewn on a crown;
on every fig-bearing branch, with relish, the fig-eating birds began to hang;
the jar's lip raised a clamour of cries, both from the smell of the must and the smell of milk.

Perhaps it may be said of this translator that he was not a practised poet and so could not make a fine translation; but we say to practised poets: kindly write, if not more, at least these six verses in eloquent Urdu verse. The man who translates the verse of another language well into the verse of his own, though this does not prove the perfection of his imaginative faculty, gives proof of another ability that not every poet can have.

Some of our poets have expressed certain thoughts that were in Persian verses so finely in Urdu that in a way they have surpassed the original verse. Naziri's verse is:

"The scent of my beloved comes from this one, weak in faith; take my rose from my hand, for I am undone."

Sauda says:

"Sauda, I remember the intoxication of her eye; take the cup from my hand, for I am going."

There is no doubt that Sauda has founded his verse on Naziri's subject; indeed it can be said that he has translated it with a slight change; but in eloquence Sauda's verse has far surpassed Naziri's. It is possible that the lover may lose himself even on remembering the friend, but to lose oneself at the sight of the wine-cup from the thought of the beloved's intoxicating eye is more probable. Besides, *az kar shudam* ("I am undone") has not the generality of *chala main* ("I am going"); one does not know whether he is going out of himself, or out of faith and the world, or out of his place, or out of what. And the greatest point is that *chala main* is always said on the occasion when a man, senseless and dazed, is about to fall; and *az kar shudam* has not this sense; being dismissed, being removed from office, being crippled and useless are also expressed by *az kar shudan*.

Author unknown:

Give no one like me a way into your gathering; a dejected heart makes a whole company dejected.

Khwaja Mir Dard:

Lest your pleasure too be spoiled, friends, do not remember Dard in the gathering.

It is possible that Khwaja Mir Dard took this subject from the Persian verse, but certainly his verse has far surpassed the Persian one. First, he brings the subject of the Persian opening couplet into his own closing couplet, where the very word *dard* ("pain") serves as proof of the poet's claim. Then he says *yad karo* ("remember") in place of *rah madeh* ("give no way"), which has two meanings: one, do not mention Dard in your gathering; the other, in the sense of "summoning", a superior calling an inferior to his presence. And the great beauty of Dard's verse is that the reason for not calling him to the gathering, which is stated as a certainty in the Persian, Mir Dard has stated as a possibility thus: "lest your pleasure too be spoiled". The difference between these two styles is like that between one man saying to a sick person, "A man dies from not keeping to his diet," and another saying, "Take care you do not lose your life by not keeping to your diet." In the second style, as is plain, there is more warning and caution than in the first.

Sa'di of Shiraz:

Friends forbid me: why have I given you my heart? They should first ask you: why are you so beautiful?

Mir Taqi:

The beauties who charge us with the sin of loving them, ask them too: why did you become so lovable?

This verse of Mir's seems on the face of it to be taken from Sa'di's verse, but Sa'di has the word *khub* ("beautiful") and Mir the word *pyare* ("lovable"). Plainly it is not necessary that a beautiful one be loved, but it is necessary that a lovable one be loved; so Sa'di's question can be answered, but Mir's question cannot.

In any case, translating, provided the duties of translation are fully discharged, can be no fault. Sa'di, who is the Homer of Persian poetry, has in his own verse translations of Arabic sayings and proverbs, or their substance; for instance:

## Sa'di and the Arabic Sayings

(1) Sa'di: Wash a dog in the seven seas; when it is wet it is all the fouler.
Arabic: The dog is at its most unclean when it has bathed.

(2) Sa'di: For you, O man of sense, silence is dignity, and for the unworthy a veil.
Arabic: Silence is the adornment of the learned and the covering of the ignorant.

(3) Sa'di: What good did you do in place of a father, that you expect the same from your son?
Arabic: Leave your father alone, and your son will leave you alone. [TN: the Arabic, *da' abaka yara' ibnaka*, is given as printed; its sense is "honour your father and your son will honour you".]

(4) Sa'di: If the bat does not want the light of the sun, the splendour of the sun's market is not lessened.
Arabic: The brightness of the sun is not diminished by the bat's prayer.

(5) Sa'di: Fortunate is he who ate and sowed, and unfortunate he who died and left behind.
Arabic: The fortunate is he who ate and sowed, and the wretched he who died and bade farewell.

(6) Sa'di: Kings need the wise more than / the wise need kings.
Arabic: The ruler needs the wise more than the wise need the ruler.

The reason the people of Europe today surpass the whole world in literature, as in the sciences, arts and crafts, is none other than this: there is no famous nation in the world the essence of whose poetry and prose does not exist in their languages. So we too should profit as far as possible from the thoughts of whatever nation and whatever language come within our reach, and not sit content with only those few worn-out and rotten thoughts that have been composed again and again for centuries; for contentment in learning and skill is as blameworthy as greed in wealth and riches.

(4) Just as the subjects of our *ghazal* are limited, so its language too cannot go beyond a particular circle; for when a few ordinary subjects are used continually for centuries, a particular part of the language becomes peculiar to them, which, from coming again and again to the tongue and being heard again and again by the ear, becomes more familiar and agreeable, so that if other words of the same meaning are used in place of these words, they seem strange and foreign. With us, love subjects are not peculiar to the *ghazal* alone; they have held sway in the *qasida* and the *masnavi* as well. In the Persian and Urdu languages all *masnavis* but a few have been written on love subjects. In the same way, in the preludes of *qasidas* too it is mostly this same lament that has been wailed. The *wasokht* (poem of the lover's estrangement) was born from the very rib of love. But since the field of the *qasida*, the *masnavi* and the *wasokht* is wide, much strange and foreign vocabulary can be absorbed in them; unlike the *ghazal*, where even one unfamiliar word seems a lump. In a bed of roses the thorns too get along with the flowers, but in a bouquet even one thorn pricks. That is why the worthies who founded the *ghazal* on Sufism and ethics also had to adopt the language generally used in the *ghazal*. The very words that were applied in their literal senses in love subjects, these worthies used as figure and metaphor, and expressed their lofty thoughts in symbol, allusion and allegory. So in the *ghazal* it is necessary to pay more attention to simplicity and clarity than in other forms. The people whose *ghazal* has been accepted to this day, in Persian or Urdu, are those who kept this principle as their goal. In Urdu, from Wali down to Insha and Mushafi, clarity, simplicity, keeping to everyday idiom, smoothness of expression and suppleness of language are generally found in everyone's *ghazals*. After them in Delhi, in Mamnun, Ghalib, Momin, Shefta and others, Persian constructions undoubtedly found more entry into the Urdu *ghazal*; but these men too held that verse to be of the highest rank in which a pure and lofty thought was expressed in the idiom of pure Urdu. Their view was that "in a *ghazal* no more than one or two verses of the highest rank can come out; the rest is padding. The earlier poets did not care at all about incongruity; one or two good verses came out, and they filled out the quota of the *ghazal* with light and limp verses. What we do is tighten our padding verses with Persian constructions so that at first sight they do not seem contemptible." The point is that these men very seldom composed the same ordinary thoughts that had long been composed in various shapes; they wished to bring novelty into every verse; so the thread of everyday Urdu often slipped from their hands. Even so, the character of the *ghazal* is found in all their verse, and clear, idiomatic and lofty verses can be found in them in about the same proportion as in the *ghazals* of the ancients.

In Zauq's *ghazal* the relish of the language is generally greater than in the verse of his contemporaries, but he too, where he creates new subjects, falls very far from clarity. Zafar's whole *diwan* is even from first to last in purity of language and excellence of idiom, but freshness of thought is found in it very little. In Dagh's *ghazal*, along with purity of language, keeping to everyday speech and abundance of idiom, there is a playfulness and sharpness in the manner of expression that is his alone. But it is most surprising that in Lucknow the later poets have paid very little attention to simplicity and clarity in the *ghazal*, although in language no appreciable difference seems to exist between Delhi and Lucknow. Besides, from the time of Shuja-ud-Daula to that of Sa'adat Ali Khan all the famous poets of Urdu crowded into Lucknow, so that Mir, Sauda, Soz, Jur'at, Mushafi, Insha and others stayed there to their last breath and died there; yet very little influence of their manner of expression is found in the *ghazal* of the later poets. Apparently, when Delhi had been ruined and the age turned favourable to Lucknow, and most of the gentle families of Delhi and, but for one or two, all the famous poets went to live in Lucknow, and along with wealth and riches the old sciences too made progress up to a point, the people of Lucknow must naturally have come to think that just as they were superior in wealth and in logic, philosophy and the like, so they were superior to Delhi in language and accent too; but to prove superiority in language it was necessary to create some distinguishing feature between their language and that of Delhi. Since there was much practice in logic, philosophy, medicine, scholastic theology and the like, natures of themselves demanded that Hindi words be gradually dropped from speech and Arabic words enter abundantly in their place, until plain and simple Urdu was not merely abandoned in the society of the nobles and the learned but, as has been heard from trustworthy people, came to be thought vulgar, the talk of the bazaar; and this same colour gradually came to dominate verse and prose as well. Comparing the *diwans* of Jur'at and Nasikh in verse, and *Bagh-o-Bahar* and *Fasana-e-Aja'ib* in prose, gives sufficient proof of this. Even so, in fairness, in the *marsiya* and the *masnavi* the demand of the age had no effect on certain particular persons (as will be described further on); they did not let go of the real essence of the language, but, regarding it as a sacred relic of their elders, kept it safe with great care in this time of revolution.

In any case, for the sake of clarity of language and expression in the *ghazal*, a few things must be kept in view:

(1) We have written above that it is not right to confine the *ghazal* to love poetry alone, and love poetry to subjects of lust and desire alone; it should be made the organ of every kind of feeling. It is also plain that by the constant composing of ordinary subjects in the *ghazal* a particular language has become fixed for it, and it has so soaked into the ears that if unfamiliar and foreign constructions and styles of expression were suddenly to enter it in abundance, the *ghazal* would become as knotty as the *ghazal* of some poets has become through adopting unfamiliar Arabic and Persian words and constructions; and yet widening the *ghazal* in subject apparently demands that the language and manner of expression be widened too. So it is necessary to adopt some method by which no great change comes suddenly into the manner of expression, and yet every kind of thought can be expressed finely in the *ghazal*. Nowadays it is seen that many new thoughts, which our earlier poets never composed, are shown in the dress of verse; but since they are not expressed in the particular language that has soaked into the ears through the poets' constant use, but are expressed in whatever words the new thoughts directly call for, they are not accepted by select and common alike. But if general poetry of the new style is not accepted for the present, no harm is done; when people's tastes gradually become acquainted with it and learn the relish and sweetness of true things, it will be accepted of itself. It is necessary, however, to make the *ghazal* from the very start as popular and pleasing to people's natures as possible, for this is the form that is current on the tongues of select and common; its verses alone can easily be learned by heart by everyone; and it alone is sung and recited at all gatherings of joy, assemblies of *sama'* and companies of friends. So there is no better way of spreading natural poetry in the country than to state every kind of subtle and pure thought in the *ghazal*, to make it the instrument for expressing all human feelings, and yet to show it in a dress that does not at first sight seem foreign and unfamiliar.

The greatest proof that new thoughts, even the very highest, should at first be expressed in the same language and the same idiom in which the old and low thoughts used to be expressed is this: in the Word of God all spiritual and moral matters have been stated in the very idioms, comparisons, metaphors and similes in which the poets of the Age of Ignorance stated subjects of love and wine, boasting, praise, blame and the like.

It is possible for a sudden and marked progress and widening to take place in a nation's thoughts, but the language cannot widen suddenly; the styles of expression are added slowly, unnoticed, and are gradually made familiar to the ears of the public, while the old styles that have soaked into the ears are kept intact and in place. So much so that even if the progress of knowledge proves many old poetic thoughts to be merely false and baseless, the words through which those thoughts were expressed are not given up. Suppose that the existence of the heavens and their revolving, the earth's being at rest, water and air being simple elements, the elements being limited to four, Jamshed's cup showing the world, the fountain of life lying hidden in the darkness, the existence of the Simurgh and of demons and fairies, and many other things of this kind were proved false by the progress of human knowledge; even then it is not the poet's business to give up these thoughts altogether; his mastery is rather to state facts and events and true and natural thoughts in the dress of these false and baseless things, and never to let the spell that the ancients cast be broken; otherwise he will very soon see that he has forgotten from his charm the very syllables that used to enchant hearts.

In any case, those who wish to develop Urdu poetry, or let us say to keep it alive on the page of time, have the duty to keep this principle in view in the forms of verse generally and in the *ghazal* especially: that in the course of composition new styles be adopted as little as possible and unfamiliar words be used little, but that they keep increasing them gradually and unnoticed; and that they found their verse mostly on the old styles and on ordinary words and idioms, yet not be content with the literal meanings of words, but use them sometimes in their literal senses, sometimes in figurative senses, sometimes by way of metaphor and allusion, and sometimes in the form of allegory; otherwise how can every kind of thought be expressed in so measured a language? At this point we do not wish to state the principles of the science of rhetoric, from which one learns to express one meaning in several forms and to use each word on different occasions, because their details can be found in Arabic, Persian and also Urdu treatises; but we quote, as examples, a number of verses of the Persian and Urdu *ghazal* in which subjects of ethics and Sufism have been expressed in the dress of earthly love and love poetry, and a limited and ordinary language has been made to serve for expressing unfamiliar thoughts.
