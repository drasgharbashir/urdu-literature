# The Ghazal, Continued: Model Ghazals, Language and Idiom

## From the Diwan of Khwaja Hafiz

Theme: The whole world longs for and seeks God without having seen Him.
Expression: No one has seen your face, yet you have a thousand rivals; you are still in the bud, yet you have a hundred nightingales.

Theme: The true seekers of God are never left deprived.
Expression: Who ever became a lover whose state the Friend did not look upon? Sir, there is no pain; otherwise there is a physician.

Theme: To shame a friend by blaming him is against the condition of friendship.
Expression: At dawn the bird of the meadow said to the newly risen rose: "Put on fewer airs, for many like you have bloomed in this garden."
The rose laughed: "We are not hurt by the truth; but no lover ever spoke a bitter word to the beloved."

Theme: The time of prosperity does not last for ever.
Expression: I said, "O throne of Jamshed, where is your world-seeing cup?" It said, "Alas, that wakeful fortune has fallen asleep."

Theme: Sin is better than obedience in which hypocrisy is mixed.
Expression: Cupbearer, bring wine, for the month of fasting has gone; pour the cup, for the season of reputation and name has gone.
Precious time has gone; come, let us make up for the life that passed without the presence of flask and cup.

Theme: Although no one can reach God, how have His secrets come to be revealed in the world?
Expression: The breeze told every rose some tale of your face; how did the guardian let the tale-bearer into your sanctuary?

Theme: Having failed in every effort, to strive in seeking God.
Expression: I practise love, and I hope that this noble art will not, like other skills, become a cause of deprivation.

## From the Diwan of Khwaja Mir Dard

Theme: To mix with everyone in the world, yet remain unattached to all.
Expression: Dard, do not give your heart to anyone here; walk along with everyone, but do not let your soul be caught.

Theme: There are great dangers in nearness to God.
Expression: Would that the moth had never passed near the candle; what havoc you have wrought, O wings and feathers of the moth!

Theme: The final goal of the seeker is annihilation.
Expression: In a single leap it reached the goal it sought; travellers, the moth's journey is a thing to envy!

Theme: The inner secret should not be revealed to anyone.
Expression: Every moment he says in my ear: "Let no one learn of this matter [1]."

Theme: There is no room for any intermediary between the servant and God.
Expression: Messenger, this is not your work; go your way. Who but the heart could bring his message?

Theme: All the splendours of creation are manifestations of the divine glory.
Expression: Tell me, breeze, who passed this way today? This scent in the garden is not the scent of your flowers.

Theme: "Every day He is about some new work." [TN: Quran 55:29.]
Expression: My heart too has learned your ways: one thing this moment, another the next.

Theme: In the company of godly people God comes to mind.
Expression: Who is it, rose-bodied one, that dwells in your heart, Dard, that the scent of roses came from your sweat?

Theme: The love of God frees one from all attachments.
Expression: The thought of her tresses has freed us from everything; though we are caught in the snare, the heart is at ease.

Theme: Where the fear of death hangs over us, one should not be heedless of God for a single moment.
Expression: Cupbearer, here everyone is on the point of departure; as long as it can go round, let the cup go round.

Expression: What business had we with this struggle of the snare? O love of the meadow, may your house be ruined!

Expression: It is best indeed not to set one's heart on the world; but what are we to do, when no work goes on without the heart's involvement?

Expression: The roses opened and at least showed the breeze something of their spring; the pity is for those buds that withered without opening.

## Ghalib

Theme: There is no danger in seclusion.
Expression: No arrow is on the bow, no fowler in ambush; in the corner of my cage I have much ease.

Theme: Everyone complains of a sharp-tongued man.
Expression: Granted there should be warmth in speech, but not so much: everyone he spoke to had a complaint.

Theme: All grief and pain come from God.
Expression: We do not fight with the executioner nor quarrel with the preacher; we know Him, in whatever guise He comes.

Expression: Despair, let me steady myself; what a calamity is this, that the hem of the beloved's thought is slipping from my grasp!

## Shefta

Theme: God is in the hut of the poor.
Expression: What is gained by lamp-shades, glass and golden basins? He is there where there is no oil in the lamp.

Theme: In the affiliation of each order of spiritual masters there is a distinct quality.
Expression: Musk is mingled in the ruby-coloured wine; a strange scent comes to our nostrils.

Theme: By whatever means the arrogance of the self can be lessened is best.
Expression: May the arrogance of the rebellious self be lessened somehow; I want an idol in whom there is little love.

Theme: God's being is free of place and direction.
Expression: That startled gazelle whose prey we are is neither in the valley of Tartary nor in the plain of Khotan.

Theme: Turning away at once from amusement and play and gaining complete peace.
Expression: I have escaped a thousand snares with a single movement; let whoever is proud come and make me his prey.

Although particular Persian *diwans* are full of verses of this kind, and in Urdu too more such verses could be found by searching, these styles belong mostly to the subjects of Sufism; these styles alone cannot suffice for expressing every kind of natural thought, unless the poet has the ability to use them well and suitably on every occasion and the gift of creating new styles akin to them. In our view the secret of this is to gain, as far as possible, command of the use of *isti'ara* (metaphor), *kinaya* (allusion) and *tamsil* (allegory), and of the use of idioms.

The definitions and kinds of metaphor, allusion and allegory should be looked up in books on the science of rhetoric; here we wish to say only that metaphor is a principal pillar of eloquence, and that poetry stands to it as the body to the soul. The case of allusion and allegory is much the same as that of metaphor. All these things put life into verse. Where the rhyme of plain language runs short, it is with their help that the poet expresses the feelings of his heart and his subtle thoughts well; and where he sees his charm failing, it is by their power that he captures people's hearts.

Some subjects are in themselves so interesting and attractive that it is enough to state them with mere clarity and simplicity; but there are many thoughts at which ordinary language breaks down and weeps when it tries to express them, and which ordinary styles are unable to make moving. In such places, if help is not taken from metaphor, allusion, allegory and the like, the verse does not remain verse but becomes ordinary conversation. For instance, Dagh says:

He had gone saying, "I'll be back at once"; the messenger, it seems, met his death. Restless heart, when you go there, don't you too stay there dead!

In this verse delay is expressed as "meeting one's death" and "staying there dead". If these two words were not there, and it were stated thus, "the messenger has been very late; heart, don't you be late too", no life would be left in the verse.

Or, for instance, Mirza Ghalib says:

After my murder she repented of her cruelty; alas for the repentance of one so quick to repent!

In the second hemistich "quick to repent" is said ironically, by way of metaphor, in place of "slow to repent", and this has put life into the verse. It is the same kind of metaphor as in the Holy Quran, where "Give them good tidings of a painful punishment" is said in place of "Warn them". [TN: Quran 3:21 and elsewhere.]

In the same way Mir Taqi says:

You say we are one with you; yes, say it: we trust you!

Here too "we trust you" is said ironically in place of "we do not trust you".

Mirza Ghalib says:

Faithfulness, provided it is steadfast, is the root of faith; if the Brahmin dies in the idol-house, bury him in the Ka'ba.

The real point of the second hemistich was that faithfulness is so fine a quality that if a Brahmin keeps faith with the idol-house all his life, he should be treated as it befits to treat a Muslim of the very highest rank. This point is expressed thus: if he dies in the idol-house he should be buried in the Ka'ba. The beauty of this way of putting it is plain.

Elsewhere Mirza Ghalib says:

What desolation, what utter desolation! Seeing the wilderness, I remembered home.

In the second hemistich, by way of allusion, "I remembered home" is said in place of "I felt afraid", because feeling afraid in the wilderness entails remembering home; and since the figure of *iham* (double meaning) has also been kept in view, still more delight has come into the verse, namely that it also yields this meaning: our home is so desolate that seeing the wilderness one is reminded of home.

A Persian verse of Mirza Ghalib's is:

The wind against us, the night pitch-dark, the sea raising storms; the anchor of the boat broken, and the boatman asleep.

In this verse he has stated his own difficulties and hardships by way of allegory. Whatever the state that the poet has described under this heading, if it were stated clearly and directly as it is, it could never fit into two hemistichs, and moreover the fearful form in which the dress of allegory shows it could never be produced.

An Urdu verse of Mirza Ghalib's is:

The snare lay hidden very near the nest; we had not yet learned to fly when we were caught.

In this verse too he has stated, by way of allegory, the fact that as soon as man comes to his senses he is caught in worldly ties; and the beauty of this way of putting it is plain.

In any case it is a necessary duty of the poet to gain command of the use of figure, metaphor, allusion, allegory and the like, so that he can state every dry and insipid subject with lustre and brilliance; but in metaphor and the rest it is necessary to take care that the figurative meaning is not far from understanding, otherwise the verse will become a riddle and a puzzle. For instance, Shah Nasir says:

On the Oxus the wine-drinking night stole the sheet of moonlight; the sun began sending the bowl round in the morning sky.

By "stealing the sheet of moonlight" he means enjoying the moonlight and taking pleasure in it, which is very far from understanding. [TN: *katora daurana*, "sending a bowl round", probably alludes to the bowl-ordeal once used to trace a thief, matching the "theft" of the moonlight.] Those who have not kept the principle above in view in using metaphor and the rest have always had their verse rejected and abandoned, like the *qasidas* of Badr Chachi, in which very far-fetched metaphors are used: in one place he means the sun by "the female gazelle", in another the stars by "Zulaikha's tears", in another the sign of Scorpio by "the highest", in another letters by "violet petals", in another a cup by "dry water", in another the five fingers by "the five rivers", and in the same way here the sky by "the earth" and there the earth by "the sky".

In Urdu poets have used metaphor mostly within idioms, because the foundation of most idioms, if one looks closely, is metaphor. For instance, *ji uchatna* ("the heart glancing off", that is, losing interest): in this the heart is likened to things that strike something hard and glance off, such as a pebble, a stone, a ball and the like. Or, for instance, *ji batna* ("the heart being divided", that is, being distracted): in this the heart is likened to a thing that can be divided and scattered. *Ankh khulna* ("the eye opening"), *dil kumhlana* ("the heart wilting"), *ghussa bharakna* ("anger flaring"), *kam chalna* ("work going on"), and thousands of other idioms like them are founded on metaphor; and these are metaphors in which the poets' handiwork has no part; they have come naturally, without thought or artifice, from the mouths of native speakers from time to time and become part of the language. Allusion too has mostly been used within idioms; but Urdu poets have used allegory very little, though in poetry of the new style it has begun to come somewhat into fashion, and need has compelled people to use it. Since on this occasion idiom has come up in connection with metaphor, it seems fitting to state a few necessary things about idiom.

(b) *Muhawara* in the dictionary means simply talking together, whether the talk accords with the everyday speech of native speakers or not; but as a technical term *muhawara* is the name of the particular everyday speech, usage or manner of expression of native speakers. So *muhawara* must almost always be found in two or more words, for single words are not called everyday speech, usage or manner of expression; unlike *lughat* (vocabulary), which is always applied to single words, or to words that are as good as single [2]. For instance, *panch* ("five") and *sat* ("seven") are two words to which the term *lughat* can be applied separately, but neither of them will be called *muhawara*; when the two are joined and one says *pansat* ("five or seven"), then it will be called *muhawara*. It is also necessary that the construction to which the term *muhawara* is applied should not be formed by analogy, but should be known to be used just so by native speakers. For instance, if by analogy with *pansat*, or *sat ath*, or *ath sat*, one says *chhe ath*, *ath chhe* or *sat nau*, it will not be called *muhawara*, because native speakers never speak so. Or, for instance, by analogy with *bila-nagha* ("without a break"), to say *be-nagha* in its place, *har din* in place of *har roz* ("every day"), *din din* in place of *roz roz*, or *aye roz* in place of *aye din* ("every other day"): none of these will be called *muhawara*, for these words never come so in the speech of native speakers.

Sometimes the term *muhawara* is applied in particular to verbs that, joined with some noun, are used not in their literal but in a figurative sense, such as *utarna*. Its literal meaning is to bring a body down from above, for instance to bring a rider down from a horse, to take a garment down from a peg, to bring a bed down from the roof; but this second meaning of *muhawara* does not apply to any of these, because in all these examples *utarna* is used in its literal sense. But *naqsha utarna* ("to take down a likeness"), *naql utarna* ("to make a copy"), *dil se utarna* ("to fall from favour"), *dil men utarna* ("to sink into the heart"), *hath utarna* ("to dislocate the arm"), *pahuncha utarna* ("to dislocate the wrist"): all these will be called idioms, because in all these examples *utarna* is applied in a figurative sense. Or, for instance, *khana* ("to eat"): its literal meaning is to chew something with the teeth, or without chewing, and swallow it, for instance *roti khana* ("to eat bread"), *dawa khana* ("to take medicine"), *afim khana* ("to take opium") and so on; but none of these will be called *muhawara* in the second sense, because in all these examples *khana* is used in its literal sense. But *gham khana* ("to bear grief"), *qasam khana* ("to swear an oath"), *dhoka khana* ("to be deceived"), *pachharen khana* ("to fall and writhe"), *thokar khana* ("to stumble"): all these will be called idioms.

The meaning of *muhawara* we stated first is general, that is, it includes the second meaning too; but the second meaning is narrower than the first. So a construction called *muhawara* in the second sense can also be called *muhawara* in the first sense; but it does not follow that a construction called *muhawara* in the first sense should also be called *muhawara* in the second sense. [TN: as printed, the two senses are given the other way round in this sentence; the reading here follows the logic of the argument.] For instance, *tin panch karna* ("to make three-five", that is, to quarrel and wrangle) can be called *muhawara* in both senses, because this construction accords with the speech of native speakers, and also the words "three-five" are used in it not in their literal but in a figurative sense. But *roti khana*, *mewa khana* ("to eat fruit"), *pansat*, *das barah* ("ten or twelve") and the like can be counted *muhawara* only in the first sense, not in the second, because all these constructions certainly accord with the speech of native speakers, but no word in them is used in a figurative sense. Hereafter, to distinguish these two meanings, we shall apply the term *roz-marra* (everyday usage) to the first kind and *muhawara* (idiom) to the second.

There is another difference in use between *roz-marra* and *muhawara*. Keeping to everyday usage has been held necessary, as far as possible, in speech and writing, verse and prose, so much so that the less everyday usage is kept to in a piece, the more it will be thought to fall from the level of eloquence. For instance, "From Calcutta to Peshawar there was built a brick *sarai* at seven or eight *kos* and a pillar at one *kos*": this sentence does not accord with everyday usage; it should rather be, "From Calcutta to Peshawar, at every seven or eight *kos*, a brick *sarai*, and at every *kos*, a pillar, had been built." Or, for instance, "Till today I *na mila* the chance of meeting him": here *nahin mila* is needed in place of *na mila*. Or, "She became *dar-gor* at her husband's death": here "she became *zinda dar-gor*" (buried alive) is needed. Or, "When fortune went to sleep, the eyes *ho gain* wakeful": here *huin* is needed in place of *ho gain*. Or, "Before one's very eyes, what is this that *hua*": here *kya ho gaya* is needed. [TN: these examples turn on fine points of Urdu verb usage that English cannot reproduce; the Urdu forms are given in italics.]

In short, whether in verse or prose, keeping to everyday usage is most necessary as far as possible; but idiom is not so. If an idiom is composed well, it no doubt raises a low verse high and a high one higher; but it is not necessary to compose an idiom into every verse; a verse may stand at the highest level of eloquence without an idiom, and some subtle and pure idiom may be placed tastelessly in a low and base verse. A famous poet's verse is:

The whole hem is brimming with pearls of tears; these days our hem is the hem of wealth.

No idiom is composed in this verse, yet the verse deserves praise. Elsewhere the same poet says:

When the fowlers see her letter, the parrots of their hands keep flying away. [TN: *hathon ke tote urna*, "the parrots of the hands flying away", is an idiom for being dumbfounded.]

In this verse there is neither merit nor subject; only an idiom is composed, and that too against everyday usage, namely *ura karte hain* ("keep flying") in place of *ur jate hain* ("fly away"). An idiom in verse should be thought of like some beautiful limb of the human body, and everyday usage like the proportion of the limbs in the human body. Just as human beauty cannot be thought perfect through the beauty of some particular limb without proportion of the limbs, so no merit can come into verse by placing idioms, fitly or unfitly, without keeping to everyday usage.

The excellence of a verse in meaning can be judged by native speakers and non-native speakers alike, but judging its excellences of language is the portion of native speakers alone. Native speakers generally like more the verse in which everyday usage has been observed, and if along with everyday usage there is also the relish of idiom, it gives them still more pleasure; but there is a very great difference between the taste of the common and of the select. The common people nod their heads on hearing any verse of idiom or everyday usage, however hackneyed, base or frivolous its subject, and however clumsily the idiom has been composed. The reason is that when they see the very styles in which they talk to one another drawn taut by metre and balanced by rhyme, and find their ordinary talk cast in the mould of verse, a kind of wonder arises in them, and with wonder, delight. But for the liking and wonder of the select it is not enough merely to cast everyday usage in the mould of metre; in their view there is nothing wonderful in mere rhyming and putting ordinary talk into metre. But if they see that a serious subject has been expressed in ordinary everyday usage with perfect beauty, clarity and ease, then they certainly feel boundless wonder and amazement; for in the art of verse, and especially in the Urdu language, nothing is harder than to express a fine subject completely in ordinary speech and everyday usage. Even the verse of those who held keeping to everyday usage above everything else shows lapses and shortfalls here and there when looked at with a critical eye. So when a verse, along with the weight and seriousness of its subject, also comes out complete in everyday usage and idiom, every person of taste is inevitably struck with wonder. For instance, Mir Insha'allah Khan states the fact that in a state of dejection the teasing of joy, pleasure and revelry is very unwelcome, thus:

Don't tease me, scent of the spring breeze, go your way; you are in a playful mood, and here we sit weary of it all.

Or, for instance, Mirza Ghalib states so large a subject as this (when I reached the beloved's house I first stood silent, and the doorkeeper, taking me for a beggar, said nothing; when the longing to see the beloved grew beyond bounds and I had no strength left to be patient, I fell at the doorkeeper's feet; now he knew that my purpose was something else; he treated me in a way not fit to be told) in two hemistichs thus:

Taking me for a beggar he was silent; then my ill luck came upon me: I rose, and rising, I clasped the doorkeeper's feet.

Or, for instance, Mirza Ghalib says:

With weeping we grew yet bolder in love; we were so washed that we came out quite clean.

It is the rule that so long as a man hides his love and affection he has regard for everything, but when the secret is out he has no shame or reserve before anyone. This verse expresses that subject. *Dhoya jana* ("to be washed") means to become shameless and without regard, and *pak* ("clean") means a free man, a rake. Being washed for weeping, and being clean for being washed: with all these verbal correspondences, and the setting of the idiom and the clarity of everyday usage, the subject is fully expressed, and nothing is unnatural.

Or, for instance, Momin Khan says:

Yesterday, when you avoided my eyes in the rival's company, I was so lost that the others found it out.

*Ankhen churana* ("to steal the eyes") is to ignore and pay no attention; *khoya jana* ("to be lost") is to be abashed and embarrassed; *pa jana* ("to find") is to understand or see through; the meaning is plain. The subject of this verse too is wholly natural, and the setting of the idioms and the clarity of everyday usage deserve praise. Although its source is this verse of Mirza Ghalib:

Though the manner of indifference keeps the secret of love veiled, yet we become so lost that she finds it out,

it is composed with more clarity in Momin. The following verses are of the same kind:

Ascetic, do not tease the ruined libertine; what is another's business to you? Settle your own.
The gait of weak me is the writhing of a slaughtered bird; at every step I am sure I will drop here, drop there.
If this is the state of helplessness, messenger, then we shall come and see her feet ourselves.
Perhaps this is what is called love, Shefta: a kind of fire burning inside the breast.
So faithfulness has vanished from the world, as if it had never been here at all.

In short, keeping to everyday usage is a most necessary thing, as far as possible, in all the forms of verse generally and in the *ghazal* especially; and idiom too, provided it is composed with skill, is an ornament of verse. Since this discussion is very long, we end it here; if we find occasion, we shall express our thoughts on this subject separately at another time.

(c) Founding verse on *sana'i* and *bada'i* (rhetorical figures and ornaments) usually loses the thread of meaning and leaves no effect at all in the verse, because the thought passing through the hearer's mind that the poet has used artifice in arranging the verse and wished to show off his craftsmanship in words wholly destroys the verse's effect. So one should always avoid being bound to figures and making a rule of them, in all forms of verse generally and in the *ghazal* especially. Figures, as set out in detail in the science of rhetoric, are held to be of two kinds: one of meaning, such as *tibaq* (antithesis), *mushakala* (similarity of expression), *aks* (inversion), *tauriya* (double entendre), *husn-e-ta'lil* (poetic aetiology), *tajahul-e-arifana* (feigned ignorance), *ta'ajjub* (wonder) and the like; the other verbal, such as *tajnis* (paronomasia), *radd al-ajz ala al-sadr* (repetition of the opening word at the close), *manquta* (using only dotted letters), *ghair manquta* (using only undotted letters), *raqta*, *khaifa*, *muqatta'*, *mausal*, *tarsi'* and the like. All the famous poets of Arabic and Persian used all the figures of the first kind and particular figures of the second kind, but never made a rule of them or founded their verse on them. True, if by happy chance some fitting word occurred to them that did no harm to the intended meaning and brought more beauty into the expression, they certainly did not let such an occasion slip; as Khwaja Hafiz says:

Under the gilded patched robe they keep lassos; behold the long reach of these short-sleeved ones!

In this verse there is the figure of antithesis in "long" and "short", and *mura'at al-nazir* (harmony of related terms) in "hand" and "sleeve"; but both figures occur so easily and fittingly that instead of disturbing the intended meaning they have given it more force and doubled the beauty of the verse. Or as Mir Taqi says:

These two eyes full of tears: both of them are one ruin of a house.

In this the word *ek* ("one") occurs so spontaneously and easily that it is as if the poet never intended it. Here *ek* means extreme, matchless, unrivalled, out-and-out; as one says, "He is *one* scoundrel," or "Those people are *one* lot of rebels." The coming of the word "one" against "both" has raised the verse very high; otherwise, in its bare subject it had no substance at all. Here in truth it is not merely the figure of harmony of related terms that has produced the highest eloquence in this verse; it is the fact that the word "one" has been applied to two things with such beauty and ease that nothing better can be imagined; otherwise, to bring together "one" and "two" in a verse or a hemistich, which is what *mura'at al-nazir* means, would have been no great thing.

*Husn-e-matla'* (the second opening couplet):
One is all fire, one all water; eye and heart are both a torment.

In this verse too the contrast of fire and water occurs with perfect ease. So if verbal correspondence of this kind arises in a verse by chance, it is an ornament of poetry; but by deliberately searching for such correspondences poetry in the end ceases to be poetry and becomes buffoonery. A famous poet says:

The cat of your door will break the bird of my heart; the mouse of your nose will gnaw the garment of my body. [TN: *chuha*, "mouse", is also the name of a nose-ornament.]

Since bringing in a mouse for the cat was obligatory, when no real mouse could be found he was forced to make do with the "mouse" of the nose.

The real merit of food is that it should be tasty, wholesome and fit to become part of the body; it should also have a good smell and a good colour and look; if, with all this, it is eaten from china dishes, so much the better. So it is with verse. The real merit of verse is that it should be natural, moving, and cast in the mould in both words and meaning; if with this some verbal correspondence is also found in it, so much the better, but otherwise there is no need of it.

In every language (if our guess is not wrong) you will find more verbal artifice in the verse of the later poets than of the earlier, because most of the later poets repeat the very subjects that the ancients composed before them; so unless they put verbal artifice to work, they cannot show any marvel in those ordinary things.

Among the later poets the idea of figures arises mostly because in the verse of the ancients some verses are found in which, along with beauty of meaning, some verbal correspondence has also arisen by chance. Since those verses are generally admired, some people mistakenly think that the cause of their acceptance is that verbal correspondence and nothing else. Now they begin to use those same figures in their verse with effort, fitly and unfitly, and give no thought at all to the real merit in the verse of the ancients. The comparison is exactly this: a handsome, well-dressed man, on whom no clothing looks ill, happens to go out in a cap of gold braid or an embroidered coat, and people in rivalry with him begin to wear the same clothes, not understanding that the real cause of his good looks is his beauty and grace, not the cap of gold braid and the embroidered coat.

Verbal artifice has done boundless harm to our poetry, indeed to our whole literature, which would need a separate book to set out in detail. The gist is that just as, by honouring the wonders of nature more and more, the world in the end fell into the worship of wonders and the thought of God was lost, so in our literature, as the taste for verbal figures grew and grew, in the end mere word-worship was left and the thought of meaning was wholly lost. Being bound to figures and ornaments is generally found very little among the poets of Delhi; indeed, one should say not at all; but some poets of Lucknow have made a strict rule of it, and compared with the people of Delhi the ordinary poets of Lucknow too pay more attention to verbal correspondence; even so, compared with Persian, Urdu poetry is well protected from this plague. So far as we know, those absurd verbal figures in which meaning is wholly set aside and a mere puzzle of words is made, such as *manquta*, *ghair manquta*, *raqta*, *khaifa*, *zu-qafiyatain* (double-rhymed), *zu-bahrain* (readable in two metres) and the like, are rare in Urdu poetry; but in place of verbal figures another disease has arisen in the Urdu *ghazal*, which murders meaning even more than the figures do.

(d) The later poets of Lucknow and Delhi have written thousands of *ghazals* in *sanglakh zaminen* ("stony grounds", that is, very difficult rhyme-and-refrain schemes). In Mir, Sauda, Jur'at, Dard and Asar very few *ghazals* are found in such grounds; it began from the time of Mushafi and Insha, and Shah Nasir tried his nature in it more than anyone. Zauq too had a great passion for it at the start of his career; in Zafar's verse too there are many such grounds. Ghalib, Momin, Mamnun, Shefta, Dagh and others, however, chose such grounds very seldom. The poets of Lucknow too have written countless *ghazals* in hard grounds.

Those who wish to discharge fully the duties of poetry can judge that in producing a verse nothing is so hard as finding a rhyme suited to the subject of the verse; that is why, when someone is in a hard spot, they say, "His rhyme has run short." To escape these very difficulties of rhyme the poets of Europe in the end devised "blank verse", that is, unrhymed verse, and now poetry there rests mostly on verse of this kind. With us, on top of this, a tail of *radif* has been attached behind the rhyme. Although the *radif* is not held to be as necessary as the rhyme, in the *ghazal*, and especially in the Urdu *ghazal*, it has been given the same rank as the rhyme; if one searched all the Urdu *diwans* for *ghazals* without *radif*, perhaps such *ghazals* would be found countable on the fingers. So since the pass of *radif* and rhyme is itself hard to cross, to make it harder still and impassable can only be the work of those who have nothing to do with meaning and think the end of poetry is mere rhyme-measuring and nothing more.

So far as one surveys the stony grounds, in them either a *radif* and rhyme are chosen that have no affinity with one another, such as *taqrir pusht-e-a'ina*, *takhmir pusht-e-a'ina*, *tadbir pusht-e-a'ina* ("the speech / fermentation / plan of the back of the mirror"); and *jabal ki makkhi*, *mahal ki makkhi*, *dol ki makkhi* ("the fly of the mountain / palace / bucket"); and *asas ki tiliyan*, *nigas ki tiliyan*, *nafas ki tiliyan* ("the sticks of the night-watch / ... / breath") [TN: *nigas* is given as printed; the word is uncertain]. Or a *radif* is chosen so long that it cannot come reasonably into more than one or two verses, such as *falak pa bijli zamin pa baran* ("lightning in the sky, rain on the earth"), *sar turra har gale men* ("a plume on the head, a garland round the neck"), *gah khadang gah kaman* ("now arrow, now bow"). In short, they deliberately devise a pattern in which composing a fine subject is certainly impossible, and even getting a verse with meaning out of it is close to impossible for ordinary poets, apart from very practised and expert masters. In such grounds the poet's great feat is thought to be that the antipathy between rhyme and *radif* should seem to vanish, as if oil and water were being mixed. There seems only a little difference between such *ghazals* and the *anmel* (nonsense verses) of Amir Khusrau. Amir Khusrau joined these four things, rice pudding, spinning wheel, drum and dog, thus:

She cooked rice pudding with great care and lit the spinning wheel for fuel; the dog came and ate it, so she sits and beats the drum.

One poet joins the snuffers and the back of the mirror thus:

When that rose, wearing a thumb-ring mirror, snuffs the candle, we will call her thumb "the snuffers of the back of the mirror."

Another poet has stitched together *kal* ("machine") and the fly thus:

Heart, go and see the craft of the Chinese doll, if you wish to see the fly of the machine.

From this one may judge that in all stony grounds nothing is intended but to prove a match between two ill-matched things. So the poet should always choose a *radif* that agrees with the rhyme, and *radif* and rhyme together should not be more than two short words; indeed he should gradually write fewer *ghazals* with a *radif* and be content with rhyme alone. He should choose a rhyme for which ten times, even twenty times, as many words exist as are needed; otherwise the subject will have to follow the rhymes, and the rhymes will not follow the subject. All the famous poets who have lived kept this principle in view, and always chose grounds with room for every kind of subject.
