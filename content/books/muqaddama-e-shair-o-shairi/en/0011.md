# The Qasida and the Marsiya

## The Qasida

The *qasida* too, if its meaning is taken as praise and blame in general, and if it is founded not on mere imitated subjects but on the poet's true passion and ardour, is a most necessary form of verse, without which the poet cannot reach perfection or discharge many of his important and necessary duties. We see that often, on seeing something or hearing of some event, a passion for praise and commendation, or for condemnation and reproach, rises involuntarily in our hearts. Sometimes, learning of someone's justice and equity, or high resolve, or love of country, or sympathy for the nation, or some other virtue, we long to praise him. Sometimes, at the death of a man of good qualities and praiseworthy character, the urge arises in the heart to grieve and to recall his virtues. Sometimes we remember the company of friends now gone, and the picture of their sincere friendship and pure love passes before our eyes, which compels us to speak well of them. Sometimes we pass through some pleasant place, and the urge rises in our heart to describe the delight we found there. In the same way, when some event seems hateful to our heart, or some act or deed deserving condemnation is done by someone, the wish to expose its evil stirs within us. On such occasions it is the poet's duty not to leave idle and useless the gift God has placed in his nature, but to put it to some use, as his nature demands. Just as it is the duty of a philosopher who investigates to inform the world of whatever properties and states of the things of creation are revealed to him, or the duty of a physician not to leave mankind, as far as he can, ignorant of the harms and benefits of drugs, or the duty of a traveller to inform his countrymen of new discoveries, so it should be the poet's duty to make the virtues of the good shine, to make their skills and merits bright in the world, and to leave the means of perfuming the minds of both the present and future generations with the fragrance of their character; and also to take vices and faults to task as far as possible, so that the people of both present and future times may stay alert and wary of the punishment of evil and its consequences. This course will be wholly in keeping with the way of God, for in the Word of God too the wicked are always remembered with their wickedness and the good with their goodness. Al-Mutawakkil asked a poet, "How far do you go in pursuing people with satire, and how long do you go on praising and commending them?" He said, "As long as they do ill and do well," that is, as long as evil and good come from them. Then he said, "God forbid that we should be like the scorpion that stings the prophet and the *dhimmi* (protected non-Muslim) alike," that is, God forbid that our state should be that of the scorpion, which stings both prophet and *dhimmi*.

When someone who deserves praise is praised, the thought arises in him of earning still more praise, or at least of keeping his earlier desert, and in others of emulating him. In the same way, when the faults of those who deserve condemnation are stated by way of allusion, it is to be hoped that, for fear of greater disgrace hereafter, they will turn to reform themselves, or at least be ashamed of or warned against their evil, and that others will think those faults blameworthy and deserving of condemnation. That is why praise should be given in such a style that it does not lead to flattery, and blame should be given in such a way that the side of heartfelt concern prevails over that of taunt and reproach.

The *marsiya* (elegy) too can be called praise, in that it mostly states the virtues and merits of the deceased. The only difference is that the praise of the living is called *qasida*, and the praise of the dead, in which regret and sorrow are also mixed, is called *marsiya*. In the ancient poetry of Arabia, odes and elegies contained such true and accurate states and events that a short life of the deceased could be drawn from them. For instance, in all the elegies written on Abd al-Muttalib, the noble grandfather of the Prophet (God bless him and his family and give them peace), with slight differences, his care for his kin, his sympathy for his people, and his standing as a shield in their difficulties and misfortunes are praised. In every elegy his handsomeness is mentioned. It is also said that he was distinguished and eminent among his people, generous, kind to his countrymen in years of famine, of high lineage, strict in keeping his word and covenants, resolute, gentle-natured, commanding awe, keeping the ties of kinship, modest, plunging fearlessly into perils and dangers, and guarding honour. Some elegies show that from the time of Qusayy ibn Kilab the guardianship of the Ka'ba, the watering of the pilgrims and the building of the Sacred Mosque had come down in the family of Abd al-Muttalib, and that the other Banu Kinana, who were not of Qusayy's line, were jealous of the Banu Qusayy over this. It also appears that the Banu Qusayy had wells dug in and around Mecca for the comfort of their countrymen and the pilgrims, for before that life depended only on the rainwater that collected in hollows and pits. It also appears that the mother of Abu Lahab ibn Abd al-Muttalib was named Lubna and was of the Banu Khuza'a, and that Sa'd, who for twenty years had led the army in defence of the tribe, and Abu Shamir, Amr ibn Malik, Dhu Jadan and Abu al-Jabr were kinsmen of Lubna. Hudhaifa ibn Ghanim, who was of the line of Lu'ayy ibn Ghalib, has mentioned in his elegy on Abd al-Muttalib the kindness that when he himself was seized in Mecca over a debt of four thousand dirhams, Abu Lahab ibn Abd al-Muttalib went and freed him from the clutches of his creditors. In the same way most of the odes and elegies of Arabia are found to contain facts and events.

The state of our *qasidas* is not fit to be told; but our poets have shown a particular and marked progress in the *marsiya*. With us the term *marsiya* is applied mostly to the elegy on the martyrs of Karbala, and especially on the Prince of Martyrs. Here the *marsiya* began on the very principle that nature has taught all human beings alike, namely to remember the dead and express sorrow and grief, and by one's telling to make others sorrowful and grieved; so the elegies first written were no more than twenty or thirty stanzas, or twenty or thirty couplets, more or less, and had no subject but mourning and lament. But since the *marsiya* was confined within the circle of one particular subject, and its value grew day by day, the later poets had no choice but to bring some novelty into it and add something to its subjects. Little by little the *marsiya* swelled greatly, so that Khwaja Haidar Ali Atish, on hearing one of Mirza Dabir's *marsiyas* in a gathering, said in amazement, "Was this a *marsiya*, or the romance of Landhaur bin Sa'dan?" [TN: Landhaur is a hero of the *Dastan-e-Amir Hamza*, the vast oral romance cycle.] Although this progress was not directly a progress of the *marsiya*, but a kind of invention in Urdu poetry, in that into a poem that should have been founded on lament and mourning alone there entered, besides lament and mourning, praise and boasting, scenes of battle and scenes of feasting, with great force and emphasis, yet the truth is that this new kind of poem brought great breadth into Urdu poetry. In this style, so far as we know, Mir Zamir was the first to write *marsiyas*; he is, as it were, its inventor; but Mir Anis, in whose family poetry and *marsiya*-writing had come down for four generations besides his own God-given aptitude, who on top of that was a master of the Urdu language and a man of Lucknow, brought this style to the summit of perfection, and stirred waves, indeed a storm, in Urdu poetry, which had long lain motionless and lifeless like stagnant water [TN: printed *mar rakid*; read as *ma'-e-rakid*, "stagnant water"]. Although the pressure of society and rivalry with lesser competitors did not let Mir Anis keep everywhere to the straight path, but, like the *dhrupad* singer who must sometimes also sing *barahmasa* and *chaubole* to please the empty-headed in the gathering, he had here and there to raise storms of exaggeration and hyperbole, such excesses are of very little substance and weight compared with the benefits the Urdu language gained from his poetry. He brought new styles of expression into Urdu poetry in abundance; by telling a single event in a hundred ways he cleared a new field for the caperings of the imagination; and a considerable part of the language, which the pens of our poets had never even touched and which was confined to the speech of native speakers, he introduced to the poets. In many places in his verse he has hinted, and quite rightly, that the *marsiya*-writers of his day were gleaners of his language and manner of expression. In one place he says:

The rivers of the bounty of the Lord of East and West are flowing; drink, you who are thirsty, this fountain is an offering for Husain.

In another place he says:

I am heaping up new subjects again; tell the gleaners of my harvest.

Nowadays in Europe a poet's mastery is also judged by how many more words than other poets he has used with skill and refinement. If we too make this the standard of mastery, we shall have to accept Mir Anis as the highest of Urdu poets. Although Nazir Akbarabadi has perhaps used even more words than Mir Anis, native speakers think little of his language; whereas before every word and idiom of Mir Anis everyone must bow the head. Mir Anis's verse, as said above, is certainly not free of exaggeration and hyperbole; but at the same time, wherever he paints a picture of events, or draws a portrait of natural states, or fills his telling with the colour of effect, there is sufficient proof that Mir Anis had raised Urdu poetry, as far as was possible in keeping with the demands of the time, to the highest level.

A saying is well known in the company of poets: "A spoiled poet becomes a *marsiya*-writer, and a spoiled singer a *marsiya*-reciter"; but Mir Anis wholly disproved this saying. He has very seldom been regarded as we regard him. Mostly he is respected as a mourner of Imam Husain (peace be upon him). There are also many who, sincerely, or merely out of partisanship for their own sect and opposition to the other, think him superior and pre-eminent only among the *marsiya*-writers; but there are very few who think him truly unrivalled in poetry as such.

If this particular style of *marsiya* is looked at from the point of view of ethics too, in our view only the verse of these men can deserve to be called ethical poetry in Urdu; indeed the equal of the high morals these men have set out in the *marsiya* will be hard to find even in Persian, or even Arabic, poetry.

What higher and nobler model of moral virtue can there be than this? The grandson of the Prophet of the Muslims, before whom every Muslim's head should have bowed and who should have had boundless hopes of them, sees every Muslim, apart from a few kinsmen and friends, thirsting for his blood. There is the scorching wind and heat of the Arabian desert; the women, the young children and the whole family are with him; a journey of months from Medina to Kufa must be made; of those who set out with him as helpers and supporters, all but a few have left him one by one and gone; those who had called him with promises of help and support, sending letters and messages one after another and making God and the Prophet their witness, he finds on arrival wholly turned away and estranged, and all his hopes have turned to despair; yet he is content with God's will, gives thanks to God in every state, and stands firm in his resolve. The man whose rule he has judged a deadly disease for the country, the community and the faith, and to whom he has refused allegiance, he still refuses, despite all these hardships.

The enemy have cut off all food and water, while the river Euphrates flows before his eyes; the enemy's horses, donkeys and even camels drink their fill from it, but his whole family has been thirsty for three days; his little children long for a single drop of water; and all this because he will not give his allegiance to an unworthy man. Yet he stands just as firm in his resolve; no hardship and no misfortune shakes his steadfastness.

His friends and helpers number in all seventy-two men, against a swarm of locusts. In fighting he sees the end of himself and of all his kinsmen and friends; the plundering of tents and goods, the captivity of the survivors, the women stripped of their veils and driven through the desert: all these calamities he sees as if with his own eyes; but he bears them all and thinks them better than giving his allegiance to an unworthy man and acknowledging his rule.

With perfect calm he arms and equips his brother, his sons, his nephews and his sister's sons, and sends them one by one to fight thousands; he sees their arms cut off by swords, their hearts pierced by spears and their breasts riddled with arrows; he carries the body of each on his shoulder and buries it in the earth with his own hands. In the tent the wailing of women and children raises a doomsday at every moment; the heart-rending cries of his wife, daughter and sisters are cutting wounds into his heart; his six-month-old infant, struck by a merciless man's arrow, writhes in his lap like a slaughtered bird, a fountain of blood gushing from its throat. All, young and old, have fallen, and the child too has but a moment to live; now after all of them he sees his own turn, and then he sees no boatman for the ship of the Prophet's household but God. All these calamities face him, and a dark cloud of misfortunes and disasters covers every side; but none of these things can shake his resolve and steadfastness. Like a firm mountain he stands fast in his resolve and does not move from his word.

The merciless people who recite the creed of the grandfather and thirst for the blood of the grandson, who have brought a swarm of locusts to face a handful of souls and are spending all their strength so that every torment and pain that no living being has inflicted on another from Adam until now may be heaped upon the darlings and the very heart's pieces of their own Prophet; who, drunk with greed and covetousness, have given up faith, belief, mercy, justice, humanity, sympathy and all human virtues, and stand ready, girded, to pull down the house of God, that is, to wipe the Prophet's family off the page of existence: he does not curse them, nor complain of them, nor grow angry with them, but with perfect coolness of heart reminds them of his rights, which they claim to acknowledge, and recalls to them the duties they owe to the Prophet's family.

From the youngest to the eldest, every heart is eager to be the first to lay down its life for the family. The father wishes to cast his own dear son into the heat of the swords before his brother, nephews and sister's sons. The brother is ready to die before his brother and nephews, and asks for the battlefield. The sister's sons long to be sacrificed first of all for their uncle and his children. The nephew longs to be the first ransom for his uncle. The sister yearns to sacrifice her children for her brother and nephews. The brother wastes away with the worry, "If my sister's sons are killed in my company, how shall I show my face to my sister?" The uncle is himself tormented by three days' thirst, yet cares nothing for his own thirst, but cannot bear in any way to see his thirsty niece's distress. He hangs the water-skin round his neck and, taking his life in his hands, cuts through the enemy's ranks and drives his horse into the river. The cold sweet water of the river is rolling in waves, and he is at his last breath from thirst; his heart is slipping out of control; two handfuls of water would quench his thirst; but honour and pride do not let him quench his own thirst before the little children's thirst is quenched. He fills the skin and turns back from the river as thirsty as he came, so as to go quickly and drip water into the children's dry throats. But the enemy have surrounded him and cut off both his arms, and even then he thinks nothing of his arms; if he thinks of anything it is the water-skin, lest the water be lost and the children stay thirsty. He takes every blow upon himself, but lets no harm come to the skin, until, broken by wounds, he falls from his horse.

Wives see their husbands, and mothers their sons, wounded and killed, but none utters a cry or even lets out a breath, only lest the heart of the patron and protector in whose company they fell be clouded, and lest he feel ashamed before them in his heart. All pray for his well-being and that of his children; none remembers her own lost ones.

There are two young brothers who, for no crime but being kin of the Prophet's grandson, have been condemned to death by order of the governor. The executioner stands with his sword raised over the heads of both. The elder brother pleads, "Take off my head first," and the younger says, "Strike me first."

A servant of God has come with the enemy's army to fight the Prophet's grandson. In siding with the enemy he has every hope of wealth, rank and office, and in leaving them he is firmly certain of the ruin of his life, property and family; among the people by whom he is surrounded there is no inducement or occasion that could turn his heart from tyranny, cruelty, irreligion and the love of rank and riches towards mercy, sympathy and piety; from every side the cry comes to him, "Quickly win victory over this small band, take off the men's heads, carry off the women and children as captives, and go to the governor to be rewarded for your services"; on the other side he sees no outward means for the sake of which he might give up all these benefits and leave his army, but on the contrary he sees calamities and disasters of every kind before him. Yet he throws dust on all worldly gains and hopes, turns away from these tyrants, holds it the supreme triumph to give his life in the cause of the truth, and is the first to sacrifice his life for the Prophet's family. [TN: this is al-Hurr ibn Yazid al-Riyahi.]

A few faithful companions and friends who are with the son of the Prophet, so few against a swarm of locusts that they can be counted on the fingers, find a whole world turned away and estranged from their chief; they have seen his own companions and comrades leave him one by one along the way, stealing away with averted eyes; they see no immediate gain and no worldly good for themselves in siding with him, but face death at every moment; for the sake of his company their lives have been on their lips for three days from hunger and thirst; there is no kinship or relationship to keep them from leaving him; but the collar of faithfulness is on their necks and the chain of friendship and sincerity on their feet; no fear and no greed can break this bond. Their constant longing is: when will leave to fight be given, and when shall we sacrifice our lives for the Prophet's family and be quit of this duty?

These few points have been drawn, merely in passing, from the general statements of the *marsiyas* as heard now and then and kept in our memory; if one searched further, many more such points could be drawn. In our view, poems setting out morals of so high a degree will be hard to find not only in Urdu but in Persian and Arabic poetry too. But alas, the effect such ethical poems should have on the human heart neither falls, nor can fall, on the hearts of the listeners to these *marsiyas*. First, the idea that the real purpose of the *marsiya* is only to weep and make others weep does not let the listeners turn their attention elsewhere at all; second, the belief (that the patience, steadfastness, courage, sympathy, faithfulness, honour, pride, firm resolve and other excellent morals shown by the noble Imam himself and his kinsmen and friends in the battle of Karbala were beyond human strength and were miracles) never lets even the thought of following and imitating them enter the heart.

In any case, we praise from the heart the *marsiya* of Mir Anis and *marsiya*-writing in the new style; but we by no means advise poets of the new trend to follow him or other *marsiya*-writers in *marsiya*-writing. First, there is no hope that anyone can now attain his mastery in this particular style. Second, to bring scenes of battle and feasting, boasting and self-praise, the head-to-foot description of the beloved and the like into the *marsiya*, to compose long preludes and introductions, to indulge in subtle fancies and high flights in praise of the horse and the sword and so on, and to show off poetic skill, are wholly contrary to the theme of the *marsiya*; it is exactly as if someone, to express his grief and sorrow at the death of his father or brother, were to think up and compose colourful rhymed phrases, and display his eloquence instead of his grief and sorrow. We do not say that no thought or reflection should go into composing a *marsiya* and no use at all be made of the poet's craft; we say that, as far as possible, all the mastery of poetry should be spent on clarity of language, simplicity and naturalness of subject, making the verse moving, and making *award* look like *amad*, so that verses composed after endless thought, reflection and pruning seem to have dropped spontaneously from the poet's pen. Third, to confine the *marsiya* to the event of Karbala alone and to keep repeating this one subject all one's life is no harm if it is done merely with the intention of earning religious merit, but the duties of poetry should be wider than this. *Marsiya* means grieving at someone's death and keeping his name alive in the world by stating his virtues and merits. So it should be the duty of the poet, who is the tongue of the nation, that when someone's death really strikes his heart, or the heart of his nation or family, he should show that feeling or state in the dress of verse with as much pain and burning as possible; for there is no better occasion for expressing the pure love that one has for another, and the sincere respect one feels for another, than when the one praised sleeps unknowing in the sleep of non-existence, and no hope of gain or fear of harm from him remains. Now if the poet's heart is truly so pure of worldly ties that it is moved and changed by no one's death except that of those near to God, then to burden him with writing elegies on ordinary people would certainly be an impossible burden; but if he has no such pure heart in his breast, but feels for ordinary human beings and his heart melts at the death of worldly people too, then he should certainly fulfil the demand of his nature.

It is true that the description of the sorrows and sufferings of the Prince of Martyrs and his kinsmen and companions, provided there is no affectation, artifice or display of poetic craft in it, refreshes a Muslim's faith, strengthens the bond of love and sincerity with the Prophet's family, which is the root of Islam, and teaches the lesson of following their matchless patience and steadfastness. But just as all these things are needed, so too is it needed to breathe the spirit of nationhood into the nation; and it is breathed in only when the members of the nation, like the members of one family, feel for one another, value one another's worthy efforts, help and support one another in good works, make one another's virtues shine in life, spread the fame of one another's accomplishments, and after death raise such memorials to them as will never be wiped off the page of existence. Odes of praise written in the patron's lifetime are not such proof of his virtues as the unbiased elegies and laments after his death. That is why our ancient poets, whose clay was of the pure dust of Arabia, when some eminent man passed away from the nation, wrote his elegies with the same eagerness, passion and fervour with which they composed odes of praise in his lifetime. Poets were put to death again and again for elegies on the Barmakids, but people did not stop writing elegies on them. For writing an elegy on Ma'n ibn Za'ida, a poet was thrown out of the court by the caliph of the time with the utmost disgrace; even so countless elegies were written on him. Alam al-Huda al-Sharif al-Murtada, despite the difference of religion, wrote an elegy on Abu Ishaq al-Sabi with such burning grief as one feels at the death of one's own closest kin, and praised his learning and merit beyond measure. In the same way thousands of elegies have been written on the deaths of men of learning, men of accomplishment, heroes, generous men, good-hearted kings, able ministers and other distinguished people.

But for one who wishes to attain mastery in writing the *marsiya*, no better guide can be found in Urdu poetry than this new style of *marsiya*. If one sets aside the things in these worthies' verse that go against the dignity of elegy, the student of the art can learn a most excellent lesson from it. But alas, the *qasida*, first, has been written so little in Urdu compared with Persian and Arabic that it is as if it had not been written at all; second, no model of it can be pointed to in Urdu whose footsteps one should follow. Sauda first and Zauq last are the only two who have written odes, more or less, in the manner of the ode-writers of Iran, and have kept up very well the course that had come down from of old; but of the kind of *qasida* that is now needed, or will be needed, or ought to be, there is no model in our language. Perhaps with much searching such models as could be followed might be found somewhat more in Arabic and here and there in Persian; but the truth is that to look in Asiatic poetry for models on which praise or satire could be founded in keeping with present-day ideas is exactly like looking for freedom of opinion among the subjects of a despotic government. In countries where from the beginning of creation kings and their officers of state have been continually worshipped, where the safety, indeed the very life, of the subject depends on flattery, obedience and submission, where "subject" and "slave" are thought to be synonyms, and where freedom is a word whose meaning no one even knows, it is not possible for the principles of praise and blame to be founded on truth, reason and justice. So there is no choice but to take the method of praise and blame from the present poetry of Europe, and to found the *qasidas* of the future on that method.
