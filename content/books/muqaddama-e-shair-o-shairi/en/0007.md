# Hebrew and Arabic Poetry Had the Most Passion

## Hebrew and Arabic Poetry Had the Most Passion

The poetry of the Hebrews has been held to be the most passionate of all. A European scholar says that "there is so much passion in the verse of the Hebrew poets that, hearing it, one feels as if a mighty tree were burning in the desert, or as if revelation were descending upon a man." The poetry of Arabia seems on the face of it to be founded on Hebrew poetry, for in it too boundless passion is found. That is why, as the historians of Europe write, the Arabs disliked the poetry of the Greeks: beside their own poetry, Greek poetry seemed to them insipid, cold and full of *award* (contrivance). Of all the books of the Greeks that they translated, not a single collection of verse was translated. They did not think Homer, Sophocles and Pindar equal to their own poets. Here, as a sample, we write the substance of a short Arabic poem in Urdu to show readers how much passion the Arabs displayed in verse; but since it is impossible for the beauty of the Arabic language to survive in Urdu, this will be an imperfect sample of Arabic verse.

Bashama ibn Hazn al-Nahshali, a poet of the Islamic period, says in verses of pride:

"We, the grandsons of Nahshal, take pride in being the grandsons of Nahshal, and Nahshal takes pride in being our grandfather."

"To whatever goal of honour and eminence the horses are raced, whenever you find those that lead them all, you will find they are the horses of the Banu Nahshal."

"No chief among us leaves the world until he leaves behind a son fit to be his successor."

"On the day of battle we make our lives cheap; but in time of peace, if anyone asks their price, they are beyond price."

"The hair at our partings is white (from the use of perfumes); our cooking-pots are hot for guests; our wealth is set aside for the blood-money of our slain."

"I am of a people whose elders, at the mere words of the enemy, 'Where are the defenders of the tribe?', gave themselves up to destruction."

"If one of us is present among a thousand, then when it is asked, 'Who is the horseman?', his eye will fall upon himself alone."

"However hard a calamity falls on our people, you will not find them weeping over their slain as others do."

"We often plunge into terrible places, but our honour and our swords, which have pledged their word to us, make all our difficulties easy."

The cause of the greater passion in Arab poetry lay partly in the innate quality of their hot blood, but mostly in this: that their poetry was founded wholly on events and on the true states and experiences of the heart. Love poems were mostly written by those who really had a lover's attachment to someone. War poems were recited by those who really were men of the field in battle and combat. In poems of pride they described only those deeds that had openly been done by themselves, by their elders or by the people of their tribe, and through which their bravery, generosity or eloquence had become proverbial. Their elegies were not mere imitation: the man whose heart was struck by the death of a friend, a kinsman, an elder or a famous man wrote his elegy, and drew a true picture of the experience of his heart. Love, enmity, sympathy, patience, steadfastness, anger, revenge, youth, old age, the instability of the world, the greatness and majesty of God, the condemnation of the oppressor, help for the oppressed, the keeping or the breaking of the ties of kinship: in short, whatever subject's passion rose in their hearts, they expressed it without artifice or affectation. But alas, from the time of the Abbasid caliphate this true passion began to decline, and in the end imitation spread through every kind of poetry. Instead of being a mirror of the poet's own feelings, verse became a mirror of the manner and style of the ancients, indeed of their very feelings, and the organ of their thoughts. The ancients truly took pride in their own notable deeds and those of their elders; the later poets began to mimic them with false self-praise, and called this the *sunna* (established custom). The ancients truly described the feelings and experiences of their hearts in love for some real beloved or other, and that is why hundreds of real names of their beloveds are found in their verse, such as Laila, Salma, Su'ad, Sa'da, Adhra, Azza, Khawla, Buthaina, Unaiza, Fatim, Zainab and so on. But the later poets, like infants at the breast who cry and do not know why they cry, merely by imitation attached imaginary names and began to wail about separation from them and their longing and desire. Little by little this colour passed from Arabia to Iran, and from there to India, and in the end the poetry of the Muslims came to be like a deserted town that was once full of people but where now nothing is to be seen but empty houses.

Now we write a few examples of verses in which all three of Milton's conditions are found, or one or two of them, or none at all.

(1) Ibn Yahya ibn Ziyada says, on accepting the hardships of the world gladly: [TN: the name is given as printed; the poet intended is uncertain.]

When I saw that grey hair had shown its whiteness at the parting of my head, I said to the grey hair, "Welcome!"
Had I feared that if I withheld my greeting it would turn aside from me, I would have sought to make it turn aside;
but when a hateful thing comes down and the soul one day bears it with good grace, that does most to take the hateful thing away.

That is: when I saw that old age had appeared in the hair of my head, I bade it welcome. Had there been any hope that it would be put off by my not doing so, I would have tried to put it off; but the truth is that there is no better way of warding off misfortune than to accept it with an open brow.

(2) Mutammim ibn Nuwaira writes in his elegy on his brother Malik:

My companion blamed me at the graves for weeping, for the shedding of flowing tears;
he said, "Do you weep at every grave you see, for a grave that lies between al-Liwa and al-Dakadik?"
I said to him, "Grief stirs up grief; so let me be, for all of this is Malik's grave."

That is: when I began to weep at the sight of the graveyard, my companion, seeing my tears flow, reproached me: "For a grave that lies (far away from here) between al-Liwa and al-Dakadik (that is, Malik's grave), you burst into tears at the sight of every grave." I said, "(My dear friend,) one sorrow brings back another; so let me weep; to me these are all Malik's graves."

(3) Nasir Khusraw states the truth about the world:

Nasir Khusraw was passing along a road, drunk and senseless, yet not like the drinkers of wine;
he saw a few graves and a dung-heap facing them; he cried out and said, "O you who look on,
behold the bounty of the world and those who feasted on it: this is its bounty, and these the feasters."

(4) Nizami says in a prayer:

Lift the veil and come forth alone; and if I am that veil, fold it up.

(5) Naziri says as he takes leave of the House of God:

I am a drunken minstrel come from the private chamber of the King; made merry by His favour, I have come back with songs.

(6) Khwaja Hafiz describes a particular ecstatic state of his own, to which the unfeeling are strangers, thus:

A dark night, the fear of the waves, and a whirlpool so terrible: how can those who walk lightly on the shores know our state?

(7) Shaikh Ibrahim Zauq expresses the thought that if no rest is found even after death, then there is no way left of comforting the heart, thus:

Now in despair we say that we shall die; but if we find no peace even in death, where shall we go?

(8) Mirza Ghalib expresses the nothingness and worthlessness of man thus:

What joy in living, what grief in dying? What is our life, and what are we?

(9) Mir Taqi draws a picture of excessive love and attachment thus:

Whenever I speak your name my eyes fill with tears; where shall I find the heart for a life like this?

(10) Khwaja Mir Dard shows that his own fame and acceptance are wholly baseless thus:

We took a few charges upon ourselves and went; why did we come, and what have we done before we go?

In all these examples, as is plain, all three things, simplicity of expression, reality and passion, are found in the finest way.

(11) Naziri describes the state when he had resolved on the pilgrimage, and the longing to be free of worldly ties and to turn to God was surging in his heart, thus:

I am the dog of the threshold, but all night I chew my collar, for I have a mind to hunt and no desire to keep watch.
It would be strange if some Khizr were not seeking me, for I have fallen into darkness like the water of life.

In the first verse, in view of his being caught in worldly ties, he calls himself the dog of the threshold that guards its master's house all night; but in view of his wishing to give up those ties and turn to God, he likens himself to a hunting dog that, in its eagerness for the hunt, chews its collar all night so that it may cut it and take the road to the forest in search of game. In the second verse he expresses this thought: that man, who has the capacity to rise until he reaches the highest assembly of angels, being soiled with worldly ties is as if the water of life were hidden in darkness. And since the attraction of divine grace is always lying in wait for man, to draw him to itself and free him from the snare of ties, and since it is also well known that Khizr took Alexander with him in search of the water of life, he likens the divine attraction to Khizr and himself to the water of life and says: it would be strange if Khizr were not seeking me, for I lie in darkness like the water of life.

In these two verses both reality and passion of the highest degree are found with perfect beauty. Although it is unfeeling to say of such eloquent verses that anything is lacking in them or that any excellence falls short, yet in the light of the meaning of simplicity explained above it can be said that the simplicity in them is not such that ordinary speakers or knowers of the language can understand it well.

(12) Momin expresses the thought that it is inevitable for the people of the world to be caught in some calamity or other, and that therefore whenever I am safe from one calamity I wait for another, thus:

I fear that lightning may fall from the sky, now that the hunter's eye is not on the nest.

In this verse both reality and passion are found, but the third thing, simplicity, by which is meant simplicity of both words and thought, is certainly not found; for unless the sentence "it is inevitable for the people of the world to be caught in some calamity or other" is added to the verse, ordinary minds cannot pass to the intended meaning. But the poet has put a subtlety into it that can be a good substitute for simplicity; had the statement been plainer, that subtlety would not have remained. He has, as it were, left out this sentence on purpose, and wishes to imply that it is so self-evident that it need not be mentioned.

(13) Atish says:

In the days of childhood I had not a moment's respite from weeping; I have been reared in the lap of the flood.
On the road to non-being the garment of the body became an offering to the grave; I carried a load of goods, but for the thief.
I saw the shore of my desire when I went into the grave; the sinking of the boat of the body was good news of the shallows.

In these three verses some kind of reality might perhaps, with difficulty, be found; but, as is plain, there is neither simplicity of expression nor passion.

(14) Naziri says:

Heaven did not give me so much room at your table that I could touch my lip with a finger of salt from your salt-cellar.
When will a resurrection come, that the order of the world be turned upside down? How long shall my goods be in the heavens and my fortune at the Fish? [TN: *samak*, the Fish on which, in the old cosmology, the earth rests: the lowest point of creation.]

The meaning of the first verse is that from the table of the knowledge of God I did not get even so much share as to put salt on my finger from the salt-cellar and taste it.

In the second verse he shows that in his capacity and aptitude he is a heavenly substance, but his fortune, because of its lowliness, lies in the depths beneath the earth. So he says: would that such a resurrection, that is, such a revolution, might come as would turn the world upside down, so that my fortune might rise from the depths to the heights. In these two verses reality and passion are well found, but the manner of expression is somewhat above ordinary minds.

(15) Atish says:

Imitating you, the mountain partridge stumbled; when a creature walks the gait of man, its ways go wrong.
Not without reason do the wounds of the martyrs laugh so much; the mouth of your sword, O swordsman, has been somewhat spoiled.
The earth kept us like a trust until the Day of Judgement; not one hair of ours grew less, not one thread of our shroud was spoiled.

These three verses are clear, but apart from simplicity of expression there is in them, as is plain, neither reality nor passion.

(16) Zauq says:

Who knows what suspicion she has of me, that she does not come alone even in a dream at night?
If we take to weeping we will pour out a river; we do not know how to weep like the dew.

In these verses too, apart from simplicity of expression, there is neither reality nor passion.

Now only two possibilities remain: one, that only passion is found in a verse, and not simplicity or reality; two, that simplicity and passion are found, but not reality. But reality is so necessary for passion that without it passion can never really exist in verse. So neither of these two cases can occur.

As for verse in which none of the three, simplicity, passion or reality, is found, the *diwans* of our poets are full of it; for our poetry is confined mostly to two kinds of subject: love or praise. Love subjects are composed in abundance in the *ghazal*, the *masnavi* and the *tashbib* (amatory prelude) of the *qasida*, and subjects of praise mostly in the *qasida*. In these three forms the poet's task is thought to be this: to keep composing, with the slightest change, the very subjects that have been composed from of old and that, by constant composing, have become like accepted principles, and never to go a hair's breadth beyond them. For instance, in the *ghazal*: always to describe the beloved as faithless, without courtesy, without kindness, merciless, a tyrant, a killer, a hunter, an executioner, fickle, hating one, consorting with others, never believing in true love, taking the lustful for true lovers, suspicious, ill-tempered, foul-mouthed, of bad conduct; in short, apart from beauty and grace, coquetry and charm and other love-stirring ways, to describe her with every evil that one human being can do to another; and to describe oneself as grief-stricken, afflicted, struck by heaven, weak, sick, ill-starred, a vagabond, disgraced, rejected by mankind, fond of vagrancy, eager for disgrace, shunning good repute, avoiding joy and well-being, a wine-drinker, a drunkard, senseless, self-forgetful, faithful, enduring cruelty, here free-spirited and there longing for captivity, here patient and there restless, here mad and there sane, here jealous of honour and there thick-skinned, the very image of jealousy, [illegible] of rivals, suspicious of the whole world, complaining of heaven, lamenting the earth, harassed by the age; in short [illegible], apart from love and faithfulness, to describe oneself with all the qualities generally thought [illegible] regrettable in a man. Or, for instance, to complain of heaven and the age, or of fortune and the stars; or to trample on the ascetic, the preacher and the Sufi, and to praise the wine-drinker, the wine-seller, the cupbearer and the tavern, and [illegible] to show devotion; to show hatred of faith and Islam, asceticism and obedience, and a liking for unbelief and irreligion, sin and disobedience; now and then to hold worldly wealth, rank and office in contempt, and to prefer poverty, love, freedom and the like to learning, reason, kingship and the like. There are a few other subjects of this kind that have become like the pillars and elements of the *ghazal*. The words peculiar to the *ghazal* too are confined within a very narrow circle. For instance: to liken the beloved's face to a *houri*, a fairy, the moon, the sun, the rose, the tulip, the garden, Paradise and so on; her eye to the narcissus, the deer, the almond, a sorcerer, drunk, sick and so on; her tresses to the hyacinth, musk, ambergris, an infidel, a magician, night, darkness, a snare, a chain, a lasso and so on; her glance, eyelashes, wink and grace to the arrow, the spear, the sword and so on; her eyebrow to the bow; her chin to a well; her teeth to pearls; her lips to ruby, garnet, rose-petal, sugar-candy, the water of life and so on; her mouth to a bud; her waist to a hair, or both to non-existence; her stature to the cypress, the pine, the box-tree, Doomsday and so on; her gait to the tumult of Doomsday, calamity, disaster, upheaval and so on; and in the same way to liken certain other parts of the body to a few particular things. Of the beloved's articles of adornment, to mention the tire-woman, the comb, the mirror, henna, *surma* (collyrium), *kajal* (lamp-black), rouge, *missi* (tooth-powder) and *pan*; sometimes the gown, the gown's ties, the cap, the turban-band, the turban; and sometimes the *burqa*, the veil, the bodice, the wrap, the braid, the bangles and particular ornaments; and to liken them to particular things.

From the garden, to select a few things, such as the cypress, the ringdove, the rose, the nightingale, the fowler, the flower-picker, the gardener, the nest, the cage, the snare, the grain, jasmine, the wild rose, the musk-rose, the Judas-tree, the lily, the thorn, the rosebush and so on.

From the desert: the valley, the spring, running water, greenery, the thirsty, the quenched, the mirage, the icy blast, the whirlwind, the simoom, the date-palm, the plane-tree, the acacia thorn, the highwayman, the guide, Khizr, the caravan, the camel-bell, the sound of the bell, the litter, Laila, Majnun, wildness, madness and so on.

From the river: the boat, the boatman, the wave, the whirlpool, the shore, the bubble, the drop, the fish, the crocodile, the dive, swimming and so on.

From the gathering: the candle, the moth, wine, roast meat, the cup, the decanter, the flask, the wine-jar, the draught, intoxication, the hangover, the morning draught, the cupbearer, the round, the song, the minstrel, the harp, the organ, the plectrum, the fret, the instrument, the dance, ecstasy, *sama'* (spiritual music) and so on.

From the stock of grief: the lament, the sigh, the cry, anxiety, restlessness, pain, jealousy, restraint, longing, separation, remembrance, desire, regret, frustration, sorrow, grief, affliction, burning, the scar, the wound, the pang, the throb, the wasting and so on. These and a few other words of the same kind are what the *ghazal*-writing of the Urdu language now rests on.

In the *qasida* too there are only a few ordinary circles in which our poets keep exercising the black steed of thought. If someone wants to show more of the jewels of poetry, he writes a prelude before the praise, in which there is either a description of the spring season, though it may in fact be autumn, and yet this description has nothing to do with the spring of this impure world but concerns some other world beyond the realm of the possible; or a complaint against the age, heaven, fortune and fate, which should really be understood as a complaint against God, made with full heart under the cover of the age and the rest; and in this too the poet does not describe his real misfortunes, nor say anything to move the patron to pity him, but describes, with the slightest change, the same kind of misfortunes that the poets of earlier times described of themselves, and lays the same kind of slanders on heaven and the age that they did; or the beauty of an imaginary beloved is praised, her cruelty and tyranny are complained of, and one's own longing and waiting are described, continuously or not, just as in love *masnavis* or *ghazals*; or the whole prelude is spent in boasting and self-praise.

After this the praise begins. In the praise, apart from a name, there is usually no particular mentioned that belongs to the patron alone; the praise is made in such general words that if, say, the panegyrist were charged in court for having praised such-and-such a person, not one word would be found in the *qasida* to prove his crime. The praise mostly states the same ordinary virtues that poets have been composing from of old, and in describing each virtue such exaggeration is used that no human being could in fact be the subject of the *qasida*. The real virtues in the patron are not touched on at all; in their place impossible things are stated that could be true of no living soul. Often the patron is credited with virtues whose opposites are present in him: for instance, an ignorant man with learning and excellence, a tyrant with justice and equity, a fool and a sluggard with wisdom and alertness, a helpless weakling with power and might, a man whose thigh has never touched a horse's back with horsemanship and knightly skill. In short, nothing is said of which the patron could be proud, or which could create respect and love for him in people's hearts, or by which his virtues and deeds could remain a memorial in the world.

The state of our *masnavis* is this: in them, after the customary praise of God and of the Prophet and so on, there is usually first a description of the beauty and so on of some prince, or vizier's son, or nobleman's son, or merchant's boy; then he is made to fall for some fairy, or princess, or vizier's daughter, or someone else; first he wanders from city to city and forest to forest in separation from her, and then in the end he succeeds in union. This success is so certain that it can be foretold from the start.

Those who really are poets of acknowledged standing, or who think themselves so, will, when they write a *masnavi*, certainly write one of this kind. It is true that *masnavis* on historical, religious or moral subjects have also been seen from those who are not poets of that rank; but first, these subjects are dry and insipid in themselves, and then their writers neither want to put any warmth into the telling nor are able to; so no one even raises an eye to these *masnavis*. So among us only those *masnavis* flourish that are founded on love.

Although the custom of founding a story on love or bravery has come down from of old, and even the refined stories of today are not very popular unless the colour of love or bravery is filled into them, there is a very great difference between our *masnavis* and theirs. Among us, whatever kinds of events two or four masters first composed, the same events are composed again and again with the slightest change. In the manner of expression, the comparisons, the beloved's head-to-foot description, the beginning and end of the story and so on, they are mostly imitated. The ending is always arranged, according to the ancient custom, as union after separation and comfort after misfortune. The states and experiences that really pass, or could pass, through the hearts of lover and beloved in their love for one another are very seldom touched on. Drawing moral lessons from love subjects is never thought of, even by mistake. There is no effect at all in the telling, because the poet, wishing to bring some novelty into his *masnavi* beyond the old *masnavis*, is wholly absorbed in working out verbal ornaments, and so finds no time to put any effect into his verse.

It is otherwise in civilised countries, where usually in every story or narrative poem something untouched and original is brought out. Things contrary to reason and experience, on which the stories of our *masnavis* are usually founded, are very rare in them. Their stories are held to be fictional only in name; otherwise all the events and experiences in them are described such as happen to people day and night; and from them they draw such moral, social or political lessons as have a most excellent effect on the morals, society or civilisation of the nation. Unlike the *masnavis* of our country, it is not only the common people and the people of the bazaar who enjoy reading them; they are valued in the society of the learned and the wise as well. Their stories do not always end in success and joy; according to the way of God, they end sometimes in success and sometimes in failure, sometimes in joy and sometimes in sorrow and grief.

In short, since our present poetry rests in every respect, not only in words and phrasing but in thoughts and subjects too, wholly on imitation of the community, and since it is unanimously accepted among us that "the best poetry is the most false" [TN: Arabic *ahsan al-shi'r akdhabuhu*], we must, in the present state of our poetry, give up both reality and passion; for reality and falsehood are incompatible, and passion cannot arise without reality. As for simplicity, in the present state it usually has to be given up perforce; for the ordinary thoughts and subjects on which our poets mostly practise have already been expressed by the ancients in every style and every form of simplicity and clarity. Now, unless some tangle is brought into the manner of expression or some clumsy addition or change into the thought, no novelty can easily be shown in an ordinary subject.

Although some of our poets have given simplicity of expression first place above all things, such as Mir, Dard, Asar and Mushafi, yet because they went very little beyond the thoughts and subjects of the ancients, their *diwans* are mostly full of padding and empty verses. Maulana Azurda of Delhi writes of Mir in his *tazkira* (biographical anthology of poets): "His low is utterly low, and his high utterly high." The reason these men have been accepted as masters of the highest rank is that in their verse the same ordinary thoughts that had been composed continually for several centuries are, for all their utmost simplicity and clarity, often expressed in such original styles as are truly matchless and without equal. In Mir's *diwan* there is a *ghazal* with the rhymes *khak men*, *chak men*, *halak men* ("in the dust", "in the rent", "in destruction"). One day a few friends of Maulana Azurda, among them Momin and Shefta, were gathered at his house. This verse of that same *ghazal* of Mir's was recited:

This time, in madness, perhaps no distance will remain between the rent in the hem and the rent in the collar.

The verse was praised without end, and everyone thought that each should compose this rhyme according to his own skill and thought and show it. They all took pen, inkstand and paper, sat apart and began to think. Just then another friend arrived. He asked the Maulana, "Sir, what are you sitting here thinking about?" The Maulana said, "I am writing an answer to *Qul huwa Allah*." [TN: *Qul huwa Allahu ahad*, "Say: He is God, the One", the opening of Sura 112 of the Quran; that is, he was attempting the inimitable.]

It is plain that tearing the collar, or the hem, or both, in the passion of madness is a most hackneyed and trodden subject that people have kept composing from ancient times. Mir has expressed this chewed-over subject, with the utmost simplicity, in a style so untouched, original and charming that no better style can be imagined. The great beauty of this style is just that it is plain and simple, it is natural, and yet it is wholly novel.

So far, in our view, the three conditions that Milton held necessary for poetry, namely simplicity, reality and passion, have been explained as far as needed. Before Milton our ancients too said something in defining good verse. Al-Asma'i defined it thus: "Its meaning should come to the mind before its words," that is, it should be quick to understand. It is as if al-Asma'i based the excellence of verse on only one of Milton's three conditions, namely simplicity. This definition is inclusive but not exclusive: no good verse can be without simplicity, but it does not follow that every verse that has simplicity must be of the highest rank. In the view of Khalil ibn Ahmad the standard of good verse is that "as soon as it begins, the hearer should know that it will have such-and-such a rhyme." This definition is neither inclusive nor exclusive; a verse may be of low rank and have this quality, and a verse may be of high rank and lack it. The author of the *Iqd al-Farid* writes that the best saying on this matter is that of Zuhair ibn Abi Sulma:

And the finest verse that you can say is a verse of which, when you recite it, it is said: "He spoke the truth."

(That is, the best verse you can say is one of which, when it is recited, people say, "He has spoken the truth.")

In this saying too, as it were, only one of Milton's three conditions, namely reality, is declared necessary. But this one condition alone is not enough. Although verse of the highest rank must have this quality, it does not follow that whatever has this quality must be verse of the highest rank. What verse could be truer than this:

"Your eyes are beneath your eyebrows; your teeth are all in your mouth."

And yet it can hardly be called even verse of the lowest rank.

In our view the finest saying on this matter is that of Ibn Rashiq. He says:

"When it is spoken, it makes all people covet it; and when it is attempted, it baffles the most miraculous."

(That is, when it is recited, everyone thinks, "I too could say the like," but when one sets out to say the like, even those whose speech works miracles are baffled.) The truth is that the grace and beauty with which Ibn Rashiq has defined good verse could not be bettered in imagination; it is as if he has composed, in defining it, verse of the very rank and standing he defines.

## The Difference Between the Statements of Ibn Rashiq and Milton

The fine difference between the statements of Ibn Rashiq and Milton should be carefully understood. Ibn Rashiq's definition implies that the achievement of good verse depends mostly on happy chance, and that the poet's intention and will have little part in it. He does not tell the poet how to write good verse; he tells which of a poet's verses should be thought good.

Milton is different: both sides are present in his statement; from it one learns both how to recognise good verse and the elements of writing it. It does not follow that keeping Milton's three conditions in view will always produce verses as "easy yet inimitable" as those whose standard Ibn Rashiq gave; but it does follow that in the verse of the poet who keeps his conditions in view those lightnings will be seen flashing here and there.

It should be remembered that among all the poets in the world who have been accepted as masters, or who should be, not one will be found whose whole verse, from first to last, stands at the highest level of beauty and grace; for this quality can belong only to the word of God, as He Himself says: "Had it been from other than God, they would have found much inconsistency in it." [TN: Quran 4:82.] The summit of a poet's perfection is that his ordinary verse should be even and in keeping with principle, and that here and there such an astonishing splendour should appear in it as stamps the poet's mastery on the hearts of select and common alike. It is true, however, that his ordinary verses too should, in particular states, affect particular persons almost as much as his special verse affects everyone in every state; and this can be found only in the verse of a poet whose verse is simple and natural. Although the occasion demanded that this discussion be set out at greater length, and what has been said is in our view far less than enough, for the present we must of necessity be content with this much; if time allows, this discussion will be written again with more clarity on some other occasion.
