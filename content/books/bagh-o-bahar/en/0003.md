# Preface (2)

First this sinful wretch, Mir Amman of Delhi, gives an account of himself. From the time of the Emperor Humayun my forefathers served in the train of every emperor, generation after generation, giving their lives in his service; and the emperors in turn cherished them and showed them every appreciation they could wish. They honoured them with grants of *jagir* (land revenue), rank and offices, made them rich and content, and with their own blessed lips called them "hereditary servants of the house" and "nobles of long standing", and these titles were entered in the imperial records. When such a house (a house through which whole households lived) came to the pass it came to (what is plain needs no telling), Suraj Mal the Jat seized the *jagir*, and Ahmad Shah Durrani plundered our home. After suffering such ruin I was exiled from that city (which is my homeland and birthplace, where my navel-string is buried), and that ship of which the emperor was captain was wrecked. I began to sink and rise in the sea of helplessness. To a drowning man even a straw is a great support. For a number of years I took breath in the city of Azimabad [TN: Patna]. Some things went well, some went badly; in the end my feet were knocked from under me there too, and times turned against me. Leaving my wife and children, I took a boat all alone and, drawn by my lot of bread and water, reached Calcutta, the noblest of cities. For a while I was out of work. Then by chance Nawab Dilawar Jang sent for me and appointed me tutor to his younger brother, Mir Muhammad Kazim Khan. I stayed there nearly two years, but I could see no future in it. Then, through Munshi Mir Bahadur Ali, I gained access to Mr John Gilchrist Bahadur (may his fortune last). At last, with the help of my stars, I have laid hold of the hem of so generous a man; it is to be hoped that some better days will come. If not, it is still a blessing that I eat a crust and sleep with my legs stretched out, and that ten people at home, young and old, are provided for and pray for this valuer of merit. May God accept their prayers.

This is the true account of the Urdu language as I have heard it from the mouths of my elders. To the Hindus the city of Delhi has stood through all four ages; their rajas and people lived there from ancient times and spoke their own *bhakha* (the local speech). For a thousand years the Muslims have ruled. Sultan Mahmud of Ghazni came; then the Ghori and Lodi kings reigned. Because of these comings and goings, Hindu and Muslim speech mingled with each other somewhat. At last Amir Timur (in whose house the name of empire still continues) took Hindustan. With his coming and staying, the bazaar of the army entered the city, and that is why the city's bazaar was called *Urdu* (the camp). Then the Emperor Humayun, harassed by the Pathans, went to Persia. At length he came back from there and punished the remaining Pathans, so that no troublemaker was left to stir up strife and disorder.

When the Emperor Akbar sat on the throne, peoples from the lands on every side, hearing of the generosity and bounty of that peerless dynasty, came and gathered at his court. But each one's speech and dialect was different. Because they lived together and bought and sold, traded and talked with one another, the Urdu tongue came into being. When His Majesty Shah Jahan, Lord of the Fortunate Conjunction, built the blessed Fort, the Jama Masjid and the city wall, had jewels set in the Peacock Throne and had a tent as vast as a mass of clouds set up on poles and stretched with ropes, and when Nawab Ali Mardan Khan brought the canal, then the Emperor, pleased, held a great festival and made the city his capital. From that time it has been known as Shahjahanabad (although Delhi is separate: that is called the old city and this the new), and he gave its bazaar the title of *Urdu-e-Mualla* (the exalted camp).
