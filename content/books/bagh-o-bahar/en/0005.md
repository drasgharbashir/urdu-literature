# The Beginning of the Tale

Now I begin the tale. Lend me your ears a little, listen, and judge fairly. In *The Adventures of the Four Dervishes* it is written thus, and the teller has told it so: in former times, in the land of Rum [TN: the Ottoman empire], there was an emperor in whom the justice of Nushirvan and the generosity of Hatim were joined. His name was Azad Bakht, and the city of Constantinople (which they call Istanbul) was his capital. In his time the people prospered, the treasury was full, the army well provided for, and the poor and humble lived at ease; they passed their days in such comfort and happiness that in every house each day was *Id* (a festival) and each night *Shab-e-Barat* (the night of lamps). He had so destroyed all the thieves and pilferers, pickpockets, dawn prowlers, snatchers and swindlers that no name or trace of them was left in all his land. All night the doors of houses stayed unbarred and the shops in the bazaar stayed open. Travellers went through forest and plain tossing gold in their hands, and no one asked them, "Have you teeth in your mouth? Where are you going?"

Thousands of cities lay under this emperor's rule, and many sultans paid him tribute. Yet over so great an empire he never let his heart forget God's remembrance and worship for one moment. Every comfort of the world that one could wish for was his, but he was denied a son, the fruit of life, which did not grow in the garden of his fate. For this reason he was often troubled. After each of the five daily prayers he would say to his Bountiful Lord: "O God, in Your kindness You have given this helpless one everything, but You have not given a lamp to this one dark house. This longing alone remains in my heart: that I have no one to carry on my name or pour water for my soul [TN: to offer the rites for the dead], and yet everything is there in Your hidden treasury. Give me one living son, so that my name and the mark of this empire may endure." In this hope the emperor reached the age of forty. One day he had said his prayers in the Hall of Mirrors and was reciting his litany when suddenly, glancing at a mirror, he saw a white hair in his moustache, shining like a thread of silver wire. At this sight the emperor's eyes filled with tears and he heaved a cold sigh. Then he thought to himself: "Alas! You have wasted so many years for nothing, and in your greed for this world you have turned a whole world upside down. Of what use will all the land you have taken be to you now? In the end someone else will squander all this wealth and goods. The summons of death has already come to you. Even if you live a few days more, your body's strength will fail.

"From this it is clear that it is not written in my fate that an heir to the canopy and throne should be born. One day I must die and leave everything behind. Better that I leave it myself, and spend what is left of my life in the remembrance of my Creator." Having settled this in his heart, he went into the inner garden, dismissed all the courtiers and ordered: "From today let no one come to me. Let them all attend the Hall of Public Audience and be diligent in their duties." With this he went and sat in a room by himself, spread his prayer mat and became absorbed in worship. He had nothing to do but weep and sigh. In this way Emperor Azad Bakht spent many days. At sunset, when breaking his fast, he would eat one dried date and drink three sips of water, and all day and night he lay on his prayer mat. Word of this spread outside, and little by little the whole country learned that the emperor had withdrawn his hand from the empire and taken to seclusion. On every side enemies and rebels raised their heads and stepped beyond their bounds; whoever wished seized land and set about rebellion. Wherever there were governors, great disorder fell upon their authority. From every province petitions about misrule reached the court. All the nobles of the court gathered and began to take counsel together.
