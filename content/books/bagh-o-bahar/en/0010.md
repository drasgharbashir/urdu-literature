# The First Dervish's Tale

The first dervish sat up on his knees and began to tell the story of his wanderings in this way: "O God, Lord of worship! Turn this way a little, and hear the story of this ruined wretch!

Lend me your ear a little and hear my story;
hear how heaven turned me upside down.

All the hardship that has come upon me,
I will tell it, listen to it from beginning to end.

"Friends, my birthplace and the homeland of my forefathers is the country of Yemen. The father of this humble one was a great merchant named Khwaja Ahmad, the chief of merchants. In his time no banker or trader was his equal. In most cities he had warehouses and agents appointed to buy and sell, and lakhs of rupees in cash and goods from every land were in his house. Two children were born to him: one is this very *faqir*, who sits before you, my masters, in a dervish's *kafni* and *seli* (the corded necklace of a dervish), and speaks; the other a sister, whom my father, while he still lived, married to the son of another merchant of the city. She lived with her husband's family. In short, in a house with so much wealth and only one son, there was no end to the petting and indulgence. I was brought up in my parents' shadow with great fondness and pampering, and began to learn reading and writing, the arts and skills of soldiering, and the account books and day-books of trade. Fourteen years passed in great happiness and freedom from care; no thought of the world ever entered my mind. Then suddenly, in a single year, both my parents died by the decree of God.

"I felt a grief so strange that I cannot describe it. All at once I was an orphan, with no elder left over my head. Because of this sudden calamity I wept day and night, and gave up eating and drinking. Somehow or other forty days passed. At the *chehlum* (the fortieth-day rites for the dead) my own people and strangers, young and old, gathered. When the prayers for the dead were over, they all tied my father's turban on my head and admonished me: 'In this world everyone's parents have died, and one day we too must die. So be patient. Look after your house. Now you are the head in your father's place; be careful in your business and dealings.' Having comforted me, they took their leave. All the agents, men of business and servants came and presented themselves, offered gifts and said, 'Please look over the warehouse of cash and goods with your own blessed eyes.' When my eyes suddenly fell on that boundless wealth, they opened wide. I ordered the reception hall to be made ready. The carpet-men spread carpets and furnishings and hung fine ceilings, curtains and bamboo blinds, and I took on handsome, smart-looking attendants and servants. From the warehouse I had glittering robes made. I sat on a cushioned seat leaning on a bolster. Then men of the same kind, rogues and jesters, spongers who eat and drink at others' cost, lying flatterers, came and made friends and became my companions. I kept company with them all twenty-four hours. They talked nonsense and idle chatter about everything under the sun, this and that, and would say, 'In the prime of youth like this, have wine of *ketki* (screw pine) or rose distilled, send for lovely sweethearts, drink with them and enjoy yourself.'

"In short, man's devil is man. With hearing such talk every moment my own mind went astray too. Wine, dancing and gambling began. Then it came to this: I forgot trade, and took to the business of watching shows and giving presents. When my own servants and companions saw this heedlessness, each put aside whatever came into his hands, as if they had started a looting. I had no idea how much money was being spent, where it came from or where it went. Free goods make a pitiless heart. Against such spendthrift ways even the treasure of Qarun would not have lasted. In a few years it suddenly came to this: only a cap and a loincloth were left. The friends and acquaintances who had shared every bite with me, and who in all their talk would offer a spoonful of their blood for me, vanished like camphor. Indeed, if I happened to meet them on the road, they would look away and turn their faces aside. And the servants and attendants, the carriage-drivers and shield-bearers, the bearers of my personal arms and the *sabit-khani* attendants [TN: a class of household servant; the exact duty is uncertain], all left me and took themselves off. No one was left to ask, 'What has become of you?' I had no companion but grief and regret.
