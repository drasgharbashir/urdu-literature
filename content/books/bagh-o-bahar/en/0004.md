# Preface (3)

From the time of Amir Timur to the reign of Muhammad Shah, indeed up to the time of Ahmad Shah and Alamgir the Second, the empire passed down unbroken from generation to generation, and in the end the Urdu language was polished and polished until it became so refined that the speech of no other city can rival it. But it needs a fair judge of merit to weigh it. And so now, after a long time, God has brought forth so wise and discerning a man as Mr John Gilchrist, who by his knowledge and skill, his research and labour, has written books of grammar. Because of this, the language of Hindustan has spread through many lands and gained fresh lustre. Otherwise no one thinks ill of his own turban, speech or gait. If you ask a villager, he will find fault with the townsman and think himself the best of all. Well, the wise know best. [TN: The last sentence is in Persian.]

When Ahmad Shah Abdali came from Kabul and had the city plundered, Shah Alam was away in the east. The country was left without heir or master, and the city without a head. It is true: the city's splendour came from the good fortune of the empire. Ruin fell on it all at once. Its nobles scattered, I here and you there, each going wherever his horns would fit. In whatever land they reached, their speech changed through living among the people there. And there are many who went to Delhi for some reason and stayed five or ten years; how well can they speak? They are bound to slip somewhere. But the man who bore all its calamities and stayed on as a stone of Delhi's streets, whose family lived in that city for five or ten generations, who for a long time attended the courts of nobles, the fairs and festivals, the *urs* (saints' anniversaries) and *chhariyan* (processions of banners at saints' shrines), its sights and shows, and walked its lanes, and who after leaving it kept a careful watch on his speech: his way of speaking is certainly correct. This humble one too has come this far, touring every city and seeing its sights.
