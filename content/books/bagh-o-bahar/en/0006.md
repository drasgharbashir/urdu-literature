# The Beginning of the Tale (2)

At last it was decided: "The Nawab Vizier is wise and sensible, the emperor's confidant and trusted counsellor, and the highest in rank of us all. Let us go to him and see what he thinks fit to advise." All the chief nobles came to the vizier and said, "Such is the emperor's state, and such is the condition of the country. If this neglect goes on a little longer, the land won with so much labour will be lost for nothing, and then it will be very hard to win back." The vizier was an old and faithful servant of long standing and a man of sense; his name too was Khiradmand (Wise), and it fitted him. He said, "Although the emperor has forbidden anyone to come before him, come along, I will go too. God grant the emperor is pleased to call me into his presence." With this he brought them all as far as the Hall of Public Audience, left them there and went himself into the Hall of Private Audience, and sent word to the emperor by a palace servant: "This old slave is here. For many days he has not seen your world-adorning beauty. He hopes to see you just once and kiss your feet, so that his mind may be at rest." The emperor heard the vizier's request. Since he knew his long service, his loyalty, his good counsel and his devotion, and usually followed his advice, after some hesitation he said, "Call Khiradmand." At last, when leave was given, the vizier came into his presence, made his bow and stood with folded hands.

He saw that the emperor was in a strange state: he was weeping bitterly, rings had formed round his eyes from wasting, and his face had turned yellow.

Khiradmand could bear it no longer; he ran forward without thinking and fell at his feet. The emperor raised his head with his hand and said, "There, you have seen me. Is your mind at rest? Now go, don't trouble me further; you govern the kingdom." Hearing this, Khiradmand wept aloud and said, "By your bounty and through your well-being your slave will always enjoy all the power he needs. But your majesty's sudden seclusion has thrown the whole country into turmoil, and the end of it will not be good. What is this notion that has entered your blessed mind? It would be better if you made this hereditary servant of the house privy to the secret, so that he may set before you whatever his poor understanding can suggest. You have bestowed these honours on your slaves for this very day, so that the emperor may live in comfort and ease while those who have eaten his salt see to the governing of the country. God forbid, but if some care has come upon your exalted mind, on what day will the servants of the empire be of use?" The emperor said, "You speak truly, but the care that is in my heart is beyond any remedy. Listen, Khiradmand. My whole life has gone in the headache of conquering lands. Now I have reached this age, and ahead only death remains, and its message has come too, for my black hairs are turning white. As the saying goes: you have slept all night; will you not wake even at dawn? Till now not one son has been born to me to set my mind at rest; that is why my heart is so heavy, and I have given everything up. Let whoever wishes take the land or take the wealth; I want nothing to do with it. Indeed, any day now I mean to leave everything, go out into the forests and mountains and show my face to no one, and so pass these few days of life. If some place pleases me, I will sit there and worship my Lord. Perhaps my end will be good. As for the world, I have seen enough of it and found no joy in it." Having said this much, and heaving a sigh, the emperor fell silent.
