# The Beginning of the Tale (4)

When the day was a watch old, the curtain was suddenly raised and the emperor came out and took his seat on the blessed throne. In the drum-house the music of rejoicing began to play. Everyone presented offerings of congratulation, and in the hall of audience they made their salutations and obeisances. Each was honoured according to his worth and rank. Everyone's heart was filled with joy and relief. At noon the emperor rose, went into the inner palace, ate his meal and rested in his bedchamber. From that day the emperor made it his rule to hold court every morning, to spend the afternoon reading or reciting blessings and litanies, and to repent and ask forgiveness at God's court and pray for his heart's desire.

One day he found it written in a book that if a man is afflicted with a grief or care that no remedy can cure, he should leave it to fate and turn himself to the graveyard; he should offer the dead the blessings due to them for the sake of the Prophet's soul, think of himself as nothing at all, keep his heart awake against this worldly heedlessness, weep at the lesson he sees there, and look at God's power: "What great lords of lands and treasures were born on this earth before me! Yet the heavens turned them all round in their revolving and mixed them with the dust." There is a saying:

Seeing the millstones turning, Kabir began to weep:
between the two stones nothing has come out whole.

"Look now: nothing is left of them but a heap of earth. They have left all the wealth of the world, home and family, children, friends, servants, elephants and horses, and lie there alone. None of it was of any use to them; indeed now no one even knows their names or who they were. And no one knows what happened to them inside the grave, whether worms and insects, ants and snakes devoured them, or what befell them and how they fared with God." If a man turns these things over in his heart and knows the whole world to be a passing show, the bud of his heart will always be in bloom and will never wither in any state. When the emperor read this counsel in the book, he remembered what the wise vizier had said and found the two in agreement. He longed to act on it, but thought: "To go about on horseback with a crowd behind me, as kings do, is not fitting. It is better that I change my clothes and go alone at night to the tombs, or to wait on some man of God who lives in seclusion, and keep vigil through the night. Perhaps through these men I may gain my wish in this world and salvation in the next."

Having resolved this in his heart, one night he put on coarse, plain clothes, took some rupees and gold coins, slipped quietly out of the fort and took the road to the open plain. Walking on, he came to a graveyard and was reciting blessings with complete sincerity. A fierce wind was blowing at that time; indeed it should be called a storm. Suddenly the emperor saw far off something like a flame, shining like the morning star. He thought to himself, "In this storm and darkness this light is not without some secret. Is it a magic trick? For if you sprinkle alum and sulphur round the wick of a lamp, however hard the wind blows the lamp will not go out. Or is it the lamp of some saint that burns there? Whatever it may be, I must go and see. Perhaps from the light of this candle the lamp of my house too will be lit, and my heart's wish be granted." With this intention he went towards it. When he came near, he saw four destitute *faqirs*, with *kafnis* (the shroud-like shirts of dervishes) round their necks and their heads on their knees, sitting silent and lost to the world. Their state was like that of a traveller who, parted from his own country and people, is caught in the grief of helplessness and poverty and stands bewildered. In just this way the poor men sat as still as figures painted on a wall. A lamp set on a stone was flickering there; the wind never touched it, as if the sky had become its glass shade, so that it burned without danger.
