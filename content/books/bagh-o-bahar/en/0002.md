# Preface

Glory be to God! What a Maker He is, who from one handful of dust has brought forth so many forms and figures of clay! Though there are only two colours, one fair and one dark, and He has given everyone the same nose, ears, hands and feet, still He has made faces of every kind, each different from the next, so that the bearing of one never matches the build of another; among millions of creatures you can pick out anyone you like. The sky is one bubble on the ocean of His oneness, and the earth a sugar-drop dissolving in water; and yet, wonder of wonders, the ocean heaves with a thousand waves and cannot bend a hair of it. Before One with such power and might, man's tongue is as good as dumb when it comes to His praise. What can one say? It is better to keep silent about a matter on which one dare not open one's mouth.

He whose furnishings stretch from the highest heaven to the earth below:
if I wished to write His praise, how would it be possible?

When the Prophet himself has said, "I have not known Him,"
then whoever claims to know Him is a great fool.

Day and night the sun and moon go round, gazing at His handiwork,
yet each of them stands like an astonished, staring eye.

He has no second and no rival, nor ever will:
all godhead befits One so single.

But this much I know: He is the Creator and the Provider,
and in every way His kindness and favour are upon me.

And blessings on His Friend, for whose sake He created the earth and the heavens and to whom He gave the rank of prophethood.

The pure body of Mustafa [TN: the Chosen One, a title of the Prophet Muhammad] is a light of God;
that is why, as everyone knows, his form cast no shadow.

Where would I find the courage to write his praise?
Yet among poets this too is the rule and the custom.

And blessings and peace upon his family, who are the twelve Imams.

Having finished here the praise of God and of Ahmad,
I now begin the work that I have in hand.

O God, for the sake of Your Prophet's family,
make this speech of mine pleasing to high and low alike.

The occasion of this work is this. In the year 1215 of the Hijra, 1801 of the Christian era, corresponding to the Fasli year 1207, in the time of the noblest of nobles, the Marquess Wellesley, Governor General, Lord Mornington (in whose praise reason is bewildered and understanding lost; God has gathered in his person every quality a ruler should have; in short, it was this country's good luck that such a ruler arrived, by the blessing of whose coming a whole world has found rest; no one dares to use force against anyone; the lion and the goat drink at the same ghat; all the poor and humble bless him and live), the pursuit of learning spread. The distinguished English gentlemen wished to become acquainted with the Urdu language, so that they could converse with the people of Hindustan and carry out the business of the country with full understanding. For this purpose a number of books were composed in that very year at their request.

To the learned who speak the language of Hindustan I humbly submit that this tale of the four dervishes was first told by Amir Khusrau of Delhi on this occasion: Hazrat Nizamuddin Auliya, Zari Zar Bakhsh (who was his spiritual guide, and whose shrine is in Delhi, three *kos* (a measure of about two miles) from the Fort, outside the Lal Darwaza, beyond the Matia Darwaza, near the Lal Bangla), fell ill. Then, to amuse his master's mind, Amir Khusrau would tell this tale every day and stay in attendance on him through his illness. In a few days God granted him recovery. On the day of his bath of health he gave this blessing: whoever hears this tale will, by the grace of God, stay in good health. Ever since then this tale has been current in Persian.

Now my patron and benefactor, a gentleman of great kindness and a true valuer of men of good family, Mr John Gilchrist (may his good fortune increase for as long as the Ganges and Jumna flow), was kind enough to tell me: "Translate this tale into pure Hindustani speech, as the people of Urdu, Hindus and Muslims, women and men, boys and girls, high and low, talk among themselves." In obedience to his order I too began to write in that very idiom, just as a person talks.
