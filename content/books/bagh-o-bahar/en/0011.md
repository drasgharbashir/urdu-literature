# The First Dervish's Tale (2)

"Now I could not get even a farthing's worth of roasted grain to chew and wash down with water. I went hungry two or three days running, and could bear the hunger no longer. In the end I drew the veil of shamelessness over my face and resolved to go to my sister. But shame kept rising in my heart, because after my father's death I had shown my sister no kindness, had not written her so much as a letter; indeed, the one or two letters of condolence and longing she wrote, I had not even answered, lost in my sleep of heedlessness. Because of this shame I did not want to go, but I could see no refuge besides that house. Somehow, on foot and empty-handed, stumbling and falling, I covered those many stages with a thousand hardships, reached my sister's city and came to her house. When my mother's daughter saw me in this state she took my troubles upon herself, embraced me and wept a great deal. She passed oil, black lentils and black coins over me as an offering to turn away ill luck. She said, 'Although my heart is very glad to see you, brother, what has become of you?' I could give her no answer; my eyes brimmed with tears and I stayed silent. My sister quickly had a fine suit of clothes made and sent me to the bath. I bathed and washed and put on those clothes. She set aside a very fine, well-furnished house near her own for me to live in. In the morning she would send for sherbet and sweet almond cakes, *halwa sohan* and pistachio sweets for breakfast; in the afternoon fresh and dried fruits; and at both meals, day and night, delicious *pulao*, bread, *qaliya* and kebabs and other dainties, and she would see me eat them in front of her before she left. She cared for me in every way. After such suffering I found this comfort, and gave God thanks upon thanks at His court. Several months passed in such ease that I never set foot outside that retreat.

"One day that sister, who looked after me as a mother would, said, 'Brother, you are the apple of my eye and the only token left of our dead mother and father. Your coming has cooled my heart; whenever I see you I am delighted. You have made me happy. But God made men to earn a living; it is not right for them to sit at home. People taunt a man who lounges idle at home, and especially the people of this city, high and low, will say of your staying here without reason, "He has squandered his father's worldly wealth and come to live on his brother-in-law's crumbs." That is very shameful, a laughing-stock for me and for you, and a cause of disgrace to our parents' name. Otherwise I would make shoes from my own skin for you to wear, and keep you inside my heart. My advice now is that you set out on a journey. God willing, your days will turn, and in place of this bewilderment and poverty you will find peace of mind and happiness.' Hearing this, I too felt my honour stirred and approved her counsel. I answered, 'Very well; now you are in my mother's place, I will do whatever you say.' When she saw that I agreed, she went into the house and had her slave-girls of good family bring fifty bags of gold coins and set them before me, and said, 'A caravan of merchants is going to Damascus. Buy merchandise with this money.

"'Hand it over to an honest merchant, get a binding deed written, and you yourself set out for Damascus too. When you have arrived there safely, take your goods back with the profit after settling accounts, or sell them yourself.' I took the cash to the bazaar, bought goods for trade and handed them over to a great merchant, and made sure of everything in writing. That merchant took ship and set off by sea. I made ready to travel by land. When I was about to take leave, my sister presented me with a rich robe of honour and a horse with jewelled harness, filled a special box with sweets and fried delicacies and hung it from the saddle, and had a water bottle tied to the saddle-straps. She bound the coin of the *Imam Zamin* (a coin tied to a traveller's arm, entrusting him to the protection of the Imam) on my arm, put a mark of curd on my forehead, swallowed her tears and said, "Go then! I have entrusted you to God. You are showing me your back as you go; show me your face again just as soon.' I recited the *fatiha* (the opening prayer of the Quran) for good fortune and said, 'May God protect you too.' I accepted her charge. Leaving there I mounted my horse and, putting my trust in God, made two stages in one and came near Damascus.
