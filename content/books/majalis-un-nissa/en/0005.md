# The Fourth Majlis: Running a Household

## The Fourth Majlis

The rest of Zubaida Khatun's account

When I had learned sewing and cooking thoroughly, and Amma-jan saw that there was no task I would now get stuck on; when she had had me cook each dish twenty or twenty-five times over and watched; when in needlework, running stitch, backstitch, hemming, trimming and cutting out, she had satisfied herself about me in every point; when, going further, she had had me do net-work, embroidery and gold lace and stamped work again and again; then one day, at the usual hour, she called me, talked of other things for a while, and then began: "Listen, Amma [1]! Whatever skill a person learns, she learns either gladly or because she must; and whatever work she does, she does either from her own liking or under someone's pressure. But there is this difference: what comes from the heart's own eagerness never wearies the mind, and one enjoys it; while work that suddenly falls on one's head all at once feels very heavy [2].

Today, in your parents' house, God has given you servants and everything; if you like, you need not stir to pour yourself a glass of water. By God's grace every task can be done by a word from your tongue. Eat what you like, wear what you like, sleep when you please, rise when you please; no one stands in your way. But, my dear, you will not live in your parents' house for ever. In a few days you will be in one place and we in another. To begin with, our life is only a matter of days: dead today, and tomorrow is another day; and once you are married, even if we go on living, what use will we be to you? And who knows whether you will go to a poor house or a rich one? And even if you find a rich house, you will not be able to sit there for one moment as carefree as this.

If your mother-in-law and sisters-in-law are there, how long will that last? A sister-in-law will be married off some day, and then where will she be and where will you? As for the mother-in-law, first, mother-in-law and daughter-in-law have never yet got on [3] under one roof anywhere; and even if they did, for how long? No one's parents or parents-in-law have lived for ever, nor will they. As for the husband, he is a man; what has he to do with the business of the house? In short, sooner or later the whole burden will fall on you alone.

Whether a girl is slovenly [4] or capable [5], in her parents' house everything passes; but God forbid that anyone's daughter should go to her in-laws and be called slovenly. If the mother-in-law and sisters-in-law are capable women, they will laugh at everything she says, jeer at everything she does, and find fault with [6] her mother's family; and if they turn to enmity [7], they will speak ill of her to her husband until she falls from his heart. And if they too are as foolish and silly as she is, in four days a house worth a lakh will be dust.

What I mean by all this is that now, by God's grace, you are grown up; whenever you are free from reading and writing, you should manage the house. There is no longer any need for you to sit for hours with the Mughlani or to do all the kitchen work with your own hands. I am sure that now no sewing or cooking, however difficult, will get the better of you. The men will look after the outside expenses, but keep a daily account of the two hundred rupees a month that are spent in the house. There is not much trouble in it, because I have never bought on credit [8]: I put the money down on the nail [9] and had the thing brought. In my view, when one has the money in hand [10], to buy goods on credit [11] is great foolishness. Besides, your Abba too cannot bear debts in the bazaar. And when the month is over, settle the whole month's account and read it out to me.

Look, there isn't any of the servants sitting here, is there?" I said, "No, no one. Please go on."

She said, "These wretched women make a great deal on the side out of the goods that come from the bazaar every day. Apart from the customary commission, four annas in every rupee are theirs by birthright. As long as my hands and feet worked and my mind was steady, I never let their tricks succeed. But for a year now I have become a complete invalid; I cannot get about, I cannot remember anything, I say one thing and something else comes out. Bickering [12] every day wears me out. They do whatever they like. If they swallow eight annas in a rupee, there is no one to ask; and if they lose the whole rupee in a muddle [13], there is no one to call them to account.

My girl, now you must take them in hand. The remedy is this: the water-carrier's wife, the sweeper woman, the greengrocer woman, the grain-grinder, the gardener's wife, the midwife and the other women who go about outside and come and go in the house know the price of everything very well; ask them the bazaar rate now and then. Whenever you find a difference in anything, charge it to the one who brought it.

And make this the rule for buying: dry goods that are in no danger of spoiling, such as salt, oil, ghee, spices, jaggery, sugar, coarse sugar, areca nut, *zarda* (scented tobacco for *paan*), cardamom, catechu, lime and so on, buy whatever is cheap in its season by the season, and the rest by the month. As for vegetables, meat, curds, milk and so on, have them brought day by day. But it is not good always to send the same person for things. By God's grace there are several women servants in the house, and besides [14] two men live at the gate; send this one sometimes and that one sometimes. That keeps the one who buys a little afraid.

As for grain, you will not have to buy it at all. At the start of each harvest the wheat, gram, millet, sorghum, lentils and maize God gives come in, enough for the whole year's use. Besides this, every morning and evening go yourself to the storeroom [15] and have the flour, lentils, ghee, rice and sugar weighed out before your eyes, as much as you see is needed. Fix an estimate of what the house uses every day, and give out that much daily; and if on some day something new is cooked, or a dinner is to be sent to someone, or a guest arrives, increase the stores by that much.

And sometimes go and sit in the kitchen yourself for a little while; if nothing else, taste the salt, peel a vegetable, roast the spices. There are several advantages in this: the cook will be careful and will not work carelessly; the food will be well cooked; and you will keep up the habit too.

Besides this, for grinding, have two maunds ten seers of wheat cleaned and winnowed every day and filled into separate jars. When the grinding woman comes, weigh the grain and give it to her, weigh the flour and take it from her yourself, and pay her for her grinding day by day. And have the grain for the horses and oxen weighed before you and sent out.

When the food is cooked and ready and your Abba comes into the house from the *diwan-khana* (the men's reception room), sit by the stove and have the food served out before your eyes. First, if there is a guest outside, send him food from the family's own [16] dishes; then have your Abba's bread-cloth and the plates of lentils and curry set on a tray and bring them in yourself. First spread the cloth with your own hands, then take the basin and ewer and pour water for him to wash his hands. Once he has sat down to eat, leave the maid to pour his water and go to the kitchen yourself. If there is a guest in the house who keeps *purdah*, take food for her and for your *ustani-ji* to the other house, and all of you sit and eat together; and if there is no guest, first eat with *ustani-ji* yourself, then from the ordinary [17] food send the outside servants their share outside and give the inside servants theirs inside.

Have so much bread made that after the water-carrier and the sweeper have had theirs, one person's meal is left over; guests often have children with them, and who knows when they may ask for food. And always keep a little minced meat ready; a guest may come at an odd hour, and then you will only have to worry about bread.

When everyone has eaten, open the *paandaan* (betel box) and make up the *gilauris* (folded betel leaves); those to be sent out, put in the *khasdan* (covered betel salver) and send out, and give those meant for the house to the people in the house. Wherever you send a plate of lentils or curry, always cover it with a lid, and send food arranged on a tray. The bread-cloths should always be clean and the vessels freshly tinned. Make the *gilauris* you send out plain, and put the *zarda* separately in the *khasdan*. Put less lime in the *paan* than seems right.

When this is done, have the maid scour all the pots, count them and put them away in the storeroom. Have the vessels tinned every eighth day, or at least every fifteenth. Have the bottoms of the cooking pots coated every second or third day. Have the stoves plastered every tenth or fifteenth day. Every six months [18] have the spice-grinding stone and the hand mill roughened.

And above all, take care of the water. The water jars and pitchers should not be left uncovered; the lids and the dippers for drawing water should always be on them. Water, whether for drinking or washing, should be kept where the sun does not reach. Pitchers of stale water should be kept separate from fresh; tell the water-carrier firmly that fresh water must never be mixed with stale or stale with fresh. Scores of diseases come from just such muddling of water.

Fix an estimate of oil: so much burns in the house, so much at the gate and in the *diwan-khana*; and see that no lamp burns needlessly, and where each one burns and for how long. Once you know this, rise every evening and weigh out that much and hand it to the servant.

When you give clothes to the washerwoman, count them as you give them, and count them as you take them back, and if you cannot remember, write it down. When dirty clothes are taken off, have the men's and women's tied up separately and put away; the washerwoman comes and takes them. And show her each garment as you hand it over, so that she cannot bring something back torn and say, 'It was torn when it came to me.' Two or four times a year put the clothes out in the sun; and above all [19] the woollens and silks need even more care.

When winter is coming, take out the winter clothes and see which are still good and which are fit to wear. Any garment you find will be good as new with fresh cotton wadding, or with unpicking and dyeing, or with a new border, edging, lining or facing, have it put right; leave what has nothing wrong with it as it is; and put aside what is badly torn and worn to give to a maid, a lady's-maid or some other servant. And whatever new cloth you see is needed, write it down on a list, so that you don't have to send to the bazaar again and again. Any old cotton wadding that comes out, have it made into *dohars* (light quilts) or rugs, or exchange it for new, and buy whatever new cotton is needed.

Have four or five braziers brought from the potter's wife. Always tell the cook firmly to put out the embers of all the wood burnt in the kitchen every day and to put them in the lidded vessel in the kitchen; if that goes on, you will not have to buy charcoal from the bazaar for the braziers in winter.

In the same way, as the hot weather comes, to have the house whitewashed, have fans hung in the ceilings, put the garden in order, change the water jars and see to the summer clothes; and before the rains, to have the house repaired, earth laid on the mud roofs and the cracks in the brick roofs sealed, the drains opened and the spouts cleaned: all these things must be remembered.

The house should be kept as bright and clean as possible. The water in the washbasin should be thrown out morning and evening. The ablution pots should never leave the prayer platform. When the floor-cloth of the house gets dirty it should be changed at once. Every morning, as soon as you rise, have the maid sweep the floor-cloth inside and the sweeper woman the whole house, before your eyes. The divan, stool, low seat, water-jar stand, a bed's legs, side-rail or head-rail: whatever breaks, send it off at once and have it mended.

Except in the rains, have the foot-cords [20] of the beds tightened every eighth or tenth day. Tell the cook firmly to clear the ash from the stoves every day and pile it in one place, and tell the sweeper woman to always carry it away in a basket with the rubbish. Each night sit down and cut up as much areca nut as is used in a day, some yourself and some by the nurses; don't let it come to cutting areca nut when it is needed. Sometimes a call for fifty *gilauris* at once comes from outside; if the nut is not cut then, it is a great trouble. The *paandaan* should be cleaned by the maid every morning as soon as you get up. Some slovens keep their *paandaan* so that it is disgusting [21] to look at.

Of the basket of fruit that comes from the garden every day, send a little to the family and some to the neighbours. And whatever comes from the villages, mangoes, maize cobs, milk, cane juice, share it out in the same way; if any is left over, keep what is needed for the house and give the rest to the servants.

Whatever guest comes to our house should be made welcome as far as possible; let her want for nothing in eating, drinking, sleeping, sitting, *zarda* or *paan*. If someone has a child with her, take it in your lap sometimes, sometimes send to the bazaar for a treat for it, and keep asking at all hours whether it wants to eat. Whenever something new is cooked, always send a little to the neighbours as well. Never let the beggars who come to the door to ask go away empty-handed.

Apart from the outside women who come every day, whenever a new woman comes into the house, be a little on your guard with her; don't sit down to chat with her familiarly. Becoming intimate with anyone and everyone at once is counted a fault. In the same way it is not good to whisper with this one and that one for no reason; it makes others suspect all sorts of things. Of course, if something really is fit to be kept secret, there is no harm in it.

If you can't find time every day, at least every second or third day wash your hair with *amla* or oil-cake and bathe. Clothes should be kept as clean as possible.

In short, apart from the seven or eight hours for sleep, don't sit idle for a single hour or moment, and count every instant and every second dearer than life.

My girl, you must be saying to yourself, 'What a misfortune [22] Amma has thrown me into; she has put a whole mountain on my head at once!' No, my dear, never think so. You will know the value of work when you learn the ups and downs of the world.

Listen: a person should first think to herself, What has God Almighty made me for? What things will be of use to me in the next world? And how will my honour and good name be kept in this one? As for what some people say, that one should spend one's life in pleasure and ease however one can, in the first place it is a mere fancy. A saint has said that those who seek comfort are seeking something that was never created in this world. And whatever little comfort there is here does not depend on wealth, or rank and office, or laughing and talking, or good food and fine clothes.

However hard and laborious the work a person does, once she is used to it after a few days, no work seems as easy to her, and she comes to take such pleasure in labour that if she sits idle for a moment she grows restless. The grinding women who sit at the hand mill all day; the water women who carry two pots at a time on their heads from the well; the bangle-makers who strain their eyes sitting all day; the milkmen's wives who pat out dung-cakes all day; and the peasants' wives who spend four watches at a stretch ploughing and threshing in the heat of Jeth and Baisakh [TN: the hottest months, May to June]: if you look into their hearts you will find them happier and more cheerful than great ranis and begums.

Your Abba used to tell of a boy of fourteen or fifteen who was imprisoned for ten years for some offence. When he had served his sentence, they brought him before the magistrate to be released. He said, 'If the government would do me a kindness, let it send me back to the gaol. Just as I used to be afraid of going into the gaol, now leaving it feels like a calamity; and if the government won't send me, I will steal again to be locked up.' The magistrate took him into service and sent him back to the gaol, and he spent the rest of his life there.

In the same way he used to say: 'When I went to the Punjab, the heat in those parts was very fierce. One day, as I travelled, noon came on the road itself, and I stopped in the shade of a tree. A little later a *pinnace* (a covered litter) came that way, with eight bearers, a water-carrier and two attendants; screens of *khas* (fragrant vetiver root) hung on both sides of it, and the water-carrier kept sprinkling water as he came. A little further on were some other shady trees, and there it stopped. Next to them were a well and a sweet-seller's shop. The bearers set the litter down with a thump [23], went to the well themselves and washed their faces and hands, bought *puris* from the sweet-seller's shop and sat down to eat in the full sun; and when they had eaten and drunk they began to play the tambourine and sing.

Meanwhile, when I looked over there, the gentleman was lying in the litter groaning Oh! and Ah! and having the water-carrier sprinkle water again and again. I asked his attendants whether he was ill. They said, Sir, he is not ill at all; he is suffering from the heat. I said to myself, Glory be to God! What a wonder of God's this is! This murderous sun, this burning ground, these bare feet, the litter carried on their shoulders, and God knows whether they have brought it eight *kos* or ten; and yet the bearers are sitting there at their ease playing the tambourine and singing, and you could not tell from their faces that they had walked from anywhere; and here is this man sitting in the litter, not moving a hand, not moving a foot, not a trace of sun inside, *khas* screens all round, a cool breeze and a sweet earthy scent coming in, and still he is dying of the heat!

From that day I was certain that from the rich man to the poor and from the king to the beggar, everyone's lot in joy and sorrow is the same; indeed, the hard-working labourer's heart is happier than the rich man's.'

And why look so far? It was only yesterday that your uncle, when he was *sarishtadar* (head clerk) at the court, had grown so used to the court's work that on holidays his spirits were always low. One day I said, 'Brother, why is it that on holidays you are always so listless?' He said, 'I don't know, madam; at work I am happy, and on a day when there is no work it is hard to get through till evening.'

In short, dear girl, understand that peace and comfort are such a thing that whatever work a person takes up, she will come to be happy in it.

Now the question remains, what work should you take up? Well, my dear: in the next world nothing but good deeds will be of any use; and what keeps your honour and good name in this world, keeps your parents and parents-in-law happy and your husband devoted, stops the tongues of your sisters-in-law and the wives of his brothers, wins praise in the family, the neighbours' blessings and the servants' goodwill, lies in just these things: reading and writing, sewing, doing all the work of the house, and treating everyone as each should be treated. It seems hard to you only now; once you have been at these tasks for a month or two, sitting idle will itself become unbearable to you; if you have no work of your own, you will go about helping others with theirs.

And, my girl, the great advantage of it is that a busy person is safe from a thousand evils; and especially for a young woman, sitting idle is like poison. As long as a daughter-in-law or daughter has parents or parents-in-law over her, there is not so much to fear, whether she does the work of the house or sits with her hands folded [24]; but once she is her own mistress and there is no elder over her, then if she has had the habit of work from childhood, well and good, and if not, the proverb will come true: 'My husband's not home, I fear no one.' And if nothing else, she will sit idle complaining about someone or finding fault with someone. Then if the children run about naked and uncared for, she does not care; if the maids are carrying off the house, she knows nothing about it; if the ceiling-cloth is torn, who cares; if the floor-sheet is dirty, she couldn't care less.

And it is a rule that in a house where the wife has no thought for its work, though she may have a thousand maids, they become even lazier than she is. And if some bad company comes along from somewhere, God knows little by little what may become of the poor thing [TN: literally 'of her enemies', a euphemism that avoids wishing harm on the person meant]; and then God alone can protect the honour of her father, grandfather and parents-in-law.

My girl, God has given woman a contrary understanding; the remedy for it is that she should be caught up in the work of the house all day, so that she has no time even to take counsel with her own heart. And for a woman who is both idle and in bad company there is no hope at all; but one who keeps busy with her own work will never be harmed by any company, however bad.

Besides this, what a great benefit it is that by moving about and working, what one eats and drinks does one good, the body stays in trim, there is less illness, and one sleeps soundly at night.

Luqman the Wise counselled his son always to sleep on soft beds of satin and silk. His son said, 'That is not in my power; if I cannot get them, what shall I do?' Luqman said, 'If you work hard all day, then even if you sleep on the ground or on a bare cot at night, you will find the same rest you would find on beds of satin and silk.'"

In short, how far shall I go on? She began to instruct me after the night prayer and went on talking in this way until past midnight. Whatever she said, she said in such a way that my heart accepted it at once [25]. In the end, then and there, she handed over to me the keys of the wardrobe-room, the storeroom and all the chests and trays, and said, "There, my girl; today I have fulfilled all that was due from me. If I had the strength, I would keep reminding you every day, correct you at every turn, and warn you wherever you slipped. Now neither is my mind steady nor are my wits sound. Now it is for you and your work. If you do as your mother says, blessings will come from my heart, your life to come will be set right, the world will praise you, you will be safe from hundreds of misfortunes, and you will be spared your father's displeasure; and if you do not, you will be sorry, if not today then tomorrow. As for my displeasure, how many days is that for? I am already sitting ready to go; this illness will go only with my life."

Amma-jan suffered from sciatica. At first she used to have an attack once or twice a month, and after a day or two she would be well; but for a year now the pain had fastened on her for good; it ached all the time, and she could no longer get about.

So when I heard all this I began to cry. Her heart too was full, but she held herself together, hugged me, kissed me and said, "My dear girl, why are you crying? When an illness grows worse and one's spirit gives out under the pains of every day, the sick person sees nothing but death; and who can interfere in God's workings? If God wills, He can make me well this very moment; for One who can bring the dead to life, what is curing my illness?" Saying this she hugged me again and said, "There, now go and sleep on your little bed; I am getting sleepy too." I got up, came to my own cot and went to sleep.

From the next day I began to do all the work of the house as she had told me; as soon as I was free from reading and writing, I would turn to my household tasks. For two or three months it was like this: at night, when I lay down on my bed, it felt as if there were no life left in my body; every muscle ached separately, my whole body was stiff; I was worn out with going about, and the moment I lay down I slept so soundly that when the nurses splashed water on me for the dawn prayer, even then my eyes would not open. But, thanks be to God, though I was on my feet all day, not once did I even have a headache; in the morning, as soon as I woke up, I was myself again.

After two or three months, once I had come to enjoy the work of the house, I don't know where my strength came from; my feet would not stay on the ground for a moment. Meanwhile I had also begun to take a lesson from Abba-jan. Apart from the three or four hours for lessons with him and with *ustani-ji*, I simply did not know what it was to sit at ease. And the same habit goes on to this day; you can see for yourself that I never sit idle, and I cannot bear an idle person.
