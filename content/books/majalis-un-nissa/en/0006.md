# The Fifth Majlis: Studies, Marriage and Widowhood

## The Fifth Majlis

The rest of Zubaida Khatun's account

When I entered my thirteenth year, and had read in Persian the *Gulistan*, the *Bustan*, the *Akhlaq-e-Muhsini* and the *Ayar-e-Danish*, and the essential primers of Arabic grammar and syntax; had learned arithmetic as far as vulgar and decimal fractions; had gone through two books of Euclid; had read the geography and history of Hindustan as well; and had made fair copies of writing boards in *naskh* and *nastaliq* and even some calligraphic pieces; then Abba-jan began to teach me two lessons himself: in the morning the *Kimiya-e-Sa'adat*, and in the evening the Arabic *Kalila wa Dimna*. [TN: The *Kimiya-e-Sa'adat* ("The Alchemy of Happiness") is al-Ghazali's Persian work on ethics and religion; *Kalila wa Dimna* is the Arabic version of the animal fables of the *Panchatantra*.] And Amma-jan was wasting away day by day. Her weakness had reached the point that when two people put their hands under her arms and sat her up she would sit, and when they laid her down she would lie. Whenever I was free from the work of the house, I would go and sit with her; sometimes I fanned her, sometimes I pressed her hands and feet, sometimes I rubbed the soles of her feet. And she too had grown so used to my hands that while I was there she never let the servants do anything for her. A few days later I was betrothed. Amma-jan had no hope at all of living.

One day she said to me, "My girl, your betrothal has taken place, and now, God willing, the wedding too will happen one of these days; but who knows whether we too shall be granted the sight of that happiness? In the first place, no one's life can be relied on; it is a breath: if it comes, it comes, and if it doesn't, it doesn't; and especially an invalid like me: only God can raise her up. Well, one had to leave the world some day; but seeing you left alone makes my heart rise to my mouth; this regret will go with me into the grave. Yet I cannot thank God Almighty enough. My prayer was always: O God, if You give me children, give me good ones; otherwise it is better to be childless than to have children like that. That Self-sufficient One accepted my prayer; just as you have kept your mother happy, may God keep you happy in both worlds. Although I know very well that you need no one's advice now, you have not yet seen the ups and downs of the world; you have read and learned, but you have not yet digested it.

Listen, my dear: knowledge is certainly great wealth; this wealth comes only to the fortunate. The great kindness your father has done you is that he has educated you and made you capable. I don't mean to boast, but by God's grace today you are the lamp of your whole family. But if you ask me, my dear, the house of knowledge is a long way off. Mind you never think to yourself that you have learned something. What the people of old said, 'A half-physician is a danger to life, and a half-mulla a danger to faith,' is true; a person of little learning sometimes does things that even an unlettered one would not do. I worry greatly that ever since you opened your eyes in your father's house you have seen nothing but reading and writing; you have always lived under a dome of *Bismillah* [TN: i.e. sheltered from the world]. What do you know of how people will die for the customs of their fathers and forefathers? The money their elders earned by wearing themselves out, and died leaving so that it would help their children in hard times, they squander for nothing on weddings, and then in a few days they themselves are reduced to want for bread. In the same way, when someone dies, they spend hundreds of rupees on the death; the person is gone, and the money is wasted too. Then again, they have piled up hundreds of useless expenses for festivals. In short, in every way they cast God's bounty into the dust.

I fear that when you go to your in-laws' house and see a new world there, and from morning to evening scores of things pass before your eyes such as you never saw in your father's house, nor ever read of in books, and which have no sense in them at all, besides the loss of money in every way, what will become of you then? If you choke on it inwardly and say nothing, God knows what will become of the poor thing; and if you cannot hold back and interfere in something and begin to show off your learning, then who will listen to you? All that will happen is that a quarrel will spring up out of nothing. On one side you will be proud of your learning, on the other they will be devoted to the customs of their elders; good hearts will turn sour for no reason, and in the end everyone will make you out to be the fool.

My girl, remember that it has been the way of the world from of old that once a bad custom takes root anywhere, it is hard to get rid of. People do not care so much for their religion as they care for the customs of their forefathers; and whoever calls customs bad or wants to abolish them is the one who is made the outcast among his people. All customs are bad, but some are bad beyond measure, and yet people come to regard even those as their faith and religion, and become the enemies of anyone who finds fault with their customs. At one time it was the custom of the Arabs that whoever had a daughter born to him would bury her alive in the ground; what custom could be worse than that? Yet they thought it a matter of the highest honour and pride. And the custom of killing daughters still goes on among the Rajputs of Hindustan; but now, for fear of the government, they very rarely do it, and when they do, they do it in secret. In the same way, God and the Prophet have permitted widows to remarry, but among the Muslims here it has become such a disgrace that they think it worse than adultery. If you tell them that its command has come in the Qur'an and the Hadith, and that, further, the wives and daughters of those whose names we bear married two or three times, they have no answer; but as for giving up their ways, there is no question of it. In short, getting the love of customs out of the heart is like separating flesh from the nail. A person has every right over her own house, to do or not do whatever she likes; but getting others to give up these habits is very hard; it needs a very wise and patient person. Mind, if you set foot in this business, set it with care and thought; don't, without thinking, stir up a hornets' nest and find the tables turned on you. Whatever you do, do it so gracefully that the snake dies and the stick does not break.

Besides that, getting on with your mother-in-law and sisters-in-law and the wives of your husband's brothers in your in-laws' house is the hardest stage of all. It is well known that mother-in-law and daughter-in-law, sister-in-law and brother's wife, and the wives of brothers never get on. My dear, you too will face this one day. Mind, never fall short in obeying your mother-in-law and pleasing your sisters-in-law. It is the way of the world that it takes two hands to clap. Now, in the first place, the people of that household, men and women alike, are all good; it is ninety-nine to a hundred that nothing of the kind will come from them; and we are all human, so if something of the sort does happen from someone, you must bear it. That way you will have a place in your husband's heart, and in the end your mother-in-law and sisters-in-law will come to dote on you. And if you look at it fairly, their resentment is not unreasonable. Tell me truly: if even half the attachment your mother has for you went to someone else's child, how bad would you feel? My girl, it is a rule that when a new partner springs up in something a person holds, she takes it very hard indeed. Before, a man had no tie to anyone except his mother and sister; now a partner has appeared for them whose share is even somewhat larger than theirs. But if the mother-in-law and sisters-in-law think about it, their displeasure makes no sense. A person is attached to thousands of things in the world, but to each thing in a different way. The tie with one's parents is not the same as the tie with one's children, and the tie with one's children is not the same as the tie with one's parents; between brothers and sisters there is one kind of love, and between husband and wife something else again. It is useless for one to envy the other. It is like this: just as a person is pleased by eating good food, so is she pleased by smelling a fragrance, hearing a fine voice and seeing a beautiful face; but the pleasure of good food goes to the tongue, a fragrance refreshes the brain, a fine voice delights the ears, and a beautiful face pleases the eyes. Now if someone were to say, 'Sir, what pleasure can he take in food when he is mad about fragrance?' or 'He loves to hear a fine voice; what can he know of the worth of a beautiful face?', everyone would call him, if nothing worse, a fool.

In the same way, mothers and sisters should understand that even if our son or our brother becomes his wife's devotee, our love has not gone anywhere; when our flesh and skin, our life and liver are all one, why should our affection go because of his love for his wife? Rather, they should, if there is some trouble between husband and wife, bring them together however they can; if the husband has no place in his heart for his wife, make a place for her there in every way they can; if the boy is ill-tempered, soften his temper by talking to him; in short, take the daughter-in-law's side as far as possible; and understand that a gentleman who has handed over to us his own life and liver did not do it trusting in the boy alone, but after seeing our conduct and our ways. He agreed to the betrothal only afterwards; first he asked everyone what the men and women of that family are like; whether the boy's parents are ill-tempered; whether the boy's sisters have sharp tongues; how they have treated the daughters of the community who married into their house before. As for the boy, at most he asked about his education; in the first place nobody asks more than that, and those who do, ask in vain. Plenty of good-tempered and virtuous boys become ill-tempered after marriage; plenty of sour, sulky ones, whose brows are never for a moment free of a frown, turn gentle before their wives; so what is the use of asking about that? In any case, my girl, you must simply obey. Your patience and forbearance will never go to waste; if your mother-in-law and sisters-in-law come round because of it, that is what we want, and if not, you will have your reward with God.

Now as for the wives of your husband's brothers, you will have nothing to do with them; in your father-in-law's house, God keep him, there is only one son among four daughters. But remember that the quarrels of the brothers' wives spread over the whole household. The remedy for it is not so hard: their husbands should divide whatever their parents left them amicably among themselves, and not imagine that the brothers will then be cut off from one another. Dividing an inheritance does not put a gulf between hearts. Which is better: the thing that ends a lifetime's quarrel, or the daily bickering? My girl, what they say, that a shared cooking pot breaks at the crossroads, is true; that is why God Almighty has set out everyone's shares in the Qur'an. And womankind has never got on with anyone in a joint arrangement, nor ever will.

As for your husband, whether anyone else is pleased or not, keeping him happy comes before everything. If at some moment something he says hurts you, keep quiet. Quarrelling at every word, being ungrateful, taunting, frowning for no reason, putting on a tearful face, are part of women's habits; for God's sake never go near these things. Listen: keeping your own people happy is no great thing; a true person is one who makes a stranger her own. And with your husband you have to spend your whole life. If husband and wife are of one heart, the shade of a single tree in the jungle is better than a hundred palaces; and if there is some discord, the house is a picture of hell and a settled place is worse than a wilderness. I have heard that our sovereign princess Queen Victoria, who today is mistress of a quarter of the world, used to care for her husband so much that she never let a speck of displeasure touch his heart; and the whole world knows the love she had for her husband. I have heard that when her husband died, Her Majesty remained in mourning for him for a long time, and she has even written a whole book about her husband's life. [TN: Presumably *The Early Years of His Royal Highness the Prince Consort* (1867), compiled by Charles Grey under the Queen's direction.] My girl, husbands have a great rank; tie this in the end of your veil [1]. And this habit women have of digging in their heels over everything, and wanting in every matter that whatever happens, it must be as they say, is a very bad way; whole households have been ruined by it; one should seek refuge from such a nature.

Well, there are many more things to say, but just now my heart is fluttering away; today my state does not look good to me; God knows how I am managing to talk to you. You do not need me to say more; the few letters you have been taught were taught to you so that whatever you do, you do with thought and understanding."

Amma-jan had not been able to finish these words when suddenly her breath began to fail.

Sayyid Abbas says that when my honoured lady [2] reached this point in her telling, she lost control of her heart and began to weep uncontrollably, and wept so much that she broke into sobs. After that I did not have the courage to ask her anything more of her story.

In short, when Nana-jan and Nani-jan had brought up my honoured lady in this way, the concern now was to find a match for her somewhere, but in a place where the boy would be distinguished in birth and in qualities. In those days my revered grandfather, the late Mir Sayyid Ali, though not so very famous for learning and scholarship, was such that in bringing up children people held him up as an example. In his whole life he had four sons and a daughter, of whom three sons died, and one, my honoured father, and the other, my aunt, remained alive and well. Dada-jan took great pains over Abba-jan's education and upbringing, but my aunt was taught only to recite the Qur'an by heart, and beyond that she learned no reading or writing. The secret of it was that Dadi-jan herself could not read or write at all; how then could my aunt have learned? In short, Abba-jan's ability and talent were the talk of the city.

Little by little this came to the ears of Khwaja Sahib. As soon as he heard it he became eager, but he could not bring himself to say anything. People who knew Nana Sahib's mind brought the matter to Dada-jan. There had been no kinship or acquaintance between our elders and Nana-jan's; besides, the quarter [3] too was different: our house was in Khan-e-Dauran Khan's *haveli*, and his property was in Aqil Khan's lane; for this reason Dada-jan had known nothing about him till then. But now that people pointed it out, he too came to know. On hearing this he grew so eager that he called Abba-jan at once, had him write a letter and sent it to Khwaja Sahib's house. He kept the letter and sent the man away.

The next day he sent word by his steward: "I accept the proposal; but you should know that marriage is a thing which, if you keep it simple, is settled with two words, and if you enlarge it, there is no harder business. Even if a man squanders a kingdom on it, he cannot escape people's tongues. There is a well-known saying: 'Not doing it is one fault, and doing it is a thousand faults.' [TN: Persian, *na-kardan yak aib, va kardan hazar aib*.] Why then do a thing in which money is wasted, months of time are lost besides, neither God is pleased nor the Prophet, and people's taunts remain on top of it all? I tell you plainly now that I will do none of the scores of absurd customs that people in the city practise at weddings, nor will I, for show, have the girl's *mahr* (dower) fixed at a lakh or two lakhs of rupees. If your sense of honour can accept this, then in God's name fix a date and come on that day."

Dada-jan accepted all these terms and sent word back: "Everything you have said is after my own heart; had you left it out, I should perhaps have had to say it myself. But if you will pardon the liberty, may I too make one request? My late father left me his instruction that sons should be married at not less than twenty-five years of age. The reason why people in our country are so often weak and feeble, and are good for nothing by the age of forty or forty-five, is chiefly that they are married at a very young age; before their bodies have gained strength or their minds have matured, this burden suddenly falls on them, and in a few days they wither like a flower. In short, the matter is settled; I have given Amjad Ali into your service. But it would be the greatest kindness if you did not hurry the bride's departure. By God's grace Amjad Ali has just begun his twenty-second year; give him three more years' grace. There will be this further advantage in it: ever since he heard that Maulvi Faizullah of Benares knows astronomy well and has globes and instruments of every kind, he has longed to go to Benares; in this time he will go there too. And I mean to send him to the Deccan from there with some trading goods and one or two experienced men; that plan too will be fulfilled, for it will broaden his mind a little, he will gain some skill in trade, and he will see ten or twenty cities for nothing. I am sure all this will be finished within three years; then it is for you to send the bride whenever you please."

Nana-jan agreed to all of this. In short, how far shall I go on? In the fourth year, when Abba-jan came back from the Deccan, the date of the marriage was fixed as soon as he arrived. I have heard that at the time of the marriage Dada-jan wanted very much for the *mahr* to be set somewhat higher, but Nana Sahib would not have it set above two thousand. And I have also heard that before the *nikah* (marriage contract) he got up and went into the women's quarters, had all the women who kept *purdah* withdraw, and said to his daughter, "My girl, by God's grace you are educated, capable and sensible, and know the good and bad of everything well; there is no need to explain much to you. Your late mother, God rest her, has told you everything about Mir Sayyid Ali's household; from what I have heard, the conduct of the men and women of that house is very good; beyond that, God knows. And his son Amjad Ali too seems, outwardly, very dutiful and worthy [4]; and in ability he is a thousand times better than his own companions. But this is the time of the *nikah*; it is required that you be asked now; say freely whatever you wish." On hearing this she remained silent. Khwaja Sahib asked once, he asked twice; at last, when he saw that this would not do, he said, "Listen, my dear: this is not a matter of two or four days, it is a bond for life. Your father is not such a fool as to put your hand in a stranger's hand without asking you. Each person's judgement is different; in my view he may be ever so good, but how do I know what is in your heart? And to be shy in a matter of business is foolish; it never ends well. We did not teach you your few letters so that you would stay caught in the prison of ignorance and go on following the same absurd customs of the old days. No: the meaning of knowledge is that a person should do what is not bad for her and is good in the sight of God and the Prophet, whether anyone calls it bad or good. Now, how long is this misplaced shyness and modesty going to last? Ten gentlemen are sitting outside waiting. If your heart is truly at ease, accept this; if not, give your answer; I will put people off gracefully this moment, and, God forbid, no blame will come to you." When the daughter heard these words from her father, she gave no thought, before his displeasure, to the taunts of the clan, and said to him plainly, "What Your Honour has decided for your servant is exactly right; beyond that, what is written in one's fate is what happens." Nana-jan was overjoyed to hear this, blessed his daughter and came out to the *diwan-khana*, and said to the Qazi Sahib, "I am the girl's guardian, and also her agent; please begin in God's name." In short, when the *nikah* was over, Nana-jan made a sign to his steward; he went into the wardrobe-room and brought five bags of rupees and some trays of jewellery, clothes and so on, and set them before the whole gathering. Nana-jan took the deed of a village out of his pocket and put it with the rest of the dowry, and turning to Dada-jan recited this verse:

I have entrusted to you my capital;
you know the account of less and more.

Dada-jan gave many thanks and said, "Among the great favours God Almighty has done me, this too is one, that Amjad Ali has been granted your service." [TN: The verse is Persian (*sipurdam ba tu maya-e-khwesh ra / tu dani hisab-e-kam-o-besh ra*), a well-known Persian couplet.] Nana-jan told the steward, "Tell the bearers quickly to bring the litter to the women's gate." And he himself got up and went inside; all the women who kept *purdah* withdrew. He went straight to his daughter, whispered something in her ear and embraced her, and said, "There, my girl, God keep you! Nurse Husaini Khanam will go with you; she will stay with you for six months or a year, until you settle in at your in-laws'." Then, when Amma-jan had taken her leave and was seated in the litter, he took Abba-jan by the hand, led him aside and spoke to him for a while, and then sent him off.

It was Nurse Husaini Khanam herself who told me this whole story. I never saw Abba-jan to remember; I was a little over three when he died, but he lived twenty or twenty-two years after the marriage. I was born some eighteen or nineteen years after their wedding. One day I asked Amma-jan what Nana-jan had said to her and to Abba-jan at the time of the departure. She got up and went inside, brought some written papers out of the bookcase and gave them to me, saying, "There, this is the testament your Abba wrote before he died; everything you are asking about is written in it. From today keep it with you, and look at it now and then." When I opened it and read it from beginning to end, truly every word of it was worth jewels. God willing, some day when there is time I will tell it all from beginning to end.

(End of the first part)
