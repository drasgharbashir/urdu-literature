# Truthfulness, Patrons and Satire

## Truthfulness

Although Asian poetry, whose foundation is laid on falsehood and exaggeration, had run through every vein and nerve of the Mirza's being, yet in reports and stories, in promises and undertakings and in conversation he was most truthful and sincere of speech. That is why he was greatly displeased with anyone who did not trust his promise or undertaking. The Mirza asked the late Tafazzul Husain Khan, son of Diwan Fazlullah Khan, for (a copy of) his own *diwan*, and undertook to send it back after looking at it. He refused to lend the *diwan*. In reply to his refusal the Mirza writes: "Well, sir! Has water been poured over all this being uncle and nephew, and pupil and master? If it were something worth a thousand or five hundred and I asked you for it, God knows what fury you would let loose! My own verse! Bought for eight or ten rupees! And even so, I do not say give it to me; may it be a blessing to you; lend it to me; let me look at it; let me copy what I do not have; then I will send it back to you. Not to give it on such a request is proof that you think me a liar, that you do not trust me, or that you are bent on vexing and tormenting me. Give that book to my man at once. By God and by God, I will copy from it what I do not have and send it back. If I do not return it to you, a curse on me; and if you do not accept my oath and do not give the book to the bearer of this note, bravo to you!"

## Anecdote

In the same way he writes in a letter to Nawab 'Ala'-ud-Din Khan: "In the hand of death there is no heart worse off than your opinion of me [TN: Persian half-line]. I have written again and again that I did not keep the draft of the *qasida*; I have written again and again that I do not remember which *ruba'i*s you are asking for; and again you write, send the *ruba'i*s, send the *qasida*! Which means, you are a liar; this time you will certainly send them. Brother, by the Qur'an, by the Gospel, by the Torah, by the Psalms, by the four Vedas of the Hindus, by the *Dasatir*, by the *Zand*, by the *Pazand*, by the *Avesta*, by the Guru's *Granth*: neither have I that *qasida*, nor do I remember those *ruba'i*s. As to the collected works, what I have already said:

I am the same as I am, and the same I shall be." [TN: Persian half-line.]

It was because of this same truthfulness that the Mirza did nothing in secret. What was in his heart was on his tongue. What he did in private he did in public too. So if he had any fault, it was one that every Tom, Dick and Harry knew; of hidden faults he was entirely free.

## Complaint of Lack of Appreciation

At the thought that those who valued his verse were very few, he was often sore at heart. So he complained of this here and there in Persian and Urdu verse and prose. One day he came straight from the Fort to Nawab Mustafa Khan's house and said: "Today His Majesty showed us great appreciation. I had written a *qasida* to congratulate him on the 'Id and taken it with me; when I had finished reciting the *qasida*, His Majesty was pleased to say, 'Mirza, you recite very well.'" After this the Nawab and the Mirza lamented for a long time over the age's lack of appreciation.

In *Mihr-e Nimroz* he expresses in one place the theme that he spent his poetic excellence only in singing the praises of those who did not appreciate it, thus: "My breast had a breath flowing like the breeze that blows from a bed of eglantine; alas for me, that I breathed it only upon what is no garden [TN: reading uncertain]. My fingers had a pen raining like the Tigris, like a cloud that rises from the west; how vainly I laboured, pouring the rain upon salt land!" [TN: Persian in the original.]

## Valuing Those Who Understood Poetry

That is why, when by good fortune he found a man who could weigh and understand poetry, he considered him an unexpected blessing. Munshi Nabi Bakhsh, whose pen name was Haqir, who was at one time *sarrishtadar* (head clerk) at Koil, and whose understanding and judgement of poetry have been heard praised by great men, once came to Delhi and stayed at the Mirza's house. Of him the Mirza writes in a Persian letter to Munshi Hargopal Tafta, the substance of which is this: "God took pity on my friendlessness and loneliness, and sent me a man who brought with him a salve for my wounds and a cure for my pain, and who lit up my dark night. With his words he lit a candle in whose light I saw the excellence of my own verse, which had been hidden even from my own eyes in the darkness of my ill fortune. I am amazed at the degree of understanding and judgement of poetry that has been granted to this unique sage, Munshi Nabi Bakhsh. Although I write verse and know how to write verse, until I saw this noble man I did not understand what understanding of poetry is, or whom one should call a man who understands poetry. It is well known that God divided beauty into two parts, gave half to Joseph and half to the whole of mankind. It is no wonder if understanding of poetry and taste for meaning too were divided into two parts, and half fell to the share of Munshi Nabi Bakhsh and half to that of the whole world. However hostile the age and the heavens may be to me, thanks to this man's friendship I am free from care about the enmity of the age, and with this blessing I am content to do without the world."

## Admission of His Own Inability

When the Mirza was asked for some piece of verse that he could not easily produce, he did not care that the fame and renown of his poetry would suffer; rather he wrote back plainly that it was beyond his power. Once, probably the late *Mujtahid-ul-'Asr* Sayyid Muhammad, on whom be God's mercy and forgiveness, asked the Mirza to write in Urdu a *marsiya* (elegy) on His Holiness the Lord of Martyrs. Since the Mirza respected him greatly and did not wish to refuse his request, he sat down to write a *marsiya* in obedience to his command. Since he had never set foot in this lane, and the request was for a thing that others had already brought to the height of perfection, and his faculties had begun to decline, with difficulty he wrote three stanzas of a *musaddas* (six-line stanza), of which we remember the first, and it is quoted here:

Yes, O breath of the morning breeze, become a scatterer of flames!
O Tigris of blood, flow from the eyes of the angels!

O chant of "Arise!" on the lips of Jesus, become a lament!
O mourners of the wronged King, where are you?

The matter has gone badly wrong; it will not come right by mending;
now nothing will do but to set the house on fire.

He wrote this and two more stanzas, sent them to the *Mujtahid-ul-'Asr*, and wrote plainly: "I have written three stanzas only in obedience to your command; otherwise I am not a man of this field. This is the share of those who have spent their lives in this valley; to reach their degree I would need another lifetime. So let me be excused and pardoned from this service." It was his saying that in India there has never been, nor will there ever be, a *marsiya* writer like Anis and Dabir.

## Anecdote

Sometimes he evaded, with great wit, requests that would have cost him trouble to carry out. It is known that he always dreaded working out chronograms. Once the late Nawab 'Ala'-ud-Din Khan asked him for a chronogram on the birth of his son and a chronogrammatic name for him; in reply he writes: "The lion feeds his cubs on the flesh of the prey, and teaches them the way of bringing down game; when they grow up they hunt and eat for themselves. You have become a poet, you have a God-given fine nature; why do you not compose the chronogram of your son's birth yourself? Why do you not find the chronogrammatic name yourself, instead of troubling me, an old, grief-stricken, dead-hearted man? 'Ala'-ud-Din Khan! By your life!! For the first boy I put a chronogrammatic name into verse, and that boy did not live; the fancy has taken hold of me that it was the effect of my ill-starred fortune. The man I praise does not live: Nasir-ud-Din Haidar and Amjad 'Ali Shah went off after one *qasida* each. Wajid 'Ali Shah bore three *qasida*s, then could not hold up. He in whose praise ten or twenty *qasida*s were written went beyond even non-existence. No, sir, I appeal to God! I will neither compose a birth chronogram nor search for a chronogrammatic name."

## Not Writing Satire

Although the Mirza's whole life was spent writing *qasida*s and singing praises, and it often happened that he received no reward at all for his praise and eulogy, and all the labour and toil he had to put into composing *qasida*s went for nothing, yet he never wrote a *qit'a* or *qasida* in satire (*hajv*) of anyone. Only one *qit'a*, which is not included in the Mirza's printed collected works, has come to our hands among his manuscript drafts, which my friend and the Mirza's dear pupil Lala Bihari Lal Mushtaq of Delhi sent me while this book was being written. From this *qit'a* it appears that the Mirza had sent a Persian *qasida* in praise of a nobleman together with a petition, and for a long time received no reply; then the Mirza sent this *qit'a* by way of a reminder, which can hardly be called even a gentle satire (*hajv-e malih*). Since the theme of this *qit'a* is not without charm, we first give its gist in Urdu, after which the *qit'a* will be quoted as it is.

The gist of the *qit'a* is this: I asked Reason, I had sent such and such a *qasida* to the Nawab and presented a petition with it; then what is the reason that no reply has been granted? Has the Nawab taken offence at me? If so, I wrote praise in vain. God knows what I may have written at which the Nawab took offence. Reason said: Why are you anxious? The equipage with which the Nawab wishes to send your reward cannot be got together quickly. He gave orders long ago that brocade from Damascus, velvet from Rum, diamonds from the mine, gold from the quarry, elephants from the Deccan, emeralds from the mountain, horses from Iraq, pearls from the sea, turquoise from Nishapur, rubies from Badakhshan, she-camels from Baghdad, swords from Isfahan, shawl wool from Kashmir and cloth of gold from Iran should all be collected and brought; then the reward will be sent to Ghalib. So since all this delay is for this reason, you should not take it as proof of the Nawab's displeasure. When Reason had coaxed me thus, all my despair and hopelessness turned into hope. I too said in my heart: since my patron wishes to do all this for me, why should I not ask for him, in return, the mirror and crown from Alexander, the ring and throne from Solomon, Jamshid's cup from the unseen world, the water of life from Khizr's fountain, eternal life, everlasting joy, strength of heart and firmness of faith from my God, and from my patron a reply to my petition and the reward for my *qasida*?

## Qit'a

I said to Reason in the privacy of intimacy:
"O candle and lamp of the seven halls,

for what reason is it that the Nawab
has not written a reply to my letter? Tell me!

A petition of the kind you know,
a dervish writing to a sultan;

a *qasida* of the kind of which you would say
a hyacinth bed has sprung up from the page:

both of these arrived, and there appears
no effect from that side in any way.

Can the Nawab have been offended by the praise?
Would that I had not become a singer of praise!

Alas, what have I said, that I should be
regretful of my own words?"

My Reason said in reply: "Ghalib!
Beware, do not be deceived by Satan.

The Nawab is thinking of a present,
so that he may send you a letter with due equipage.

He knows, for it has passed through his mind,
that all that cannot be collected quickly [TN: reading moderately confident].

It is soon for it all to be collected,
but long since he gave the order,

that travellers by sea and land, going round,
should bring with great effort

brocade from Damascus and velvet from Rum,
diamonds from the mine and gold from the quarry,

elephants from the Deccan and emeralds from the mountain,
a steed from Iraq and pearls from Oman,

fine turquoise from Nishapur,
choice rubies from Badakhshan,

a swift camel from Baghdad,
a cutting sword from Isfahan,

costly shawl wool from Kashmir,
precious cloth of gold from Iran.

In short, since the delay is for this reason,
there is no proof of offence or displeasure."

When the old man Reason, with heart-beguiling art,
had told all these hidden secrets,

hope rose up within me [TN: reading moderately confident],
a salve for the wound of despair and disappointment.

I said: since he has shown me this kindness,
that *qibla* and object of reverence of the great,

of necessity, by way of doing what is due,
so that some return may be made for it,

I too will ask for him,
though this wish is not easy:

the mirror and the crown from Alexander,
the ring and the throne from Solomon,

from the unseen world the cup of Jamshid,
from the fountain of Khizr the water of life,

eternal life and everlasting joy,
strength of heart and firmness of faith,

grace to answer my letter,
the seal of a gift and the bestowal of bounty.
