# Qati'-e Burhan and the Controversy

## Qati'-e Burhan

When the Mirza had finished *Dastambu*, and the same state of solitude and silence still continued, what remedy was there but to treat ink and pen as companion and friend, and to forget his grief and divert his heart by reading and writing something? At that time the Mirza had no book with him except *Burhan-e Qati'* [TN: a Persian dictionary compiled in India in the seventeenth century] and the *Dasatir*. He picked up the *Burhan* and began to look through it cursorily. At the very first glance some incoherences appeared. Then, looking more carefully, he found the definitions of many words wrong. He saw one and the same word entered in several sections in different forms. He saw words which poets have used figuratively and by way of metonymy entered as independent words. He found the manner of explanation often clumsy and contrary to the principles of lexicography. He also saw explanations of many words whose meaning he could not understand at all. As a memorandum the Mirza began to note down the places that seemed open to objection. Little by little it became a book, which was named *Qati'-e Burhan*, and was printed and published in 1862 [TN: year printed small; reading uncertain]. Then in 1865 [TN: reading uncertain] the Mirza had it printed a second time, with other matter and observations added, and named it *Dirafsh-e Kawiyani*.

It seems proper to give here two or four examples of the words over which the Mirza has faulted [TN: printed *takhliya*, probably for *takhti'a*, "faulting"] the author of the *Burhan*. For example, the author of the *Burhan* gives the meaning of *'anbar-e arzan* as the tresses of the Holy Prophet, and then says that it is also called *'anbar-e larzan*. The Mirza explains the source of the error thus: the author had seen this verse of Nizami's, which is in praise of the Prophet: "The scent that you give from that trembling ambergris (*'anbar-e larzan*), if you give it for both worlds, you give it cheap (*arzan*)." So he took the metaphor in *'anbar-e larzan* for a real word, and did not understand at all the place and sense of *arzan* in the second half-line, and gave the name *'anbar-e arzan* to the Prophet's ambergris-scented locks, which would be cheap even in exchange for both worlds. Or, for example, it is written in the *Burhan*: "*Qafila shud* means *qafila raft* (the caravan went), that is, the caravan leader went, which is a metonymy for the death of the Prophet." In the first place, to take *qafila shud* as a word at all is meaningless; then to say that it means "the caravan leader went", and to understand by the caravan leader's going the death of the Lord of the Universe, is error upon error and folly upon folly. The Mirza traces the error to this verse of Maulana Nizami: "The caravan has gone; look at our being left behind; O our Someone, look at our having no one." This verse occurs in the invocation of *Makhzan-ul-Asrar*, but the Mirza by an oversight has ascribed it to Jami. The meaning of the verse is that our friends, companions and fellow travellers have all gone, and now we have no friend or helper but You. Or, for example, the author of the *Burhan* writes: "*Shash-zarb-e natija-e khub* is a metonymy for jewels and gold, and a metonymy for tears, and also for sugar, honey and kinds of fruit; and it has also been seen with *zarb* dropped, as *shash natija-e khub*." The Mirza's ridicule of this is long; the gist is that only the author of the *Burhan* could make this compound and meaningless phrase into a word. And there are hundreds of words of this kind on which the Mirza has seized, and errors and incoherences of every sort, which cannot be grasped without looking through *Dirafsh-e Kawiyani* from beginning to end.

At the time when the Mirza wrote *Qati'-e Burhan* he had no dictionary of words except a manuscript *Burhan*, nor any other material on which an investigation of words could be based. Whatever he wrote, he wrote either relying only on his memory or on the evidence of his taste and intuition. In spite of all this, apart from a few places where the Mirza really did slip, and some errors which he himself acknowledged, all his objections appear justified. When writing *Dirafsh-e Kawiyani*, however, it seems that the corrected and printed *Burhan* of the scholars of Calcutta was before the Mirza.

## Opposition to Qati'-e Burhan

No sooner was this book published than every Tom, Dick and Harry girded himself to oppose the Mirza. In answer to the one *Qati'-e Burhan* several treatises were written: *Muhriq-e Qati'*, *Qati'-e Qati'*, *Mu'ayyid-e Burhan*, *Sati'-e Burhan* and others.

## The Reason for the Opposition

The reason for the opposition is plain. Imitation (*taqlid*), not only in matters of religion but in everything, every task, every science and every art, has become so necessary a thing that the idea of investigation neither enters anyone's own heart, nor is anyone else thought fit to say anything against the ancients. A book written a hundred or two hundred years ago is considered as binding as revealed scripture. So however correct and justified the Mirza's objections to *Burhan-e Qati'* might have been, it was not possible that they should not be fiercely opposed.

Some people think that the opposition arose mostly because the Mirza, out of his playful nature, ridiculed the author of the *Burhan* here and there, and in places harsh words also dropped from his pen in anger and wrath; but this idea is not correct. Even if the Mirza had not written such words about the author of the *Burhan*, there would certainly have been opposition. For the old educated men of India, who are now in a most neglected condition, have no other chance left of coming out of the corner of obscurity and namelessness except to write a refutation of the book of some eminent and distinguished man, and to show people that we too are something.

When the treatises written in reply to *Qati'-e Burhan* are looked at cursorily, most of the answers to the Mirza's objections seem quite correct. Each respondent supports the *Burhan* by saying that the word is explained just as the author of the *Burhan* explains it in the *Farhang-e Jahangiri*, or the *Farhang-e Rashidi*, or the *Siraj-ul-Lughat*, or the *Mu'ayyid-ul-Fuzala*, or the *Haft Qulzum*, or some other dictionary. And from this it plainly appears at first sight that the Mirza's objection is wrong. But when one considers that most dictionaries of Persian words were written in India, and that later compilers mostly followed the dictionary that was written first, no respondent's answer retains any weight.

## Support for Qati'-e Burhan

A famous Iranian author, Riza Quli Khan Hidayat, in 1873 [TN: year printed small; reading uncertain], that is, four years after the Mirza's death, wrote a comprehensive work on Persian vocabulary, which is named *Farhang-e Nasiri* [TN: the *Farhang-e Anjuman-ara-ye Nasiri*], and which came to India ten or twelve years after the Mirza's death. Clearly whatever he wrote about Persian words will in any case be more reliable and more worthy of confidence than the researches of the lexicographers who wrote books of Persian vocabulary sitting in India. At the beginning of his dictionary he devoted a chapter to describing the errors and slips of all three, the *Farhang-e Jahangiri*, the *Farhang-e Rashidi* and the *Burhan-e Qati'*, and after that in one chapter he exposed the errors of the *Burhan-e Qati'* alone. For example, that Andalus is an island on top of a mountain; or that Granada is a province of India; or that it gives three meanings for *chakak*: forehead, writer of deeds, and seal-engraver (and all three meanings are wrong); or that Karukh, which is a village in the environs of Herat, is described in the *Burhan* as "a village among the villages of the world". Here the author of the *Farhang-e Nasiri* writes ironically: "Truly he has made a subtle investigation." In the same way he has exposed many errors of the author of the *Burhan* in this chapter, and besides this has faulted him here and there throughout his whole dictionary.

The objections that the Mirza raised against the *Burhan* are also supported here and there by the *Farhang-e Nasiri*. Among them, the explanation of the words *abjin*, *astakhr* and *istakhr*, *jamdar*, *bakhtar*, *rawash* and *zawash*, *karbakiya*, *vizha* and *avizha*, and many other words of this kind [TN: several of these Persian words are read with low confidence], is found in the *Farhang-e Nasiri* in agreement with the Mirza's statement. Besides this, where the Mirza has called a statement of the *Burhan* meaningless and absurd, Riza Quli Khan too calls it absurd. For example, in explaining the word *anjugak* the author of the *Burhan* writes: "However much the sweeper of imagination may sweep with the broom of hyacinth upon the saddle-cloth of the bear's beard, he cannot clean it from its skin" [TN: Persian; reading uncertain, and the sense, as Ghalib says, obscure]. The Mirza writes of this: "The last phrase is surely the speech of a demon; when the excellence of the research is such, and the beauty of the style such, how can the real purpose, which is to learn unknown things, be achieved from *Burhan-e Qati'*?" Riza Quli writes ironically on this same phrase: "In this place this wondrous composition and eloquent expression were the offspring of his refined nature; as proof of the sound taste and straight judgement of the author of the *Burhan* these phrases alone are enough. What more is to be expected of you than this?" In the same way he quotes many of the absurd phrases of the *Burhan* and laughs at them, and says: "In the land of India, where they know neither Turkish nor Persian, how can they record and correct Persian words?" In one place he quotes the opinion of the author of *Burhan-e Jami'* (who is an Iranian) on *Burhan-e Qati'*, the meaning of which is this: "In *Burhan-e Qati'* words are recorded without authority or evidence; they should not be relied upon. In it metonymies are also treated as separate words, and it indulges in useless prolixity in describing obsolete words of Syriac, Hebrew, Turkish, Zand and Pazand, and in mentioning one and the same word again and again in different forms." After this Riza Quli confirms the author of *Burhan-e Jami'* and agrees with his opinion. Since this account will seem tedious in the life of the Mirza, we leave it aside; whoever wishes to see it in more detail should consult the *Farhang-e Nasiri* himself.

It is true that the Mirza made some wrong objections in *Qati'-e Burhan*; in particular he made a very gross error about the word *afsos* (regret), declaring it to be of Arabic origin, derived from *asaf*; and in the end he himself acknowledged this error, and admitted his ignorance in the investigation of Arabic words; and it is possible that besides this he erred here and there elsewhere too. But if one looks fairly, *Qati'-e Burhan* gives sufficient proof of the Mirza's soundness of nature and correct taste; and, as he writes in one place, "The rules and principles of the Persian language are settled in my mind as the temper is in steel": he does in fact appear to have had a natural affinity with Persian. The opinion that he formed of the *Burhan* guided only by his sound intuition is the same opinion that the scholars of Iran have expressed about it; and the errors and incoherences that the Mirza pointed out in the *Burhan*, these and countless other errors besides, the author of the *Farhang-e Nasiri* has marked in it. What greater proof could there be of the soundness of nature of an Indian scholar?

At the end of *Qati'-e Burhan* the Mirza wrote some observations; the substance of the first of them is this: "Since there is a smell of self-display in presenting these observations, people may perhaps say that for one who is himself an Indian not to accept Indians as authorities, and himself to claim mastery of the language, is meaningless. So I confess that my grandfather came from Turan, my father was born in Delhi, and I in Agra. God forbid that I should consider myself a native speaker; I am without doubt a master of the language; and my mastery of the language is, first, through the God-given soundness of my nature, which does not accept error and is not satisfied without the truth; second, because my nature happens to have a natural affinity with Persian; third, through the blessing of the company of Maulana 'Abdus-Samad, which I enjoyed continuously for two years. At the age of fourteen I received training from him, and for fifty-two years I have practised poetry. Now, in my sixty-sixth year, I thank God, and no one but God can know, how many doors of meaning He has opened to me in these fifty-two years, and to what height He has raised my thought. Alas that people did not understand the excellence of my verse; and the greater pity is that they remained deprived of recognising the divine glory, and did not raise their eyes to look at the wonders of my verse and prose. It is as if the closing couplet (*maqta'*) of Naziri, whose resting place is Paradise, fits my case:

You, Naziri, had come from heaven like the Messiah;
you went back again, and no one knew your worth, alas!" [TN: the second half-line is partly blotted in the print and read from context.]

Of all the people who wrote replies to *Qati'-e Burhan*, the Mirza too answered some, and in these answers he mostly made use of wit and playfulness. Here he has ridiculed their style, there made fun of their researches.

## Anecdote

The Mirza gave no reply to Maulvi Aminuddin's book *Qati'-e Qati'*, because it was full of obscene and indecent language. Someone said, "Sir! You have written no reply to it." The Mirza said, "If a donkey kicks you, will you kick it too?"

There is one Mirza Ahmad 'Ali Beg, a resident of Calcutta, who wrote a long book against the Mirza, *Mu'ayyid-ul-Burhan*, in writing which he ransacked the whole library of the Asiatic Society to refute a few leaves of *Qati'-e Burhan*, and, like the other respondents, did not accept any of the Mirza's objections. And the same kind of contemptuous words that the Mirza had used of the author of the *Burhan* he used of the Mirza. He declared himself to be of Isfahani descent, praised Tek Chand Bahar and Qatil highly, and had commendations (*taqriz*s) and chronograms written in praise of his book and printed at the end of it. In reply to this the Mirza wrote a treatise named *Tegh-e Tez* ("The Sharp Sword"), and also wrote and sent him a Persian *qit'a*, some verses of which are quoted here, and which are not without charm:

What good is it to the gentleman that his forefathers were of Isfahan?
His Creator brought him into being in the land of Bengal.

With Qatil, and the compiler of the *Burhan*, and Lala Tek Chand
he has practised flattery[1], partisanship, kindness and courtesy.

He has founded a court of justice[2], and in it all three
he has made *munsif*, *sadr amin* and *sadr-e a'la* [TN: grades of Indian civil judges under British rule].

If he has such love for Indians in matters of speech,
I too am from India: why has he turned away from me?

What is the purpose of his abusing me? It seems the good man
has hoped for forgiveness from God as the wage of this work.

A man of learning and letters! And then, from excess of wrath,
he has opened, like fools, a register of curses and blame.

In argument abuse is the business of the rabble; yes!
Learning is ashamed of the deed that the Agha has done.

He is taking revenge for the compiler of *Burhan-e Qati'*;
what we did to him, the gentleman has done to us.

I am a soldier's son, my speech is bound to be harsh;
woe to him if he has done these things in imitation of me.

I spoke harshly, but I gave wit its due;
the playful nature that I have demanded it.

He supports the *Burhan*, but the proof (*burhan*) is nowhere to be seen;
whatever he has composed is nothing but acceptance of its word.

The looseness of the gait of the pen of the *Burhan*'s writer
he either did not know, or knowingly concealed.

For me insult, and for himself praise, everywhere;
he has disgraced both me and himself in the world.

I found, on seeing the chronograms of that book,
that he abused me himself and hinted it to his friends.

He brought warriors (*ghazi*s) with him for the holy war,
lest you should think he waged this battle alone.

In *Qati'-e Burhan* and all the Mirza's writings connected with it, besides objections and replies, there are also many valuable observations, delicate and entertaining stories, and witticisms and jests.

## Anecdote

The author of the *Burhan* counts the word *faraz* among words with opposite meanings (*azdad*), and gives *faraz kardan* both meanings, to shut and to open. But the Mirza does not count it among such words; he gives it only the meaning of shutting; and he has supported his claim with the very verses that his opponents cited as authority. But since all the lexicographers of India counted *faraz* among words of opposite meaning, the Mirza writes of this that "to count it among such words is a consensus of the same kind as the consensus of the people of Syria on the caliphate of Yazid."

## Anecdote

After exposing some vulgar errors of the author of the *Burhan* and the incoherence of his statements, he writes in one place: "Worshippers of God! For God's sake, I do not ask who this man is who does not understand Arabic and does not know Persian (that is, the compiler of the *Burhan*); I ask, what is he?" In another place, in great anger, he writes: "Since he has no knowledge of the true essence of a word, why does he write a dictionary? He should have woven mats, twisted rope, sold firewood, kindled the bath-house furnace."

The Mirza also brought a suit for defamation against the author of a Persian treatise which had been written in reply to *Qati'-e Burhan* and which was full of obscenity and abuse; but when no hope of success remained he finally filed a deed of compromise. In the course of the proceedings some learned men of Delhi were summoned to the court to be asked whether the phrases which the plaintiff had put forward in proof of his claim did in fact convey obscenity and abuse or not. To save the poor accused from punishment they explained those phrases in such a way that no charge could be laid against him.

## Anecdote

These maulvis were on visiting terms with the Mirza. Someone asked, "Sir! Why did they give evidence against you?" The Mirza recited this Persian verse of his:

Whatever you look at inclines only to its own kind;
the measure of my friendlessness is my nobility of descent[3].

[2] Hali's note: *Davari-gah*: a law court.

[3] Hali's note: That is, the reason for my friendlessness is my noble descent; for everyone inclines to his own kind, and since no one is of my kind in nobility of descent, no one sides with me.
