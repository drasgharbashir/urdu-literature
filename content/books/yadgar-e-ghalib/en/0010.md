# Learning and Accomplishments

## Arabic

In Arabic the Mirza had studied nothing with a teacher except morphology and syntax; but since he had a natural affinity with the science of language, on reading his Urdu and Persian verse and prose the thought never so much as crosses one's mind that this man could be unacquainted with Arabic and the art of letters. Everywhere he has used Arabic words with the same skill with which a good scholar and man of letters ought to use them.

## Knowledge of Persian

Apart from poetry, the gift of which had been placed in his nature, the Mirza had such mastery of the Persian language, of the investigation of Persian words and idioms, and of the styles of expression of native speakers, that even among native speakers only exceptional men would have such mastery of the language of the authoritative poets of Iran.

## Prosody

Besides this he seems to have had a sufficient command of the art of prosody (*'aruz*) too. Many great and famous poets have been seen and heard of who, for all their excellence in poetry, are wholly unacquainted with this art, and cannot compose verse in metres other than the straightforward ones whose measure and rhythm can be judged by a sound natural ear alone. Thus Maulana Rumi says: "I do not know *fa'ilatun fa'ilat*; I speak verse better than sugar and candy." The Mirza's case was not like this; for in Persian and Urdu he wrote a number of *ghazal*s, and also one or two Persian *qasida*s, in such crooked metres that most people with a natural ear cannot move in those metres without a knowledge of prosody.

## Astrology

He had some knowledge of astrology (*nujum*) and complete familiarity with its technical terms; sufficient proof of this is found here and there in his Persian verse.

## Mysticism

He had a special affinity with mysticism (*tasawwuf*), of which it has been said that "it is good for making poetry"; and a great many books and treatises on spiritual truths and mystic knowledge had passed through his reading. And to tell the truth, it was these mystical ideas that had made the Mirza distinguished not only among his contemporaries but among all the poets of the twelfth and thirteenth centuries [TN: of the Hijra].

## History

He had no attachment at all to the art of history, or to accounts, mensuration and the like. At the time when he was writing the history of the house of Timur, that is *Mihr-e Nimroz*, someone took him for a historian and asked him some questions; in reply he writes: "I am so much a stranger to the arts of history, mensuration and accounts that I cannot even understand these arts. The officials of the royal record office write a summary of events from the books in Urdu and send it to me; I put it into Persian and hand it over. I have not a single book in my house. I am only this much, that I can write verse and prose according to my capacity; I am not a historian:

We have not read the tale of Alexander and Darius;
ask us nothing but the story of love and faithfulness."[1]

## Handwriting

The Mirza's hand was *nasta'liq* mixed with *shafi'a* [TN: a cursive variety of the *shikasta* style], very sweet and attractive, such as most Iranians have; and for all the beauty of his hand he was a very quick and rapid writer.

## Reciting Verse

His manner of reciting verse too, especially at *mushaira*s (poetry gatherings), was extremely attractive and moving. A few years before the Mutiny, when *mushaira*s were held in the Diwan-e 'Amm, I heard the Mirza recite at a *mushaira* once only. Since his turn to recite came after everyone else's, morning had come. The Mirza said, "Gentlemen! I too will sing my *Bhairavin*" [TN: a morning *raga*]; and with these words he recited first an Urdu *ghazal* on the set pattern (*tarh*), and after that a Persian one not on the set pattern, in a most plaintive voice. It seemed as if he found no one in the assembly who appreciated him, and so a note of lament had come into his recitation.

At the time when Mir Nizam-ud-Din Mamnun held *mushaira*s in Shah Sahib's old *madrasa*, at one *mushaira* the Mirza recited his Persian *qasida* with the refrains *darya giristan* and *tanha giristan* ("to weep a river", "to weep alone"), which he had written in praise of His Holiness the Lord of Martyrs [TN: Imam Husain]. It is said that the gathering of the *mushaira* turned into an assembly of mourning. As long as the *qasida* was being recited people wept continuously; the late Mufti Sadr-ud-Din Khan was also present. As it happened, in that very state the rain too began to fall. The Mufti said, "Heaven too has wept."

Concerning this same *qasida*, Sayyid Akbar Mirza, worthy son of the late Nazir Sayyid Husain Mirza, relates that in the port of Basra there was a mourning assembly (*majlis-e 'aza*) in a certain place, and it was raining. The host of the gathering said to me, "You too recite something." At that time I had nothing with me to recite, neither elegy nor book. I knew a few verses of this same *qasida* by heart, and I recited those. At only five or seven verses there was much weeping in the gathering. Arabs, Persians and Indians were all present in that gathering. After the gathering every Persian asked me whose verses these were. They especially praised this verse again and again:

The wage of intercession, the reward of patience, the blood-price:
he asked nothing of anyone except weeping.

He also used to say that once the late Mirza Dabir had added half-lines to this very verse [TN: that is, expanded it into a longer stanza], but he himself did not like them, and said that half-lines of the rank of this verse cannot be added.

## Notes

[1] Hali's note: Anecdote. Someone asked Shams-ul-'Ulama Maulvi Zaka'ullah whether the Mirza had any acquaintance with mathematics too. He said, "Just as much acquaintance as I have with poetry."
