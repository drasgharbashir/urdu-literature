# Lucknow, Refusing Government Service, and Imprisonment

## Arrival in Lucknow

When the Mirza set out from Delhi for Calcutta, he had no intention of stopping on the way. But since some influential people of Lucknow had long wished that the Mirza should come to Lucknow once, on reaching Kanpur it occurred to him to see Lucknow on his way. At that time Nasir-ud-Din Haidar was the ruler and Raushan-ud-Daula the regent (*na'ib-us-saltanat*). The people of Lucknow entertained the Mirza handsomely, and a fitting occasion was arranged for him at Raushan-ud-Daula's house. In that state of distress the Mirza could not complete a *qasida*; but he had written a piece of panegyric prose in the device of *ta'til* (writing only with undotted letters), which survives among his drafts, to present to the regent. But before the meeting the Mirza put forward two conditions which were not accepted: first, that the regent should rise to receive him; second, that he should be excused from presenting a *nazr* (ceremonial offering). For this reason the Mirza left for Calcutta without meeting Raushan-ud-Daula or presenting that prose. But it appears that after returning from Calcutta he wrote from Delhi a *qasida* in praise of Nasir-ud-Din Haidar and had it presented through a friend, and an order was given that five thousand rupees should be paid to him as a reward. Shaikh Imam Bakhsh Nasikh wrote to the Mirza that five thousand had been granted; Raushan-ud-Daula swallowed three thousand, and gave two thousand to the intermediary, saying, send the Mirza whatever of this you think proper. On hearing this the Mirza took some further steps. But three days later the news came that Nasir-ud-Din had died. Then in the time of Wajid 'Ali Shah the Mirza set things moving again, and five hundred rupees a year were fixed for him from there in perpetuity. But only two years had passed when the State was annexed, and that whole record was lost [TN: *daftar gau-khurd ho gaya*, literally "the register was eaten by the cow"].

## Anecdote

At a gathering in Lucknow, when the Mirza was present there, one day there was a discussion about the language of Lucknow and Delhi. A gentleman said to the Mirza that where the people of Delhi say *apne tain* ("myself"), the people of Lucknow say *ap ko*; in your opinion, which is correct, *ap ko* or *apne tain*? The Mirza said: "What you say does seem to be correct; but the difficulty is this. Suppose, for instance, you were to say of me, '*Main ap ko* (you) consider to have the qualities of an angel', and I in reply were to say of myself, '*Main to ap ko* (myself) consider worse than a dog': there would be a serious difficulty. I would be speaking of myself, and you might take it as said of yourself." All present were delighted on hearing this witticism. The Mirza only meant to say that *ap ko* is in any case generally used for the person addressed; if it is used for the speaker as well, confusion will arise on some occasions. He expressed this meaning in this witty form. But this was only a witticism to please the company; otherwise the people of Delhi too often say *ap ko* instead of *apne tain*, and there is nothing peculiar to Lucknow in it.

## Anecdote

Another witticism of the Mirza's of the same kind about language is well known. In Delhi some people make *rath* (a covered carriage) feminine and some masculine. Someone asked the Mirza: "Sir! Is *rath* feminine or masculine?" He said: "Brother! When women are sitting in the *rath*, call it feminine, and when men sit in it, take it as masculine."

## Refusing Government Service

It is written in the *tazkira* *Ab-e Hayat* that in 1842, when Delhi College was established on a new footing, Mr Thomason, Secretary to the Government of India, who afterwards became Lieutenant-Governor of the North-Western Provinces, came to Delhi to examine teachers, and wished that, just as an Arabic teacher was employed in the college at a hundred rupees a month, so a teacher of Persian should be appointed. People mentioned the Mirza, Momin Khan and Maulvi Imam Bakhsh. The Mirza was sent for first of all. The Mirza mounted his palanquin and arrived at the Secretary's camp. The Sahib was informed and at once sent for him. But the Mirza, having got down from the palanquin, stood waiting for the Secretary to come out to receive him according to custom. When much time had passed and the Sahib learned that this was why he did not come in, he came out himself and said to the Mirza: "When you come to the Governor's *darbar* you will be received in this way; but at present you have come for employment, and that treatment cannot be given on this occasion." The Mirza said: "I thought of entering government service so that my honour might be somewhat increased, not so that the honour I already have should be diminished." The Sahib said: "We are bound by the rules." The Mirza said: "Then let me be excused from this post"; and with these words he came away.

## The Imprisonment

The Mirza was much in the habit of playing chess and *chausar* (a dice game). And whenever he played *chausar* he played for some nominal stake. Because of this same *chausar* a most unpleasant thing befell the Mirza in 1264 of the Hijra [TN: 1847 to 1848]. The Mirza himself has described this event briefly in a Persian letter, a translation of which we give here: "The Kotwal (chief of police) was hostile and the Magistrate ignorant; mischief was lying in wait and my star was in its evil turn. Although the Magistrate is the Kotwal's superior, in my case he became subject to the Kotwal and passed the order for my imprisonment. The Sessions Judge, although he was my friend, always treated me with friendship and kindness and often met me informally in company, he too chose to look away and take no notice. An appeal was made to the Sadr (the higher court), but no one listened and the same order was upheld. Then I do not know what was the reason, but when half the term had passed the Magistrate took pity and reported to the Sadr for my release, and the order of release came from there, and the authorities of the Sadr praised him highly for sending such a report. I have heard that the kind-hearted officials reproached the Magistrate severely and informed him of my humility and my free way of life, so that he sent the report for my release of his own accord. Although, since I regard everything as coming from God, and one cannot fight with God, I am free from shame at what has passed and content with whatever is to pass, yet to wish is not against the rule of servanthood. My wish is that I should no longer remain in the world; and if I remain, that I should not remain in India. There is Rum, there is Egypt, there is Iran, there is Baghdad; let even these go: the Ka'ba itself is the refuge of the free, and the threshold of the Mercy to the Worlds [TN: the Prophet] is the resting place of the lovelorn. Let us see when the time will come when I am freed from the prison of helplessness, which is more wearing to the soul than this past imprisonment, and go out into the wilderness without fixing on any destination. This is what befell me, and this is what I long for."

This event was very hard for the Mirza to bear. It is true that during the three months of the six-month sentence that he spent in prison he suffered no hardship of any kind; he lived in the prison in just the same comfort as at home. Food, clothing and all his needs reached him from home as he wished. His friends went to visit him. And he lived only as a detainee, in a separate room of the jail. But since the Mirza had never until then seen the gentlemen and notables of the city treated in this way, he regarded it as a great disgrace. Thus in the *tarkib-band* (stanzaic poem) that he wrote in prison he says:

O confidant! The grief of lasting disgrace is a calamity;
my grief did not come from the pain of the Franks' prison.

The tyranny of enemies leaves the heart with release, but
the taunts of friends were no less to me than an arrow's wound.

The late Nawab Mustafa Khan fully discharged the duty of friendship towards the Mirza at that time. Whatever was spent on the appeal he spent from his own pocket, and for three months he was constantly occupied in consoling him and looking after him in every way. Thus in the same *tarkib-band* he says of the late Nawab:

Why should I drink blood out of grief myself, when for my comfort
it is as if the mercy of God has come in human dress?

There is a gentleman in this city through whose enquiring after me
it is as if my own worth has come into my sight.

Mustafa Khan, who in this affair is my comforter:
if I die, what grief at death, since he will mourn for me?

## Anecdote

When the Mirza was released from prison he came to live in the house of Miyan Kale[1] Sahib. One day he was sitting with Miyan Sahib when someone came and congratulated him on his release from prison. The Mirza said: "What pimp has been released from prison? First I was in the White man's prison, now I am in the Black man's prison." [TN: a pun on *gora* (white, an Englishman) and *kala* (black), the name of his host.]

## The Tarkib-band

In prison the Mirza wrote a Persian *tarkib-band* describing his condition and sent it to his friends. This poem has seven stanzas in all, with twelve couplets in each. The Mirza's relatives and friends had not allowed this poem to be printed in his Persian *Kulliyat*; but some time before his death the Mirza published a collection of his newer verse named *Sabad-e Chin*, and included this *tarkib-band* in it. But *Sabad-e Chin* did not circulate widely, so this *tarkib-band* has come before very few people. Since this *tarkib-band* is among the Mirza's finest occasional poems, some verses from its various stanzas are quoted here:

(From the first stanza)

I wish to begin speaking from fetters to prison;
having torn the veil from my heart's grief, I will make lament my music.

With a melody that makes bloody water drip from the plectrum,
I will set myself to humming poetry.

When I sing, I will ask the criminal for justice;
when I write a *ghazal*, I will take my thought from the informer.

Old friend, do not trouble to come, for here
there is no room for you to knock and for me to open.

The people of the prison gave me a place on their heads and eyes;
how much shall I pride myself on this seat of honour!

Come, captive thieves! There is no faithfulness in the city;
I will make myself your companion and confidant.

(From the third stanza)

Guards, gather together, for I am coming;
open the prison door, for I am coming.

Whoever saw me at his own door used to thank me;
sing a welcome, for I am coming.

I do not know the road, and I fear your throng [TN: reading uncertain];
show me the way from afar, for I am coming.

The traveller on the road of submission does no violence;
why are you so harsh in seizing me? I am coming.

Come, dear ones who reside in this hut,
praise your good fortune, for I am coming.

To the prison gate, to bring me in,
take the trouble of a step, for I am coming.

Since the weighing of words and wisdom are my way,
snatch your share from me, for I am coming.

(From the fourth stanza)

It is as if what belongs to tomorrow has arrived today;
it is as if the sun has risen from the direction of the *qibla*.

The heart and hand I had have given up work;
it is as if the nights and days I had have come to an end.

Since the portion of the world's people from the world is pain and grief,
it is as if my portion from the world has come larger.

My wounding and binding are nothing but the watchman's doing; go!
It is as if these things have come upon me from fate and destiny.

My art cannot be wasted by wounding;
it is as if weariness has come as rouge for the face of art.

The heavens want one man of worth in prison;
it is as if Joseph has come out of Zulaikha's prison.

(From the seventh stanza)

Companions! You are all the pain of my heart hidden from sight;
you are all the soul and spirit of grief-stricken Ghalib.

Praise be to God that you are all in pleasure and joy;
thanks be to God that you are all in pomp and glory.

I lie in blood, and I see that you all see it all;
I am wounded at heart, and I know that you all know it all.

Between us there was a covenant of love and faithfulness;
I broke it, so that you might all be sure to come out well.

Not one day did you say out of love, "How is so-and-so?"
Yet say, at least out of kindness, how you all are.

If you cannot be healers, a prayer is enough;
if you have no heart, you are all lords of a tongue.

Seven stanzas they are that I have written in fetters;
copy them out, and look, and read them all.

I would not be one whom you remember in every assembly;
I hope that you will remember me in the assembly of poetry.

## Notes

[1] Hali's note: Hazrat Muhammad Nasir-ud-Din, known as Miyan Kale Sahib, was the spiritual guide (*shaikh*) of the late Bahadur Shah and the grandson of Maulana Fakhr-ud-Din, may his secret be sanctified. The Mirza lived in his house for a long time. He had the greatest affection for the Mirza, and it was through him that the Mirza's connection with the Fort came about.
