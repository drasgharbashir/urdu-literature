# Home Life, Last Years and Death

## Domestic Relations

The Mirza's wife, who was the daughter of Ilahi Bakhsh Khan Ma'ruf, was most devout and God-fearing and strictly observant of prayer and fasting. As careless as the Mirza was in religious matters, so observant of the religious ordinances was his wife; so much so that the wife's vessels for eating and drinking were kept separate from the husband's. In spite of this the wife left nothing undone in serving her husband and looking after him. The Mirza always lived in the men's quarters of the house, but his meals, his medicines and cooling drinks, his winter clothing and the like were all arranged from the women's quarters. As long as the Mirza had the strength to walk about, he always went into the house once at a fixed time. And he treated his wife and all her relatives very well, and cared for their needs and expenses more than for his own life. But since playfulness and wit had been given him with his first nourishment, things often slipped from his tongue and pen about his wife which a stranger might take as aversion or indifference.

## Anecdote

Someone wrote to the Mirza of the death of the second wife of a pupil named Umrao Singh, and wrote too that he has little children; now, if he does not marry a third time, what is he to do, and how are the children to be brought up? The Mirza writes in reply: "At Umrao Singh's case I feel pity for him and envy for myself. Good God!!! There is one man whose fetters have been cut twice over, and here am I, with the noose of the gallows round my neck for fifty-one years, and neither does the noose break nor does the breath leave me. Make him understand: brother, I will bring up your children; why do you get caught in the calamity?" He always described domestic ties, in earnest or in jest, as a grievous affliction.

## Anecdote

One winter day a parrot's cage was set before him. Because of the cold the parrot sat with its face hidden in its feathers. Seeing it the Mirza said: "Master Mitthu! You have neither wife nor children; what care makes you sit with your head bowed like this?"

## Anecdote

Once the Mirza wished to change house. He went and saw a house himself; he liked its reception hall, but could not see the women's quarters himself. On coming home he sent his wife to see it. When she came back he asked her whether she liked it or not. She said, "People say there is a calamity (*bala*, an evil spirit) in it." The Mirza said, "Is there any calamity in the world greater than you?"

Here, as the occasion requires, a *qit'a* and a *ruba'i* of the Mirza's are given:

## Qit'a

Granted that on the Day of Resurrection, as you said,
they will set my dark body over the mouth of Hell [TN: a gloss printed above "dark body" is illegible];

yet in that narrow place of affliction there will not be
the struggle with a wife over the demand for bread and clothes;

yet in that place of hardship there will not be
the clamour of the moneylender's unjust demands.

## Ruba'i

O you who have set your face towards the road to the Ka'ba,
I know that you have some chosen longing;

from the way you are striding so fast, I know
you have at home a wife of quarrelsome temper.

## Anecdote

The Mirza was at the mercy of his playful nature, and never missed an occasion for a jest.

Even before Mirza Ilahi Bakhsh Khan Ma'ruf, in whose presence, because of his holiness and saintliness, his own elder brother sat with knees folded in respect, and who, being the Mirza's father-in-law, was his *qibla* and Ka'ba, the Mirza would not refrain from his playfulness. He also used to take disciples (*murid*s), and when many disciples had gathered he would have the genealogy (*shajra*) of all the *shaikh*s of his spiritual line written out and distribute a copy to each of them. He gave the Mirza the genealogy to copy. He copied it in this way: he wrote one name, left out the second, wrote the third again, dropped the fourth again. In short, having made many omissions and deletions in this way, he went and handed the copy and the original to him. On seeing it he was very angry: "What outrage is this?" The Mirza said: "Sir! Pay no attention to it; a genealogy is really a ladder for reaching God; and if a rung of the ladder is taken out here and there, no great harm is done; a man can climb up by stretching a little." Hearing this he was much vexed, tore up that copy and had it copied by someone else; and the Mirza was freed from that trouble for ever.

## The Longing for Death

Either because his life had really been spent in afflictions and hardships, or because unpleasant circumstances affected him very strongly, at the end of his life the Mirza often longed for death. Every year he would work out a chronogram for his death and think that this year he would certainly die.

## Anecdote

In 1277 of the Hijra [TN: 1860 to 1861] he composed this chronogram for his death: *Ghalib murd* ("Ghalib died"). Before this several chronograms had proved wrong. He mentioned this chronogram to Munshi Jawahar Singh, whose pen name was Jauhar and who was one of the Mirza's intimates. He said, "Sir! God willing, this chronogram too will prove wrong." The Mirza said, "Look here, sir, do not let such an omen pass your lips; if this chronogram does not come true, I shall dash my head and die."

## Anecdote

Once there was a severe epidemic in the city. Mir Mahdi Husain Majruh enquired, "Sir! Has the epidemic left the city, or is it still there?" In reply he writes: "My friend, what epidemic? When it could not kill an old man of seventy and an old woman of seventy, fie upon such an epidemic!" Many other sayings and stories of this kind are reported of him, from which it can be judged how much he longed for death at the end of his life.

## His State in Old Age

For several years before his death his walking about had stopped altogether. He lay on his bed most of the time. He took hardly any food. His bowels moved only every six or seven days. A commode stood near the bed, somewhat out of sight. When he felt the need, a curtain would be drawn; without the help of any servant he would take off his clothes and, still sitting, shuffle along to the commode. To go from the bed to the commode, to climb onto the commode, to sit on it for a long time, and then to come down from the commode to the bed, was like completing a long stage of a journey. But even in this state he went on answering letters regularly, either writing them himself as he lay on his bed, or dictating to someone else who wrote them down.

## His Last Illness

A few days before his death unconsciousness came over him. After a watch or two of the day he would come round for a few minutes, then lose consciousness again. Perhaps one day before the day of his death I went to visit him; he had just come round after several watches, and was dictating a reply to a letter from the late Nawab 'Ala'-ud-Din Ahmad Khan. He had asked from Loharu how he was; in reply he dictated a sentence and a Persian verse, probably by Shaikh Sa'di. The sentence was: "Why do you ask me how I am? Ask the neighbours in a day or two." I do not remember the first half-line of the verse; the second was: "It showed no kindness at all; may your head be safe." [TN: reading uncertain.] Before his death this verse was often on his tongue:

The last breath is ready on the road;
dear ones, now there is God, only God.

## The Date of His Death

At last, on the 2nd of Zi-Qa'da 1285 of the Hijra and the 15th of February 1869, at the age of seventy-three years and four months, he departed from the world, and was buried at the shrine of Hazrat Sultan Nizam-ud-Din, may his secret be sanctified, at the foot of his father-in-law's grave. The chronograms of his death, which went on being printed in the Urdu newspapers of India for a long time, are beyond counting; only one chronogram, on which ten or twelve people hit independently, is worth remembering (namely *Ah Ghalib bimurd*, "Alas, Ghalib died", 1285), which different people set in different ways in *qit'a*s. Besides chronograms, Mirza Qurban 'Ali Beg Salik, Mir Mahdi Husain Majruh and the compiler of this book wrote elegies (*marsiya*s) on the Mirza in Urdu, and Munshi Hargopal Tafta in Persian, which were printed and published at that time.

## The Funeral Prayer

At the Mirza's funeral, when the prayer was said outside the Delhi Gate, the present writer too was present; and most of the notables and distinguished men of the city, such as Nawab Ziya-ud-Din Ahmad Khan, Nawab Muhammad Mustafa Khan, Hakim Ahsanullah Khan and others, and people of both sects, the Sunnis and the Imamis (Shi'as), took part in following the bier. Sayyid Safdar Sultan, grandson of Bakhshi Mahmud Khan, said to the late Nawab Ziya-ud-Din Ahmad Khan that the Mirza was a Shi'a; let us be permitted to prepare and shroud him according to our rite. But the Nawab did not agree, and all the rites were performed according to the Sunni way. There is no doubt that no one could know his real religious views better than the Nawab; but in our view it would have been better if Shi'as and Sunnis had said the funeral prayer over him together, or separately, and just as in his life his conduct had been the same towards both Sunnis and Shi'as, so after his death both sects had shared in paying him his due.
