# Food, Mangoes and Wine

## Food

The Mirza's favourite food was nothing but meat. He could not do without meat for a single meal; so much so that even on the day of taking a purgative he never ate *khichri* (rice and lentils) or *shola* (rice gruel). Towards the end his diet had become very small. In the morning he usually drank almond syrup. In the midday meal that came for him from the house there was only a quarter-seer of meat stewed as *qorma*: in one bowl the pieces of meat, in another the thick gravy or broth, in a small bowl the outer skin of one thin *phulka* bread soaked in broth, in another small bowl sometimes the yolk of an egg, in another small bowl two or three pice-weights of curd, and in the evening some *shami kabab*s or skewered kababs; that was all, and his diet was nothing more than this.

## Anecdote

One day the midday meal came and the cloth was spread; there were many dishes, but very little food. The Mirza smiled and said: "If you look at the number of dishes, my table seems the table of Yazid; and if you look at the amount of food, that of Bayazid." [TN: a pun: Yazid, the caliph, stands for worldly plenty; Bayazid Bistami, the Sufi, for ascetic abstinence.]

## Fondness for Mangoes

Among fruits he was extremely fond of the mango. In the mango season his friends sent him fine mangoes from far and near; and he himself had mangoes sent by pressing some of his friends again and again. Among his Persian letters is one which he probably wrote during his stay in Calcutta to the Sahib who was trustee (*mutawalli*) of the Imambara, asking for mangoes. In it he writes: "I am a slave to my belly [TN: reading uncertain], and somewhat weak; I seek both the adornment of the table and the comfort of the soul. The wise know that both these qualities are in the mango; and the people of Calcutta hold that the realm of the mango is the port of Hooghly. Yes: the mango from Hooghly, and the rose from the garden; the giving from Your Honour, and the thanks from me. Longing reckons that before the end of the season I shall pass two or three times through the thoughts of my benefactor; and greed laments: God forbid that I should be content with so small a share of enjoyment!" [TN: Persian in the original.]

## Anecdote

One day in the mango season the late Bahadur Shah was walking in the Hayat Bakhsh garden or the Mahtab garden with a few companions, among whom was the Mirza. The mango trees were laden with mangoes of every colour. The mangoes from here could be had by no one but the King, the princes or the Begams. The Mirza kept looking intently at the mangoes. The King asked, "Mirza, what are you looking at so intently?" The Mirza folded his hands and replied: "My Lord and Guide, a certain sage has said, 'On every grain it is plainly written: this is for so-and-so, son of so-and-so, son of so-and-so.' I am looking to see whether my name and the names of my father and grandfather are written on any grain." The King smiled, and that same day sent the Mirza a carrying-pole (*bahangi*) load of fine mangoes.

## Anecdote

Hakim Raziuddin Khan, who was a very close friend of the Mirza's, did not care for mangoes. One day he was sitting on the verandah of the Mirza's house, and the Mirza too was there. A donkey-man passed through the lane with his donkeys. Mango peels were lying there; a donkey sniffed them and left them. The Hakim said, "You see, the mango is a thing that even a donkey does not eat." The Mirza said, "Certainly a donkey does not eat it."

The Mirza could never get enough of mangoes. The people of the city sent them as gifts, he had them brought from the bazaar himself, and mangoes came from far away as presents from other places; but His Honour's appetite was never satisfied.

## Anecdote

The late Nawab Mustafa Khan used to relate that at a gathering Maulana Fazl-e Haq, the Mirza and other friends had assembled, and each was giving his opinion on what qualities a mango ought to have. When everyone had had his say, Maulana Fazl-e Haq said to the Mirza, "You too give your opinion." The Mirza said, "My friend, in my view a mango should have only two things: it should be sweet, and there should be plenty of it." All present burst out laughing.

## The Habit of Drinking

For a long time the Mirza had been in the habit of drinking a little at night at bedtime. He never drank more than the amount he had fixed. The key of the box in which the bottles were kept stayed with the steward (*daroga*), and he was under strict orders: if at night, when I am merry, the thought of drinking more comes to me, on no account do as I say, and do not give me the key. It often happened that he asked for the key at night and in the irritation of drink abused the steward roundly; but the steward was most loyal and never gave him the key. In the first place he drank very little; second, he mixed two or three parts of rose-water with it, which lessened its heat and strength. Thus he says in one place:

May Ghalib's mind be at ease, for it is his habit
to mix rose-water with clear wine.

But in spite of so much care and moderation, the habit of this infidel intoxicant in the end did great harm to the Mirza's health, and all his Urdu letters are full of complaints about it.

The Mirza's particular pupils and friends, with whom he was on the most informal terms, often went and sat with him in the evening; and at that time, in a pleasant glow, the Mirza used to talk most delightfully.

## Anecdote

One day Mir Mahdi Majruh was sitting there, and the Mirza lay groaning on his bed. Mir Mahdi began to press his feet. The Mirza said, "My friend, you are a descendant of the Prophet (*sayyid-zada*); why do you make me a sinner?" He would not listen, and said, "If that is what you think, then pay me the wages for pressing your feet." The Mirza said, "Yes, I have no objection to that." When he had finished pressing his feet he asked for his wages. The Mirza said, "Brother, what wages? You pressed my feet; I pressed (kept back) your money; the account is square." [TN: a pun on *dabna*, "to press, massage" and "to withhold".]

## Anecdote

One day, before sunset, the Mirza was eating his evening meal, and there was nothing in it but *shami kabab*s. I too was present and was sitting before him, waving the flies away with a handkerchief. The Mirza said, "You are taking this trouble in vain; I shall not give you any of these kababs." Then he himself told this story: "At the table of Nawab 'Abdul-Ahad Khan every kind of dish was laid out for his companions, relatives and friends; but for himself only one thing was always prepared, and he ate nothing else. One day a *muza'far* (saffron rice) had been cooked for him, and that was set before him. Among his companions was a Dom (a musician) who was much indulged, and he was present at table at the time. The Nawab called for an empty plate to give him some food. There was a delay in its coming. The Nawab went on eating and asked again and again for an empty plate. That companion began to wave a handkerchief before the Nawab and said, 'What will Your Honour do with another plate? This one is about to be empty now.' The Nawab was delighted at this remark and pushed that very plate towards him."

## Anecdote

Once at night, lying on his bed, he was looking at the sky. Seeing the apparent disorder and scattering of the stars, he said: "Whatever is done by someone who follows only his own will is usually ill-shaped. Just look at the stars, in what confusion they are scattered! There is no symmetry, no arrangement, no creeper, no flower pattern; but the King is absolute, and no one can breathe a word."

## Anecdote

One day the late Sayyid Sardar Mirza came in the evening. When, after staying a little while, he rose to go, the Mirza himself took the candlestick in his hand and, shuffling along, came to the edge of the floor-covering, so that he might see his shoes in the light and put them on. He said, "Revered sir, why have you taken this trouble? I would have put on my shoes myself." The Mirza said: "I have not brought the candlestick to show you your shoes, but so that you should not go off wearing mine."

Although as a poet he praised wine here and there, in his beliefs he considered it very wrong, and was deeply ashamed of this habit of his. In spite of this he never concealed it.

## Anecdote

His witty remarks about wine are very well known. A man spoke very strongly against wine in his presence and said that the prayer of a wine-drinker is not accepted. The Mirza said, "My friend, if a man has wine, what else does he need to pray for?"

## Anecdote

He begins one letter thus: "Without wine the pen does not run in my hand; for the present, the air [cries]: O smokeless fire, where are you? [TN: Persian verse; reading uncertain.] Mir Mahdi! It is morning. The cold is severe. A brazier is set before me. I write two words and warm my hands. There is heat in the fire, granted; but where is that liquid fire which, when two draughts had been drunk, ran at once through every vein and nerve? The heart grew strong, the brain grew bright, the rational soul was brought to ecstasy. A servant of the Cupbearer of Kausar [TN: 'Ali, who in Muslim belief will give drink from the heavenly pool of Kausar], and thirsty-lipped!!! Alas, the outrage! Alas, the outrage!" This letter was written after the Mutiny, at the time when his pension and everything else was stopped and, because of hardship and want, there was no drinking or offering of drink.

## Anecdote

Mir Mahdi Majruh sent a letter from Jaipur and wrote of the sherbet of several hundred maunds of sugar-candy that had been made there for the guests at some ceremony; in reply the Mirza writes: "Mir Ahmad Husain and Mirza Qurban Beg read your letter, and at the relish of sherbet made of seven hundred maunds of sugar-candy both their mouths watered. The talk was not of pure wine; otherwise my heart too would have gone out of its place." [TN: Persian in the original.]

The themes about wine that the Mirza has used in his *ghazal*s, *qasida*s, *qit'a*s and *ruba'i*s will not be fewer than those of Khwaja Hafiz and 'Umar Khayyam; here one verse of an Urdu *ghazal*, one of a Persian *ghazal* and one Persian *ruba'i* are given:

Do not be stingy with wine today for the sake of tomorrow;
this is an ill thought of the Cupbearer of Kausar.

See my shame, that on my day of reckoning they found nothing in my record [TN: the key word is unclear in the print]
but whole fasts broken with wine.

## Ruba'i

Ghalib, though in poetry he has no equal [TN: the first two lines are unclear in the print and read with doubt],
from the intoxication of sense there is nothing in his head.

You want wine, and free, and paid on the spot, and plenty besides!!!
This wine-seller is not the Cupbearer of Kausar.
