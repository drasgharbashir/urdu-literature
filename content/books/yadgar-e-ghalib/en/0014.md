# Judging Poetry, Commendations and a Critical Eye

## Giving Praise to Poetry

There is a general custom in our society that when a man recites his verse, every one of his couplets, good or bad, receives equal applause and acclaim, and no distinction is made between good and bad verse; the Mirza's habit was entirely contrary to this. However honoured and respected a man might be, the Mirza would never praise any verse of his unless he really liked it. At the end of his life his deafness had reached the extreme, but earlier it was not so; he could hear conversation and verse spoken in a somewhat raised voice; but until a verse pierced his heart he would not stir an inch. Some of his contemporaries were offended by this, and for this reason found fault with his poetry. But the Mirza, although his nature was most peace-loving, never gave up the manner he had adopted of giving praise to verse.

## Fairness

But a verse that did pierce his heart he would praise in a way that reached the limit of exaggeration. In truth he did not do this to please anyone; rather his love of poetry carried him away. Shaikh Ibrahim Zauq, with whom the Mirza is said to have had a rivalry: one day, when the Mirza was busy at chess, the late Munshi Ghulam 'Ali Khan recited this verse of Zauq's to someone else:

Now in distress we say that we shall die;
if even in death we find no peace, where shall we go?

The late Khan used to say that a murmur of it reached the Mirza's ear too; at once he left the chess and said to me, "Brother, what did you recite?" I recited the verse again. He asked, "Whose verse is it?" I said, "Zauq's." On hearing this he was greatly astonished, and made me recite it again and again, swaying his head with delight. We too see that the Mirza has mentioned this verse here and there in his Urdu letters. Where he has given examples of fine verse, he has certainly written this one. In the same way, when he heard this verse of Momin Khan's:

You are with me, as it were,
when no one else is there,

he praised it greatly and said, "If only Momin Khan would take my whole *diwan* and give me this one verse." This verse too he has quoted in several of his letters. In the same way he has written this verse of Sauda's in one place:

I would take you and show you the market of Egypt,
but there is no buyer there for costly goods.

At a gathering he kept reciting this verse of Nawab Mirza Khan Dagh, and was enraptured by it:

Setting a candle before her bright face, she says:
"Let us see whether the moth goes that way or comes this way."

Sometimes he was so moved by his pupils' verse that he praised it with excessive exaggeration, perhaps to encourage them. At the end of his life, on seeing a *ghazal* by one of his pupils, he praised it beyond measure and said, "If I were still capable of envy, you would be the envied and I the envier."

## The Manner of Writing Commendations

The Mirza received countless requests for commendations (*taqriz*s). And, as is plain, very few books really deserve praise. Since the Mirza's nature was peace-loving and he neither took offence nor gave it, he did not refuse anyone; but he had adopted such a manner of writing commendations that nothing should be contrary to the truth and yet the author should be pleased. Much of it would be taken up by a preamble, or by a description of the author's person and character, or of his love and friendship, or by the mention of other fine and pure matters. At the end he would write a few sentences about the book which were not without truth and were enough to please the author. For this reason it sometimes happened that people complained to the Mirza that you have been sparing in your praise.

## The Commendation of Tafta's Diwan

When the Mirza wrote and sent the commendation of Munshi Hargopal Tafta's *diwan*, which is in Ghalib's collected prose, he too made a complaint of this kind. The Mirza writes in reply: "What can I do? I cannot give up my way. I do not know the manner of the Indian writers of Persian, to begin babbling just like the bards (*bhat*s). Look at my *qasida*s: you will find many verses of prelude and fewer verses of praise. In prose too it is the same. Look at the commendation of Nawab Mustafa Khan's *tazkira*: how much praise of him is there? Look at the preface to the *diwan* of Mirza Rahimuddin Bahadur, whose pen name is Haya. Look at the commendation I wrote, at the request of John Jacob Bahadur, for the printing of Hafiz's *diwan*: his name and praise come in only one couplet, and the rest of the prose is about quite other matters. By God, if I had written the preface to the *diwan* of some prince or nobleman's son, I would not have praised him as much as I have praised you. If you knew me and my way, you would think this much praise a great deal. In short, I have humoured you, and changed one phrase about your name and written another phrase in its place. More than that is not my way. Evidently you do not think for yourself, and are led astray by certain gentlemen. Those gentlemen will most likely call this verse and prose nonsense, because their ears are not familiar with this sound. People who count Qatil among good writers, how will they recognise the excellence of verse and prose?"

## The Commendation of the Edition of the A'in-e Akbari

When (Sir) Sayyid Ahmad Khan, with great toil and sweat, edited the *A'in-e Akbari*, the notables of Delhi wrote commendations on it in prose, and the Mirza wrote a *masnavi* in verse, which is in his collected works. Although the Mirza valued Sir Sayyid's regard very highly[1], and treated him and his family as his own kin, yet since the Mirza did not like Abu'l-Fazl's style of writing, considered the institutions (*a'in*s) described in that book worthless beside the institutions of the present age, and, as he himself admitted, had no taste for history at all, he thought the editing of the *A'in-e Akbari* a useless task. Whether this opinion of his was right or wrong, he could not refrain from expressing in the commendation what he thought of the *A'in-e Akbari* and its editing. So we quote here a few verses from the beginning of that *masnavi*:

Good news to friends, that this ancient book
has, through the fortune of the Sayyid, found the door opened.

The eye has become seeing and the arm strong;
oldness has put on the robe of the new.

But that his judgement should be spent on correcting the *A'in* [TN: first word uncertain]
is a shame and disgrace to his lofty spirit.

He bound his heart to a task and made himself happy;
he set free a slave called Mubarak[2].

One who cannot praise his true worth
knows how to praise him for this work of his.

A work such as this, whose root is this,
only one whose rule is hypocrisy will praise.

I, who am the enemy of the rule of hypocrisy,
I myself know the measure of faithfulness.

If I do not say "Bravo!" to him for this work,
it is fitting that I should seek a "Bravo!" for myself.

After this he has described to some extent the institutions, laws and inventions of the English, and written that before these things all the earlier institutions have become last year's almanac; after this he writes:

If you say its style of writing is pleasing,
it is pleasing, no more than whatever you seek.

Every pleasing thing has had something more pleasing;
if there is a head, there has been a crown too.

Do not count the Bountiful Source a miser:
light still pours, the dates still fall, from that palm[3] [TN: an interlinear gloss reads *hanoz* (still); reading uncertain].

To cherish the dead is not a blessed work;
say yourself: that too is nothing but talk.

Ghalib, the way of silence is charming;
though you have spoken well, not to speak is also good.

In the world, devotion to the Sayyid is your religion;
pass beyond praise: prayer is your way.

For this embodiment of splendour and learning,
Sayyid Ahmad Khan, 'Arif Jang,

whatever he desires of God, may it be present;
may happy fortune be his servant.

Since in this commendation the *A'in-e Akbari* had been disparaged, and no credit had been given for the very useful work Sir Sayyid had done, but on the contrary it had been made out to be useless, he did not have the Mirza's commendation printed at the end of the *A'in-e Akbari*.

## A Critical Eye

A great proof of the Mirza's perceptiveness and lofty nature is that, although he was surrounded by a society in which to step one pace beyond the imitation of the ancients was considered unlawful, he followed a critical path in his art and never blindly imitated those who had gone before. That is why the fame and renown of the compiler of *Burhan-e Qati'* did not prevent him from faulting it. He writes in one place: "God has given us a knowing heart and a seeing eye so that we may accept the work of knowledge and sight from these two jewels, and accept nothing we look upon except by the leave of knowledge. Mastership and discipleship are not the relation of spiritual guide and disciple, in which faith alone is enough, so that with the well-known saying, 'My guide is a straw, but my faith is enough', one may be safe from questioning." [TN: Persian in the original.] Although he showed the greatest respect for the famous poets of Iran and always mentioned them with honour and reverence, he did not imitate them blindly. In matters that depend on hearing and transmission he accepted their word without question; but in matters that depend on reason and understanding he did not think it right to follow them. In a letter he quotes this opening couplet of Hazin's, whom he considered a very great master: "From the raiding of that delicate rider, still, from the grass the finger of 'Beware!' sprouts still." Then he writes: "In this opening couplet one *hanoz* (still) is redundant and pointless; it cannot be an authority for a follower; it is plainly wrong; it is a flaw; it is a fault; who will follow it? Hazin was a man; if this opening couplet were Gabriel's, do not take it as an authority, and do not follow it." In a letter he writes to Munshi Hargopal: "Do not think that whatever the ancients have written is the truth; were not fools born in those days too?"

## Love of Truth

If anyone made a correct objection to the Mirza's verse, or made some good change in a verse of his, he would accept it at once and change the verse. In the *masnavi* *Dard-o-Dagh* he had a half-line: "He became a pig and set about clawing (*panja zadan*)." When the Mirza sent this *masnavi* to Tufa-e Natiq Makrani, he wrote to the Mirza: "A pig has hooves, not claws; if among the masters it is permissible to use hoof and claw in the same sense, kindly let me know." The Mirza wrote back plainly that if your letter had arrived before the printing of my Persian collected works I would have changed this word and made the half-line thus: "He became a pig and set about wickedness (*bad-nafasi*)." And so, when the Mirza's works were printed a second time, he made the half-line just so.

This is a verse from the prelude of one of the Mirza's Persian *qasida*s:

Even so, within the veil of the unseen they have fixed being (*subut*),
with an existence which as external entities they do not have.

The Mirza himself told me that he had written *numud* (appearance) in place of *subut* (fixed being). When he recited this verse to Maulvi Fazl-e Haq, he said that the word *numud* is unsuitable for the *a'yan-e sabita* (the fixed archetypes); put *subut* in its place. So in the second edition he put *subut* instead of *numud*. In the same way the first half-line of the opening couplet of a *qasida* is this:

'Id-e Azha has come at the start of winter;

The Mirza had first written *'Id-e Qurban*; then it was changed to *'Id-e Azha* at the suggestion of the late Nawab Mustafa Khan [TN: both names mean the Feast of Sacrifice]. And this although the said Nawab himself consulted the Mirza about his verse and, after the late Momin, always showed his verse to him. The purpose of relating these things is not to show the public the Mirza's slips, but to show in his person the noble quality of fairness and love of truth, and that faculty without which a man can never advance. Those who lack the capacity to admit their own errors cannot possibly advance in their art.
