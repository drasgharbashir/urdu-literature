# The Revolt of 1857 and the Rampur Pension

## The Events of the Mutiny and the Book Dastambu

During the Mutiny the Mirza did not leave Delhi, nor even his house. As soon as the turmoil of the rebellion arose he shut the door of his house, and in the corner of solitude began to write an account of the Mutiny.

After the conquest of Delhi a guard had been posted, on behalf of the Maharaja of Patiala, for the protection of the houses of the late Hakim Mahmud Khan and his neighbours, among whom the Mirza was one; so they were safe from the plundering of the victorious soldiers. Even so he had to suffer hardships of every kind. The Mirza's younger brother had gone mad at the age of thirty [TN: reading of the number uncertain] and remained in that state to his last breath; when the Mirza settled in Delhi he had brought him here with him too. His house was about two thousand paces from the Mirza's. A doorkeeper and a maidservant, both of them aged, lived with him. When Delhi was conquered, the city emptied of its people and the roads were closed, the Mirza began to be deeply anxious about his brother. He had no news at all of whether his brother was eating, drinking, sleeping, dead or alive. One day news came that some soldiers had broken into Mirza Yusuf's house too, and had taken whatever goods they found. Then one day that same old doorkeeper who lived at Mirza Yusuf's entrance brought the news that after five days in a severe fever Mirza Yusuf had died that night, after midnight. At that time neither could cloth for a shroud be had in the bazaar, nor was there any trace of a washer of the dead or a gravedigger, nor was it possible to go from the city to the graveyard; but the Mirza's neighbours helped him greatly. They took with them a soldier of the Patiala force who had been posted for protection, and two of the Mirza's men, and, taking two white sheets from the Mirza's house, went to Mirza Yusuf's house. And after washing, preparing and shrouding the body, they buried him in the courtyard of the mosque that was near the house. At this point in *Dastambu* the Mirza wrote these verses:

Alas, that in a span of thrice twenty[1] years
he lived thirty years happy and thirty unhappy.

In the earth, he had not even a brick for his pillow;
nothing but dust was written in his fate.

O God, grant forgiveness to this dead man,
who in life saw no comfort.

Send an angel to console him;
send his soul to everlasting Paradise. [TN: several words of this Persian elegy are blotted in the print and read with some doubt.]

And he wrote the chronogram of his brother's death thus:

About the year of the death of the wronged Mirza Yusuf,
who lived in the world a stranger to himself,

someone in company was enquiring of me;
I heaved a sigh (*ahe*) and said: "Alas, the madman!"

In this the value of the word *ahe* is subtracted from *darigh diwana* ("Alas, the madman"). [TN: the abjad value of *darigh diwana*, 1289, less that of *ahe*, 16, gives 1273, the Hijri year of 1857.]

One day some White soldiers broke into the Mirza's house too. The Raja's soldiers tried hard to stop them, but they paid no attention. The Mirza writes in *Dastambu* that, out of their good nature, they did not touch the goods of the house at all; but they took me, the two children, and two or three servants, together with a few neighbours, before Colonel Brown, who was staying in the house of Haji Qutb-ud-Din the merchant near my house. Colonel Brown asked about us with great gentleness and humanity, and dismissed us with a smile.

## Anecdote

It is said that when the Mirza went before Colonel Brown he was wearing a *papakh* (a tall Persian cap of lambskin). Seeing the Mirza's unfamiliar dress, he asked, "Well, you Musalman?" The Mirza said, "Half." The Colonel said, "What does that mean?" The Mirza said, "I drink wine; I don't eat pork." Hearing this the Colonel began to laugh. Then the Mirza showed him the letter of the Secretary of State for India which had come as an acknowledgement and reply to his *qasida* in praise of Her Majesty the Queen. The Colonel said, "Why did you not present yourself on the Ridge after the government's victory?" The Mirza said, "I was the officer of four palanquin-bearers; all four left me and ran away; how could I present myself?" The Colonel dismissed the Mirza and all his companions with great kindness.

At this point the Mirza writes in his book *Dastambu*: "To hide the truth is not the way of the free. I am half a Muslim; just as I am free from the bonds of creed and community, so I am free from the fear of ill repute and disgrace. For a long time it had been my habit to eat and drink nothing at night except French[2]; and if I did not get it I could not sleep. If the manly, God-loving, God-knowing, large-hearted Mahesh Das had not sent me Indian wine, which was like French in colour and better than it in fragrance, I should never have survived." After this he wrote this *ruba'i*:

## Ruba'i

For long my heart was seeking alms[3] at every door,
seeking one or two cups of pure wine.

The wise Mahesh Das bestowed on me
the water[4] that Alexander sought for himself.

Since the city was then empty of Muslims, the Mirza had no one left to share his troubles except his Hindu friends, who kept coming to see him and comforted him in every way. The Mirza had only two sources of livelihood, the government pension and the salary from the Fort, and both these sources had been cut off. All the Muslim notables of the city, who were the Mirza's friends and relatives, were caught up in their own troubles. Besides, whatever jewellery or other valuables his wife had in the house had been sent elsewhere to be buried and hidden when the plundering of the city began; and from there the victorious soldiery dug it all out and took it. But even in this state of hardship and want the Mirza did not dismiss any of his many servants, and the servants too shared equally in whatever, good or bad, befell him and his family. Besides the servants, those people to whom the Mirza had always been generous in times of peace pestered him even in this state, and willy-nilly the Mirza had to look after them too. The Mirza writes: "In this time of destitution I sold and ate up all the clothes, wraps and bedding that were in the house; as if other people ate bread and I ate cloth." After this he ends the book thus: "How long shall the pen be worn out writing this children's plaything, the book *Dastambu*? The state that now confronts me is plain: its end is either death or begging. In the first case this tale is sure to remain unfinished; and in the second, what else can the outcome be than this: driven away from one shop, and a cowrie or a pice got from some door? So nothing remains to be written in it now except my own humiliation and disgrace. Even if the old pension is restored, I do not see things going on; and if it is not, then all is over. The difficulty is that in either case, since the air and water of this city no longer seem to suit broken hearts, I shall certainly have to leave the city and go and rest in some other settlement."

## The Rampur Pension

For two years after the Mutiny this remained the Mirza's state. But after two years the late Nawab Yusuf 'Ali Khan, ruler of Rampur, fixed a hundred rupees a month for the Mirza in perpetuity, which the late Nawab Kalb-e 'Ali Khan also continued as before up to the Mirza's last breath. And three years after the Mutiny, when the Mirza was proved innocent of every charge, the government pension too was restored.

## Anecdote

When Nawab Yusuf 'Ali Khan died and the Mirza went to Rampur to offer condolence, a few days later the late Nawab Kalb-e 'Ali Khan had to go to Bareilly to meet His Honour the Lieutenant-Governor. The Mirza too was present at his departure. As he was leaving, the Nawab said to the Mirza, in the usual way, "I commit you to God." The Mirza said, "Your Highness! God has committed me to you, and you turn it round and commit me to God."

## Notes

[1] Hali's note: *Thah bist* (thrice twenty), that is, sixty. In the second half-line *sih dah* (thrice ten) means thirty.

[2] Hali's note: *Farinch* is a thing connected with France, and here French wine is meant.

[3] Gloss printed above the word in the original: *bhik* (alms).

[4] Gloss printed above the word in the original: that is, the water of life.
