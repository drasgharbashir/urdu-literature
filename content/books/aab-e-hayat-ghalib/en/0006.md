# Persian Works (continued)

## Persian Works (continued)

In Calcutta there were many Iranians and great scholars and learned men, but it is a pity that the Mirza's accomplishment did not meet there with the esteem that befitted his standing. In truth he ought to have been esteemed, and certainly would have been, but a chance twist came in the way. The story of it is this. At some gathering the Mirza recited a Persian *ghazal*, and some people objected to one word in it; the objection was based on a rule that Mirza Qatil wrote in one of his treatises. Hearing it, the Mirza said: "Who is Qatil? And what have I to do with Mirza Qatil? He was a Khatri of Faridabad; I recognise no one but those whose mother tongue it is." Those people were mostly pupils of Mirza Qatil, so they shut their eyes to the rules of hospitality, and an uproar arose among high and low. The Mirza was astonished, and with the idea that this trouble might somehow be calmed, he chose the path of peace and wrote a *masnavi*, and there is no doubt that in it he did full justice to the poet's art. He put the whole story of the quarrel into verse with great skill, answered the objection with authorities, and on his own side fully discharged the duty of apology with fitting humility. But the greater pity is that when the *masnavi* was read in his opponents' gathering, instead of acknowledging its merit or apologising to their guest for their excesses, one of them asked on purpose, "What is the name of this *masnavi*?" It turned out to be *Bad-e Mukhalif* ("The Contrary Wind"). Another recited the sentence from the *Gulistan*: "A contrary wind twisted in the belly of one of the pious," and they all laughed.

Anecdote: there was a *mushaira* in Delhi, and the Mirza recited a Persian *ghazal* of his. Mufti Sadr-ud-Din Khan Sahib and Maulvi Imam Bakhsh Sahbai were present at the gathering. When Mirza Sahib recited this line:

"In a valley where Khizr's staff has fallen asleep"

at Maulvi Sahbai's prompting the Mufti Sahib said that there was some question about "the staff has fallen asleep". Mirza Sahib said: "Sir, I am of Indian birth, so you have caught hold of my staff; the staff of that man of Shiraz was not caught hold of:

'At the first onslaught the Shaikh's staff fell asleep.'" [TN: The man of Shiraz is Sa'di.]

He said that there was no question about the idiom itself; the question was whether it was fitting in this place.

Anecdote: once the Mirza fell deep into debt. His creditors brought suits, and he was summoned to answer them. It was the Mufti Sahib's court. When he came up for hearing he recited this couplet:

We drank our wine on credit, but we knew that, yes,
our starving revelry would bear fruit one day.

Because of a sudden misfortune, Mirza Sahib had to stay some days in the jail, as the Prophet Joseph stayed in the prison of Egypt. His clothes grew dirty, and lice had got into them. One day he was sitting picking the lice out of them when a nobleman arrived there to visit him. He asked, "How are you?" He recited this couplet:

From the day that we sorrowful ones were caught by misfortune,
the lice in our clothes outnumber the stitches of the seams.

On the day he was to come out of there, when the time came to change his clothes, he tore up the shirt he had worn there, threw it away on the spot, and recited this couplet:

Alas for the fate of that four-span piece of cloth, Ghalib,
whose fate is to be a lover's collar!

One day the little boy Husain Ali Khan came in while playing: "Grandfather, send for some sweets." He said, "There is no money." The boy opened the little box and began groping here and there for coins. He said:

Where would I have money or a penny?
Where would there be meat in a kite's nest?

His pension from the government used to come month by month; after the rebellion at Delhi an order came that it should be paid every six months. On this occasion he writes to a friend:

The first of the month is a dead man's, and the six-monthly one is one;
the world's affairs depend on this very way.

Look at me: I am bound to life,
and the six-monthly payment comes twice in a year.

But these two couplets are in fact from a *qasida* through which he obtained from the court of the King of Delhi an order for monthly instead of six-monthly pay. In his Persian *qasidas* too he often made such pleas and requests, and it is nothing strange; Anvari and many other poets have done the same.

Anecdote: Maulvi Fazl-e Haq Sahib was a great friend of the Mirza's. One day the Mirza went to see him. It was his habit, whenever an intimate friend came, to recite this line from the *Khaliq Bari*:

"*Biya, biradar*; come, O brother!"

So he rose to receive Mirza Sahib and seated him with this line. They had only just sat down when the Maulvi Sahib's mistress too got up from the other hall and came and sat beside them. The Mirza said: "Yes, Sir, do recite the second line too:

"*Binishin, madar*; sit down, O mother!"" [TN: The *Khaliq Bari* is a Persian–Hindi verse vocabulary in which each Persian word is followed by its Hindi equivalent.]

Anecdote: many people wrote replies to the Mirza's *Qati'-e Burhan*, and used much abusive language. Someone said: "Sir, you have not written a reply to so-and-so's book." He said: "Brother, if a donkey kicks you, what reply will you give it?"

Anecdote: his sister was ill, and he went to visit her. He asked, "How are you?" She said: "I am dying; I am worried about my debts, which I am taking with me round my neck." He said: "Sister, what sort of worry is this? Is Mufti Sadr-ud-Din Khan sitting at God's court, to give a decree and have you arrested?"

Anecdote: one day a favourite pupil of the Mirza's came and said: "Sir, today I went to Khusrau's tomb. There is a *khirni* tree over the grave, and I ate its fruit to my heart's content. No sooner had I eaten the *khirnis* than the door of eloquence and fine speech seemed to open; just see how eloquent I have become." The Mirza said: "Why, sir, why did you go three *kos* (about six miles)? Why didn't you eat the little figs of the *pipal* behind my house? All fourteen worlds would have lit up for you." [TN: Amir Khusrau (1253–1325), the great Persian poet of Delhi; the joke is that Ghalib's own house would give more inspiration.]

Anecdote[1]: some pupils said to the Mirza: "You have written many *qasidas*, and very powerful ones, in praise of Hazrat Ali; have you written nothing in praise of any of the Companions?" The Mirza thought a moment and said: "Show me one among them like him, and I will write his praise too." Mirza Sahib's playful nature always kept him soaked in this colour, so that people who did not know him accused him of unbelief. And since this colour looked strange against his appearance and dignity, his friends were startled to hear such things, and the more they were startled, the more he splashed them. His nature was used to the pleasure of wine, but he considered it a sin against God, and he also had a vow that he would never drink in Muharram.

Anecdote: a few days after the Mutiny, Pandit Moti Lal, who was in those days translator to the Government of the Punjab, came to Delhi with the Chief Commissioner of the Punjab, and out of love for his native place and for the art he called on Mirza Sahib. In those days the pension was stopped, and he was not allowed at the court. Because of his broken heart the Mirza was brimming with complaints and grievances. In the course of conversation he said: "If there is a day in my life I have not drunk wine, I am an infidel; and if I have said my prayers even once, I am not a Muslim. So I do not know how the government came to count me among the rebel Muslims."

Anecdote: a gentleman from Bhopal came to see the sights of Delhi. He was eager to meet Mirza Sahib too, and so one day he came to call. From his manner he appeared to be a very pious and abstemious man. The Mirza received him with the greatest courtesy, but it was his usual hour; he was sitting enjoying himself, with a glass and a decanter of wine before him. The poor man did not know that he had this taste too; he took it for a decanter of some sherbet and picked it up. Someone sitting near by said, "Sir, that is wine." The gentleman from Bhopal at once put the decanter down and said, "I only picked it up thinking it was sherbet." The Mirza looked at him with a smile and said: "How lucky you are! You were saved by a mistake."

Anecdote: once at night he was sitting in the courtyard; it was a moonlit night, and the stars were scattered about. Looking at the sky he began to say: "Work done without advice and consultation comes out clumsy. God did not make the stars in the sky after consulting anyone; that is why they are scattered about, with no order, no chain, no trailing vine, no flower pattern."

Anecdote: a Maulvi Sahib who was a Sunni by faith came to see him in the days of Ramazan. The afternoon prayer was over, and the Mirza asked the servant for water. The Maulvi Sahib said: "Sir, this is outrageous! Don't you fast in Ramazan[2]?" The Mirza said: "I am a Sunni Muslim; I break my fast with four *gharis* (about an hour and a half) of daylight left." [TN: The joke turns on the Shia practice of breaking the fast a little later than the Sunnis.]

Anecdote: it was the month of Ramazan, and he was sitting at Nawab Husain Mirza's. He sent for a *pan* and ate it. An angelic gentleman[3], very devout and abstemious, was present at the time, and he asked in astonishment, "Sir, don't you fast?" He smiled and said: "Satan is *ghalib* (overpowering)."

This anecdote was famous among wits even before him: Alamgir's mind was set against Sarmad, so he always kept an eye on him. And so Qazi Qavi, who was the city's *qazi* (judge) in that reign, on one occasion caught Sarmad drinking *bhang* (an intoxicant made from hemp). At first there were questions and answers full of wit and jest, and at last, when the *qazi* said, "No, this is the command of the law; why do you make up talk against God's command?", he said: "What can I do? Satan is *qavi* (strong)."

Anecdote: it was the cold season, and one day Nawab Mustafa Khan Sahib came to the Mirza's house. He filled a glass of wine and set it before him, and the Nawab began to look at his face. He said, "Do take it." As the Nawab had repented, he said, "But I have forsworn it." He said in astonishment: "What, even in winter?"

Anecdote: in his presence a gentleman said that drinking wine is a grave sin. He laughed and said: "Well, what happens to one who drinks?" He said: "The least of it is that his prayers are not answered." The Mirza said: "Do you know who drinks wine? First, one who has a bottle of Old Tom set before him with all that goes with it; second, freedom from care; third, good health. Tell me: what more does a man who has all this need, that he should pray for it?"

Twenty years before his death Mirza Sahib hit upon a phrase giving the date of his own death; he liked it very much and put it into verse:

Chronogram of death

Who am I, that I should live for ever?
When Naziri is no more, and Talib has died,

if they ask in which year
Ghalib died, say: "Ghalib died."

By this reckoning he should have died in [illegible] AH. In that very year a severe epidemic came to the city, and thousands of people died. In those days the grief of Delhi's ruin was fresh, and so in reply to Mir Mahdi Sahib he says: "What do you ask about the epidemic? In the quiver of fate the archer, this one arrow was left. Killing so general, plunder so harsh, such a famine: why should there not be an epidemic? Lisan-ul-Ghaib (the Tongue of the Unseen)[4] said ten years ago:

All the calamities are over and done, Ghalib;
there is one more, sudden death. Sir, the matter of 1277 AH was not wrong, but I did not think it worthy of me to die in a general epidemic. Truly it would have lowered my dignity. It will be considered after the trouble is over."
