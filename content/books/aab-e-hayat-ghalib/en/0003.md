# Mirza Sahib's Life and Natural Habits

## Mirza Sahib's Life and Natural Habits

There is no doubt that among the people of India the Mirza was a consummate poet of Persian; but he did not acquire the school learning in the manner of a student, and, if truth be told, it is a matter of great pride that a young noble should lose in childhood the guiding hand of his elders and yet raise himself by natural taste alone to such a height of accomplishment. What a nature he must have brought with him, that it gave his thought its lofty flight, his mind its creation of meanings, his ideas their manner, his words their new cut and his phrasing its unusual way! He says himself here and there, and it is truly not without charm, that he has an affinity with the Persian language from eternity. In another place he says that his nature has a natural bent towards that language. He sent *Qati'-e Burhan* to Mir Mufti Abbas Sahib with a letter, in which he says: "All I have written in the preface and the epilogue is true; for the substance of the work I ask for justice separately. The writing will not be without grace. I am bare of learning and skill, but for fifty-five years I have been absorbed in making verse. The Bountiful Source has done me a great kindness. My sources are sound and my nature is healthy. I have brought with me an affinity with Persian from eternity and for ever, and, like the people of Pars, I have brought the taste of its idiom with me for ever too."

There was a Parsi named Hormuzd, a scholar of the Zend and Pazend. He accepted Islam and took the name Abdus Samad. In the course of his travels he came to India, and he met the Mirza too. Although the Mirza was only fourteen at that time, the affinity from eternity was in his nature, and it drew the man to him; he kept him in his house as a guest for two years and acquired accomplishment from him. He was proud of the blessing of that enlightened man's company, and in truth the thing is worth being proud of.

I wanted to paint Mirza Sahib's portrait in words and meanings, but I remembered that in one place he has painted his own portrait with these very colours and oils. What more could I do than that? A copy of it is enough. But first hear this much: there was a man in Agra, Mirza Hatim Ali Mihr, a fellow-townsman of the Mirza's, and late in life a correspondence began with this brother from his home town. He was a handsome and stylish young man. The two never met face to face, but through their once being townsmen, their verse-making, their sharing one faith and their likeness of ideas, the Mirza perhaps said at some gathering, "I hear Mirza Hatim Ali Mihr is a stylish man; I should like to see him." When this news reached him, he wrote to the Mirza and described his own looks as well. Now the portrait that the Mirza paints of himself in reply is worth seeing: "Brother, I had heard of your stylishness from Mughal Jan, at the time when she was in the service of Hamid Ali Khan and there was a free friendship between her and me; I used to spend hours together with Mughal Jan. She also showed me your verses in her own praise. Anyway, seeing your description, I did not envy you your tall stature, because my height too is so long that people point at it. I did not envy your wheaten colour, because when I was alive my colour was *champa*-fair (pale gold), and discerning people used to praise it. Now whenever I remember that colour of mine, it is as if a snake crawled over my chest. Yes, I did envy you, and ate my heart's blood, at this: that your beard is well trimmed. Those pleasures came back to me; what can I say of what passed through my heart? In the words of Shaikh Ali Hazin:

As long as I could reach, I tore my collar open;
I feel no shame at the woollen cloak."

"When white hairs came into my beard and moustache, and on the third day ants' eggs began to show on my cheeks, and, worse than that, my two front teeth broke, I had no choice but to give up *missi* (the black tooth-powder) and to let my beard grow. But remember that in this clumsy city (that is, in Delhi) there is a common uniform: the mulla, the *hafiz* (one who knows the Qur'an by heart), the pedlar, the hookah-maker, the washerman, the water-carrier, the innkeeper, the weaver, the greengrocer, all have a beard on the face and hair on the head. The day I grew a beard, that same day I shaved my head." This sentence shows that he wanted to keep his style apart from everyone's. His dress was mostly that of the people of *wilayat* (Central Asia and Iran). Although he did not wear a Persian *papakh* cap on his head, he wore a tall cap of black lambskin; and so it had to be, for he kept up his Persian writing not only as a taste but as a passion of the heart; and it was not only his dress and speech: he loved everything that belonged to his old descent, and above all he always guarded the honours of his family with soul-wearing labour. On the honours that were still left to him heaven struck two blows: first when his uncle died, and second when in 1857, for the crime of a rebellion he had not committed, his chair at the court and his robe of honour were stopped along with his pension. In *Urdu-e Mualla* there are letters to scores of friends, and not one is free of mourning for this. Blood drips from his words in this grief, and only God knows what passed through his heart. In the end he took back his place and his right again, and kept his forefathers' name standing.

In 1842 the English government decided to reorganise Delhi College. Mr Thomason Sahib, who was later for some years Lieutenant Governor of the North-Western Provinces, was then Secretary. He came to Delhi to examine the teachers, and wished that, as there was a teacher of Arabic at a hundred rupees a month, there should be one of Persian as well. People named some accomplished men, and among them the Mirza's name came up. Mirza Sahib came as he was asked. The Sahib was told, but the Mirza got down from his palanquin and stood waiting for the Secretary Sahib to come out and receive him, as the old custom was. When neither did the one come out from his side nor the other go in from his, and there was a delay, the Secretary Sahib asked the head servant. He came out again: "Why do you not come in?" The Mirza said, "The Sahib has not come out to receive me; how could I go in?" The head servant went and reported again. The Sahib came out and said, "When you come to the Governor's durbar in your rank as a gentleman of standing, you will be received with that honour; but now you have come for a post, and you are not entitled to that honour." Mirza Sahib said, "I thought government service would add to my honour, not that I should lose even the honour of my forefathers." The Sahib said, "We are bound by the rules." Mirza Sahib took his leave and came away. The said Sahib called Momin Khan Sahib, had him read from a book, talked with him and fixed his pay at eighty rupees. He would not accept less than a hundred. The Sahib said, "If you take a hundred rupees, come with us." His heart would not agree to sell Delhi so cheap.

The Mirza's open heart and open hand always kept him in straits, but even in that poverty the badges of nobility remained. Most of the letters in *Urdu-e Mualla* are a mirror of this. In one letter he writes to Mirza Tafta[1], his favourite pupil: "I have cashed the bill for a hundred rupees. Twenty-four rupees had been borrowed through the steward, and those I paid; fifty rupees I gave in the women's quarters; twenty-six were left, and those I put in the box. Kalyan has gone to the bazaar to buy provisions; if he comes back soon I will post this letter today, otherwise tomorrow. May God keep you alive and reward you. Brother, things have come to a bad pass; I see no good end. To make a long story short, the story is over."

Kedarnath was his steward, and in this same state he would come and hand out the monthly accounts. When he had gone somewhere on a journey he sent him orders again and again in his letters. In one letter he writes: "This bill had a term of twelve days; six days had passed and six were left. Where was my patience? I had it discounted and took the money. All my scattered debts are paid, and I am much lightened. Today I have forty-seven rupees in cash in my box, and four bottles of wine and three flasks of rose-water in the store-room. Praise be to God for His kindness."

In another place he writes to someone about his illness: "Although the women's quarters are very close to the reception room, how is it possible for me to walk there? The food comes here at nine in the morning. I slide down off the bed, wash my hands and face and eat, then wash my hands again, rinse my mouth and go and lie down on the bed. There is a chamber-pot kept by the bed; I get up, make water in the pot and lie down again."

Mirza Sahib was married to the daughter of the late Nawab Ilahi Bakhsh Khan, and he was then thirteen years old. Although he kept free ways and manners, he was after all a man of family, and out of regard for the honour of the household he paid great attention to his wife's feelings. Yet when he was much vexed by that restraint, which was against his nature, he would turn it off with a joke. I heard some stories from friends, and it is often found in his letters too. With one old pupil he was free and easy in such matters. This man wrote to Mirza Sahib of the death of the wife of another pupil named Umrao Singh, and wrote too that there were little children, and what was he to do now if he did not marry? Who would bring up the children? One wife of this man had died before, and this was the second wife who had died. Now his honour writes in reply: "At Umrao Singh's state I feel pity for him and envy for myself. Good God! There is he, whose fetters have been cut twice, and here am I, with the noose that has been round my neck for more than fifty years: neither does the noose break nor does the breath go out. Explain to him: brother, I will bring up your children; why are you getting caught in trouble?" When his pension was restored he writes to another person: "By my life, if I were alone, how free and happy I would live on this small income!"

Mirza Sahib left behind, as his memorial, a numberless host of spiritual children, that is, pure thoughts and lofty ideas; but alas, as fortunate as he was on that side, so unfortunate was he in his children of the flesh. In one place he says: "Seven children were born, but within a year or so of one another all went to the land of non-being." His wife's nephew, the grandson of the late Ilahi Bakhsh, was Zain-ul-Abidin Khan. He too wrote verse, under the pen name Arif. Arif died young and left two little children behind. His wife loved these children very much, so the Mirza brought them up like his own. In his old age he went about with them hung round his neck like a garland; wherever he went, they were in the palanquin. He went without comfort for their comfort and did whatever they asked. Alas, after the Mirza both of them died young. The worthy sons of the late Nawab Ahmad Bakhsh Khan could not bear to see Mirza Sahib in trouble; they took the wealth of his art from him, and gave him ease in the needs of the world. Nawab Zia-ud-Din Khan Sahib is his pupil, and the late Nawab Amin-ud-Din Khan of Loharu also served him with the respect of a younger man. Nawab Ala-ud-Din Khan, the present ruler, was then the heir apparent and has been his pupil since childhood. Mirza Sahib writes to Nawab Ala-ud-Din Khan Sahib: "My dear, I am in great trouble. The walls of the women's quarters have fallen, the privy has collapsed, the roofs are leaking. Your aunt[2] cries, 'Oh, I'm crushed! Oh, I'm dead!' The reception room is in a worse state than the women's quarters. I am not afraid of dying; it is the loss of comfort that has unnerved me. The roof is a sieve: if the clouds rain for two hours, the roof rains for four. If the owner wants to repair it, how can he? When the rain stops everything can be done, and then how am I to stay sitting here while the repairs go on? If you can, get your brother to give your aunt, until the rains are over, the house in which Mir Hasan used to live, and give me, from the mansion, the upper room with the hall below it that was the dwelling of the late Ilahi Bakhsh Khan. The rains will pass, the repairs will be done, and then the Sahib[3] and the Mem and the *baba log* (children) will come back to their old home. Among all the kindnesses of your father's generosity and gifts to me, let this one kindness of courtesy also be added at the end of my life. Ghalib."

The Mirza had many friends. He kept up friendship with his friends so well that his devotion to friends, joined with his good humour, always showed a circle of gentlemen and young nobles around him. With them his sorrow was forgotten, and in that was his life. The charm of it was that he talked with his friends' sons too just as he talked with his friends. On one side the promising young men sitting respectfully, on the other his showering the flowers of an elder's jokes; on one side the dutiful young men silently smiling, and, when they did speak, not stepping beyond the bounds of respect; on the other his still not giving up his playfulness: it had a wonderful charm. In any case, in these graces and jests he put off the misfortunes of the age, and, making the unpleasant bearable, went on laughing and playing. There are letters in *Urdu-e Mualla* to Mir Mahdi, Mir Sarfaraz Husain, Nawab Yusuf Mirza and many other young gentlemen, which show photographs of those gatherings.

The faithlessness of the age did not grant the Mirza the ease and freedom from care that befitted his family and his accomplishment, and he thought a great deal about these two things; but he did not burn his heart over it or grow sick at heart; he laughed it away. As proof of both these things I copy two letters[4]. One letter is to Mir Mahdi Sahib, who is a gentleman of high family and his favourite pupil. The other is to Munshi Hargopal Sahib Tafta, who has been mentioned briefly before.

## Notes

[1] He seemed to be older than Mirza Sahib himself. He loved Persian, and so, although he was a Hindu, he was very pleased to be called Mirza Tafta. He had his *diwan* of *qasidas* and *diwan* of *ghazals* printed. He wrote verse in Persian.

[2] The daughter of the late Nawab Ilahi Bakhsh Khan, and so the own niece of the late Nawab Ahmad Bakhsh Khan. She was his wife.

[3] Since he has asked for an upper-storey house to live in, he has made himself the Sahib, his wife the Bibi Sahiba and the children the *baba log*.

[4] See the letters of *Urdu-e Mualla*.
