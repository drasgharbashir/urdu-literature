# Persian Works

## Persian Works

To give an account of the Persian works and to write an opinion of them is not the business of a writer of an Urdu *tazkira*, so I give only a list.

*Qasidas*: praise of God, praise of the Prophet, praise of the sinless Imams, and praise of the King of Delhi, the King of Oudh, the Governors and some gentlemen of high rank.

The *diwan* of *ghazals*, with the *diwan* of *qasidas*, was compiled in 1834 and 1835 and spread among people of taste through copies; it has been printed several times since.

*Panj Ahang* ("Five Modes"): it has five chapters on five modes, a fine work for Persian prose-writers who want to write in his manner.

In 1862 *Qati'-e Burhan* ("The Cutter of the Burhan") was printed; after some changes he had it printed again and named it *Dirafsh-e Kaviani* ("The Banner of Kava"). It points out the errors of the *Burhan-e Qati'* (a well-known Persian dictionary), but those who claimed mastery of Persian opposed it with fierce attacks.

*Nama-e Ghalib* ("Ghalib's Letter"): several people wrote replies to *Qati'-e Burhan*. So in Meerut there was a blind teacher named Hafiz Abdur Rahim, who wrote a reply to it, *Sati'-e Burhan*. Mirza Sahib wrote a few leaves in reply, in the form of a letter to the said Hafiz Sahib, and named them *Nama-e Ghalib*.

*Mihr-e Nimroz* ("The Midday Sun"): Hakim Ahsanullah Khan was the King's personal physician; he had a taste for history, and generally kept close ties with men of accomplishment. At his suggestion the Mirza first wrote one part of the said book. Through it, in 1850, he was admitted to His Majesty's presence and appointed to the office of writing history, and he received the title Najm-ud-Daula Dabir-ul-Mulk Mirza Asadullah Khan Ghalib Bahadur Nizam Jang. So in the first volume he told the history from Amir Timur to Humayun and named it *Mihr-e Nimroz*. He meant to write the history from Akbar to Bahadur Shah and name it *Mah-e Nim Mah* ("The Half-Month Moon"), when the Mutiny came.

*Dastanbu* ("A Nosegay"): an account of the rebellion from 11 May 1857 to 1 July 1858, the story of the city's ruin and his own experiences; in short, an account of fifteen months in all.

*Sabad-e Chin* ("The Gathered Basket"): it contains two or three *qasidas*, some *qitas* and some Persian letters that had not been included in the *diwan*.

At the end of his life he did not keep his own verse with him. His Urdu works stayed with Nawab Husain Mirza Sahib, who went on arranging them; the Persian he sent to Nawab Zia-ud-Din Ahmad Sahib, to whom he had given the pen name Nayyar-e Rakhshan and whom he had declared his worthy pupil and first *khalifa* (successor). His second *khalifa* was Nawab Ala-ud-Din Khan Sahib.

His letters show that he kept up his love of fine prose with great effort and toil; for that reason, ten or fifteen years before his death, his writings were in Urdu. So in a letter to a friend he himself says: "Kind sir, the writing of letters in the Persian language has long been given up. Under the blows of old age and weakness I no longer have the strength for hard labour and heart-wearing toil. My natural heat is in decline, and the state of things is this:

My powers have grown feeble, Ghalib;
where now is the balance among the elements?

It is not you alone; to all the friends with whom I correspond I write my humble letters in Urdu. Of the gentlemen to whom I used to write and send letters in Persian, those who are still alive, with them too, when there is need, I correspond in this current language."

In *Urdu-e Mualla* he writes to Mirza Hatim Ali Mihr: "I have a *qita* that I made in Calcutta. The occasion was this: Maulvi Karam Husain was a friend of mine. At a gathering he put a very clean and fibre-free smooth betel-nut on the palm of his hand and asked me to put its likenesses into verse. Sitting there, I wrote a *qita* of nine or ten couplets and gave it to him, and as my reward I took that betel-nut from him.

*Qita*

This smooth betel-nut that lies on the gentleman's palm:
however well you speak of it, it becomes it.

The pen bites its finger in wonder: what shall I write of it?
Speech hangs its head in its collar: what shall I say of it?

Liken it to the burnt-out star of Qais;
call it the musky mole on Laila's lovely cheek.

Suppose it the Black Stone in the wall of the Kaaba;
call it the musk-pod of the deer of the desert of Khotan.

In the cloister, take it for the clay tablet of prayer;
in the tavern, call it the brick on the mouth of the wine-jar.

Write it the *missi*-stained fingertip of the beauties;
call it like the nipple of a fairy-born maiden.

Suppose my lord's palm to be the heart,
and call this smooth betel-nut its black core.

In short, there are twenty-two likenesses in it; but when do I remember all the verses? I have forgotten them[1]."

Nawab Zinat Mahal had great influence over the King's mind. Mirza Jawan Bakht was her son, and although he was much younger than many of the princes, the King was striving to make him heir apparent. When the time came for his wedding, preparations of great splendour were made. The Mirza made this *sehra* (wedding-garland poem) and presented it to His Majesty.

*Sehra*

Rejoice, O Fortune, for today the *sehra* is on your head:
tie the *sehra* on the head of Prince Jawan Bakht.

How well it looks upon that moon-like face!
The *sehra* is the jewel of your heart-brightening beauty.

To ride upon the head becomes you, but, O brim of the cap,
I fear the *sehra* may snatch away your rank.

The pearls must have been strung by the boatload;
or why have they brought the *sehra* set in a boat?

The pearls of the seven seas must have been gathered;
only then was a yard-long *sehra* of this style made.

When sweat dripped from the bridegroom's face in the heat,
the *sehra* was wholly the vein of a pearl-raining cloud.

It would have been a discourtesy to go beyond the robe;
so the *sehra* came and stopped level with the hem.

Let the pearls not swell with pride that they alone are something:
there should also be a *sehra* of flowers, as is the rule.

When the flowers cannot contain themselves for joy,
how then could anyone weave them into a *sehra*?

The glow of the bright face, the gleam of the rolling pearls:
why should the *sehra* not show the radiance of the moon and stars?

This is not a silken thread; it is a vein of the spring cloud:
the *sehra* will bear the heavy weight of the pearls.

We understand poetry; we are not Ghalib's partisans:
let us see anyone say a better *sehra* than this.

When His Majesty heard the closing couplet, he thought there was a dig at him in it, as if it meant that no one could make a *sehra* equal to his: "We have made Shaikh Ibrahim Zauq our master and *Malik-ush-Shu'ara* (King of Poets); this is far from understanding poetry, it is partisanship." So that same day, when my late master went to the presence as usual, the King showed him the *sehra*: "Master, look at this." He read it and, as was his habit, submitted: "Spiritual guide, it is right." The King said, "Master, you too make a *sehra*." He submitted, "Very well." Then the King said, "Write it now, and keep an eye on the closing couplet too." My late master sat down there and then and submitted:

*Sehra*

O Jawan Bakht, blessed be the *sehra* on your head;
today the *sehra* of good omen and good fortune is on your head.

Today is the day when heaven has brought, made from the pearls of the stars,
the *sehra* set in the golden boat of the new moon.

One says "God bless the Prophet!", another says "Glory be to God!",
when moon and stars see the *sehra* on your face.

So that sincerity may stay between bride and groom,
weave the *sehra* while reciting the Sura of Sincerity.

The fame of this *sehra* fills the garden of the world;
why should the singing birds not sing the *sehra*?

From the lights that rain upon your fortunate face
a *sehra* is made wholly of threads of rain.

By the shining of your beauty, like the rays of the sun,
the *sehra* is bright upon your radiant face.

At the time of adorning, each adorns the other:
on the head the turban, and above the turban the *sehra*.

Not a single pearl is left in a hundred mines of pearls,
from which, taking them all, your *sehra* was made.

The spring breeze goes about proud of its fragrance;
Lord, Lord, the fragrant *sehra* of flowers!

On the head an ornamented crest, round the neck the *budhi*;
the bracelet graces the hand, and the *sehra* the face.

As the face-showing gift, let the sun and moon of heaven be given you,
when you open your face, lifting the *sehra* from it.

From the multitude of the onlookers' threads of sight,
as they gaze, there is a *sehra* on your fair face.

Making it from the lustrous pearls of themes,
your praise-singer Zauq has brought the *sehra* for you.

Whoever lays claim to poetry, recite this to him:
"See, this is how the poets make a *sehra*."

The women singers and dancers who were in His Majesty's service received it that very moment; by evening it had spread through every lane and street of the city, and the very next day it was published in the newspapers. The Mirza too was a great reader of moods and understood poetry; he had meant one thing and something else had come of it. He presented this *qita* to His Majesty:

*Qita* of apology

I wish to set out the true state of affairs;
to speak of my own good nature is not my wish.

For a hundred generations[2] soldiering has been my forefathers' calling;
poetry is no means of honour for me.

I am free in my ways, and my creed is peace with all;
I have never had enmity with anyone at all.

Is it a small honour that I am Zafar's slave?
Granted that I have no rank, office or wealth.

That I should think of quarrelling with the King's master:
I have no such strength, no such daring, no such power.

The Emperor's heart is a world-showing cup;
I have no need of oath or witness.

Who am I, to make *rekhta* (Urdu verse)? Yes, in it my aim
is nothing but the delight of His Majesty's heart.

The *sehra* was written in obedience to command;
I saw I had no course but to obey.

In the closing couplet a poet's flourish slipped in;
I did not mean by it to break off affection.

If my words are aimed at anyone, may my face be blackened!
I have no madness, no frenzy, no wildness.

My fortune may be bad, but my nature is not bad;
it is a cause for thanks that I have no complaint.

I am true to my word, Ghalib; God is my witness:
I speak the truth, for I have no habit of lying.
