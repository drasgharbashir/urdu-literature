# Azmat's Accounts

After Shab-e-Barat, news began to come of Asghari's father's arrival, and nine or ten days passed in no time. Four days before Ramzan, Durandesh Khan Sahib reached Delhi.

Asghari had heard beforehand that her father was coming, and it had been agreed with her mother-in-law and husband that on the day the Tahsildar Sahib arrived she would go to see him. When Asghari learned that her father had come, she at once sent for a *doli* and went to him. Her father hugged her and his eyes filled with tears. For a long time they asked and told each other their news, and he said to Asghari, "As you asked, Khairandesh Khan has gone to Lahore, and, God willing, he will arrive tomorrow or the day after with your father-in-law. I had a letter from him on the way too; your father-in-law has been given leave."

So Asghari spent the whole night and the next day at her mother's house, and towards evening she said to her father, "If you will allow me, I'll go back today."

Her father said, "Why, stay a week at least; we'll send word to your mother-in-law."

Asghari said, "Whatever you command, I will obey; but it seems wise to be at home before my father-in-law arrives."

Her father thought and said, "Yes, that's right."

So Asghari took leave of her father and was back home before sunset. The very next day, just at mealtime, Maulvi Muhammad Fazil Sahib, Muhammad Kamil's father, arrived quite suddenly.

This Maulvi Sahib was an agent in the service of a nobleman of Lahore. His salary from that household was fixed at fifty rupees a month, with house and carriage provided by the nobleman. As Asghari had written, Khairandesh Khan had gone to Lahore and shown her letter to Maulvi Muhammad Fazil Sahib. The Maulvi Sahib was delighted when he saw his daughter-in-law's letter; ordinarily he might not have taken leave at all, but now, in his eagerness to see his daughter-in-law, he pressed the nobleman hard and got a month's leave, and came with Khairandesh Khan.

Since Asghari had not appeared before her father-in-law since her marriage, when she saw him coming she went up to the roof out of shyness. Muhammad Kamil's mother was amazed at how he came to be there. After they had eaten and drunk, they began to talk. The Maulvi Sahib said to his wife, "Listen, madam, it is your younger daughter-in-law who has drawn me here." And he told his wife all about the letter and about Khairandesh Khan's coming, and said, "Call the bride."

Her mother-in-law went up to the roof and said, "Come, my daughter; what is there to be shy about? You played on his lap as a child."

At her mother-in-law's bidding, Asghari got up and went with her, greeted her father-in-law very respectfully and sat down.

The Maulvi Sahib said, "Listen, my dear, I have come only because you sent for me, and seeing your letter made me very happy. May God bless you with long life and good fortune! Truly our house is fortunate that you came into it. And now I am sure that the fortunes of this house have turned, and tomorrow, God willing, its management will be as you wish and as you advise."

So for two or three days the Maulvi Sahib, having just arrived, was busy meeting people, and then during the first few days of the fast he did not turn his attention to household affairs because of the fasting. One day he called his daughter-in-law and sat her down beside him, called Mama Azmat and said, "Mama, while I am here, settle all the accounts. Write down everyone who is owed or owes anything. Each shall be paid as much as is suitable, and for what remains we will arrange instalments."

The maid said, "If it were only one account I might keep it in my head; but there's the grain-dealer, the cloth-merchant, the butcher, the greengrocer and the confectioner, all to be paid, and Hazari Mal's account is a separate sum altogether. Give me whatever is to be paid to each of them and I'll go and pay it; it will be credited to your account."

The Maulvi Sahib was a simple, straightforward man, and was ready to give it to her. Asghari said, "What is the use of paying on account like that? First we should know how much each one is owed, and then pay after thinking it over."

The maid said, "When I have finished with the cooking, I'll go and ask each of them."

Asghari said, "What good will going and asking do? Let everyone who is owed money come here and settle his account."

The maid said, "Madam, that's easily said; now am I to go round calling them from everywhere? And when do those people get away from their work and business to come along with me?"

Asghari said, "Mama, it's not calling them every day; it's a matter of one day. Go and call them. Some arrangement will be made for the evening meal; you just do this today. And people who are owed money will come running when they hear it is to be paid. Hazari Mal went two *kos* to the court, after all; has he got henna on his feet, that he can't come here? And who lives far off? The greengrocer, the butcher, the grain-dealer and the confectioner are all in this very lane; only the cloth-merchant and Hazari Mal are far away. Leave them for tomorrow, and let these small accounts be settled today."

Mama Azmat did not want the accounts gone into at all, but Asghari pressed her so hard in argument that she had no answer. The confectioner came first. He was asked, "Lala, how much are you owed?"

The confectioner said, "Thirty rupees." He was asked, "What things have come from your shop? Thirty rupees is a great deal to claim." The confectioner said, "Sir, is thirty rupees so much? Fifteen rupees' worth of goods came this very Shab-e-Barat; one item is ten *sers* of sugar."

Muhammad Kamil's mother said, "What sugar? This time everything we had cooked came ready-made from the bazaar, for cash."

Hearing this, Mama Azmat turned pale and said to the confectioner, "Why did you write those ten *sers* of sugar in their account? I took that for another house, and I told you so."

The confectioner said, "You never mentioned anybody's name to me; you got it in the name of this household. Otherwise, what would I gain by writing someone else's goods in their name? And I have no credit dealings with any other household through you."

Then the maid began to babble in confusion.

The Maulvi Sahib said, "Well, leave the sugar aside; tell us the other items."

In the same way he named many things that had never come into the house in all their lives: four *sers* of *balushahi* [TN: a fried sweet] for the Maulud Sharif, and the joke was that no one had ever held a *maulud* gathering [TN: the celebration of the Prophet's birth] in that house at all! In short, only six or seven rupees turned out to be true; all the rest was false.

The Maulvi Sahib was furious and terribly angry, and he asked, "So, you treacherous Azmat, is this how you have loaded this house with debts to all the world? Is this how you have ruined it?"

When the confectioner had finished, the greengrocer came. He said, "Sir, mine is the usual account: two annas' worth of vegetables a day."

Muhammad Kamil's mother said, "What! One *ser* of vegetables comes into my house; how does that make two annas a day?"

The greengrocer said, "Your honour, the maid takes three *sers* from my shop."

The maid said, "Yes, I take three *sers*: one in your name, one for my daughter and one for another house. Do I deny it? This wretch puts it all down to your name."

The greengrocer said, "You dishonest old woman! You have always taken three *sers* on this house's account, and when money was paid, it came from this house."

When the butcher's and the grain-dealer's accounts were gone into, thousands of frauds came out in them too, and it was proved that the maid had been supplying her daughter Khairatan and two or three neighbouring houses from this household's shopping, and that she bought goods in this house's name and sold them elsewhere. So until evening the small accounts were gone into; only the cloth-merchant and Hazari Mal remained.

The Maulvi Sahib said, "Let us stop for today; we'll see to the rest tomorrow." But he added quietly, "I hope Azmat won't run away."

Asghari said, "Where will she run to, leaving her house and home, her children and her family? It's true that if she had any shame she might take something and poison herself; but if she had such shame, why would she do such things? Still, she must be watched, but only so far as to have someone keep an eye on her comings and goings."

He quietly told one of the servants who had come with him, "Keep watch on the maid's comings and goings."

When supper was over, the maid quietly got up and went out; the servant followed her. The maid went first to her own house, and from there, with something tucked under her arm, she went straight as an arrow to the cloth-merchant's house and called him. The cloth-merchant came out in alarm: "Old lady, what are you doing here at this hour?"

Azmat said, "The Maulvi Sahib has come home, and everyone who is owed money is having his account settled. Tomorrow you will be sent for too; don't say anything that will disgrace me."

The cloth-merchant said, "What is there in the accounts to disgrace you?"

The maid said, "Lala, you know how evil this wretched greed is. On the household's account I have sometimes taken long-cloth, fine muslin and *daryai* silk from your shop for myself too."

The cloth-merchant said, "How do I know what you have taken for yourself?"

The maid said, "I'm in no state to reckon it up just now; but two or three pieces of silk, and some long-cloth and muslin, and ten yards of purple satin will come to my account. Here, these four bracelets from my wrists are worth sixteen rupees; with wear they may have lost a rupee. Take fifteen rupees off their account; and if two or three rupees more come to my name, I am ready to pay it."

The cloth-merchant said, "You're giving me bracelets; very well, I'll take them. But it's night-time and the account books are at the shop; how can I know without looking what has gone and what is owed?"

Azmat said, "My honour is in your hands at this moment; save it however you can."

Leaving the cloth-merchant, she went straight to Hazari Mal's house. He too was astonished: "You here at this hour!" She fell at his feet and said, weeping, "I have done something wrong."

Hazari Mal said, "What is it?"

Azmat said, "Promise to forgive me, and I'll tell you."

Hazari Mal said, "Tell me first what it is."

Azmat said, "Four months ago money came from Lahore, and the Maulvi Sahib sent you a hundred rupees. I spent it, and out of fear I didn't tell the household. Now the Maulvi Sahib has come and will send for you about the accounts. I'll find the money somehow; don't you mention that sum."

Hazari Mal said, "If it were a matter of a few rupees I might hide it; but I can't hide a whole hundred rupees."

The maid said, "Can't you trust me even for a hundred rupees?"

Hazari Mal said, "To tell you plainly, I wouldn't trust you for a cowrie. If you have treated the very people in whose house you were maintained all your life like this, would you be one to spare anyone else?"

Azmat said, "Yes, Lala, when bad times come, one's own people become one's enemies. Well, if you don't trust me, here, keep these wrist-bracelets and this armlet of my daughter's."

Hazari Mal said, "Yes, that's businesslike; but when it's day the goods will be assayed, and then we'll know what they're worth. At a guess, though, the lot would be worth fifty or sixty."

Azmat said, "Oh, Lala, don't be so cruel! It's only four months since I had both pieces made; they cost a hundred, a hundred and twenty-five."

Hazari Mal said, "What is there to take offence at? Whether your things are worth a hundred or two hundred, is anyone going to take them from you? When they're weighed, we'll know what they're worth."

Having made all these arrangements, the maid came home; and the Maulvi Sahib's servant, while he was rubbing his master's feet, told the Maulvi Sahib the whole story, and through Muhammad Kamil Asghari learned of it too.

In the morning the cloth-merchant and Hazari Mal were sent for. There was some argument over the accounts, and the maid was shouting louder and louder. The cloth-merchant said, "Old woman, what are you jabbering about? Take back your bracelets. You said they were worth fifteen rupees; in the bazaar they're valued at nine."

Then Hazari Mal took out the wrist-bracelets and the armlet, put them down in front of them, and said to Azmat, "No, madam, these goods are no use to us." The Maulvi Sahib asked both the cloth-merchant and Hazari Mal, "Well, friends, what are these things?" Then they both told the story of the night before, and it was as though a hundred thousand shoes were raining down on Azmat's face.

When the accounts had been settled and the Maulvi Sahib took out money to pay, he paid everyone half of what was rightly owed, and said, "I have sent to Lahore for money. When it comes in five or ten days, the rest will be paid too."

They all asked, "And what has been found owing to us from the maid, who are we to get that from?"

While this was going on, Muslim came that way on his way home from school and heard what was being said. When he got home he said to Tamasha Khanam, "There's a great crowd at sister Asghari's door today; her father-in-law is settling accounts." The moment she heard it, Tamasha Khanam got into a *doli* and arrived. When she got down she reproached Asghari: "Well! What happened, that you didn't let me know?"

Asghari said, "The accounts are still going on. When this business was over I would have let you know."

Then the Maulvi Sahib said to the people, "What is owed to you by the maid, get from the maid." And turning to Azmat he said, "Now, madam, pay them their money."

Azmat lowered her eyes and said, "I have my daughter's jewellery; let these people take their dues out of that."

All her daughter's jewellery went at half its value to the accounts of the greengrocer, the butcher, the grain-dealer and the cloth-merchant; and for Hazari Mal's hundred rupees she had to mortgage the hovel she lived in. It was all written down on stamped paper and witnessed by four respectable men.

The Maulvi Sahib said to Azmat, "That's enough; now please take yourself off, and good luck to you. We have no use in our house for a treacherous, deceitful, dishonest creature like you."

Asghari said, "Besides treachery she has one more quality: she was trying to stir up strife in the house. Well, Azmat, do you remember the matter of the *karhai* that Mahmuda's brother asked for, when you went and falsely said on my behalf that the bride said, 'I have a headache'? Speak up: when did you ever tell me about it, and when did I ever plead a headache?"

Azmat said, "Madam, you were reading the Quran on the roof; I went up to tell you, but seeing you reading, I came back down."

Asghari said, "And the headache you made up yourself?"

Azmat said, "I thought, you have been reading since morning; now why should you weary your head over the stove?"

Asghari said, "Well then, why did you say I was going to the hills? Did I ever consult you about it, or did you hear me say so?"

Azmat had no answer to that.

Then Asghari took out the notice and laid it before the Maulvi Sahib and said, "Look, this is the kind of virtue Bibi Azmat has. She herself tore the notice down from the gate of the quarter and stuck it on our house, and then ran off herself to tell Mother."

As Asghari was telling all this, the Maulvi Sahib's face grew redder and redder, while Tamasha Khanam ground her teeth.

The Maulvi Sahib said, "Turning you out is not enough; you are a thoroughly wicked woman." With that he called his servant and said, "Bahadur! Take this foul creature to the police station; I'll write down everything about her in a note."

Asghari said to the Maulvi Sahib, "That's enough; she has had her punishment now. Please spare her the police station." And she signed to the maid to be off; indeed she went with her as far as the door.

So Mama Azmat was turned out of that house for her tricks. When she reached home, her daughter clung to her like a curse: "Didn't I tell you? 'Mother, don't plunder like that. A hundred days are the thief's, but one day is the honest man's; one day you'll be caught.' But whom did you ever listen to? Serves you right! As you sowed, so you have reaped. Now don't bring disgrace on my name in my husband's family; go wherever your God takes you; there is no place for you in my house. I have resigned myself to losing my jewellery; if it is in my fate, I'll get it again."
