# A Father's Letter

When Khairandesh Khan informed his father Durandesh Khan that "by the grace and kindness of God, my dear sister's marriage has taken place happily and well on the eleventh of Zil-Hijja, with the *mahr-e-fatimi* [TN: a modest marriage settlement on the model of the Prophet's daughter Fatima]", Durandesh Khan said a prayer of two *rak'ats* in thanksgiving; but he grieved for a long time at being parted from his daughter.

The letter that Durandesh Khan wrote to Asghari after her marriage is worth seeing. By chance a copy of it came into our hands; this is the letter:

"Comfort of my heart and soul, my dear child Asghari Khanam, may God Most High keep her safe! After prayers for you and longing to see you and kiss you, be it known that I learned of your departure to your new home from your brother Khairandesh Khan's letter. For years I had wished to carry out this duty under my own special care, but my superior would not give me leave and I could do nothing. You must have seen that of all my children I had a particular affection for you; and I do not write this as though conferring a favour, for you won a place in my heart and in everyone's by your service and obedience. From the age of eight you carried the whole burden of my household on your head. I always knew that because of you the Begum, that is your mother, was quite free from care. Whenever I happened to go home in that time, I was always delighted to see how you managed things. Now that you have gone, the loss is such that I cannot hope to see it made good in my lifetime. May God reward you well, and as a recompense for this service may the effect of my prayers be seen in you. From Khairandesh Khan's letter I also learned that you did not want a larger dowry than Akbari Khanam. That shows your high-mindedness and generosity of spirit; but I am sending you something better in its place, and that is this letter. Keep it with you as a rule of conduct and act on its advice; God Most High willing, every difficulty will become easy for you, and you will spend your life in comfort and ease.

You must understand what marriage is. Marriage is not merely putting on coloured clothes, and guests gathering, and receiving money, goods and jewellery; rather, with marriage a new world begins. One has to deal with new people and live in a new house. Just as a yoke is first put on young bullocks, so marriage is the yoke for the young of mankind. The marriage is made, the girl becomes a wife, the boy becomes a husband: this means that both have been caught and yoked to the cart of the world. Now they must draw this cart as far as the stage of the grave. So it is best to strengthen one's heart and bear this great burden, and to pass the days of one's life, however many they may be, in honour and good name, in peace and harmony; otherwise, with fights and squabbles, quarrels and wrangles, uproar and strife, cries and lamentations, the trouble of the world becomes still more painful.

Now, my dear daughter Asghari Khanam, you should consider how great a difference God has made between husband and wife. It is written in the books of religion that Adam was lonely and restless by himself in Paradise; to cheer him, God created Mother Eve, the first woman who ever lived in the world. So woman was created only for man's happiness, and it is a woman's duty to keep her husband happy. It is a pity how few women in the world fulfil this duty! God has given men a higher rank than women, not only by giving them authority but by putting more strength in their bodies and more light in their minds. The management of the world is carried on by men. Men are the earners, and women are those who spend their earnings fittingly and are the guardians of them. A family is like a boat, and men are its boatmen. If there is no boatman, the boat will sink in the waves of the water, or strike some shore and break to pieces. If the man in a family is not a good manager, every kind of ruin may befall it.

You must never think that happiness in the world comes from riches and wealth. Although there is no doubt that wealth is often a cause of happiness, we find more quarrels and strife in very great and lofty houses. In household life happiness comes only from harmony and peaceableness. We see poor people whose income is very small, who earn their living by toil and labour in the day; at night they all fill their stomachs together with lentils and bread and are happy with one another. Certainly, because they live in peace, these people are more at ease with their lentils and bread and their coarse cloth than nawabs and begums, all of whose pleasures are made bitter by their discord with each other. O my dear daughter Asghari Khanam, create harmony, and count peaceableness a blessing.

Now we must see what things create harmony. It is not only that a wife should love her husband; besides love, she must also show him respect. It is great foolishness for a wife to think of her husband as her equal; indeed, in these times women have adopted such a bad custom that it is utterly opposed to respect. When a few friends sit together and talk, the conversation often turns to how so-and-so's husband behaves towards her. One says, 'My dear, I have got him so much under my thumb that he wouldn't dare contradict me or answer me back.' Another boasts, 'I won't eat until he has coaxed me for ages.' A third brags, 'When he asks me ten times, I give him one answer, and that with difficulty.' A fourth swaggers, 'He may sit on the floor himself for hours, but your humble servant has sworn not to get down from the bed.' A fifth shows off: 'Whatever I say, I make him carry it out.' The charms and spells at weddings, too, were invented for the purpose of making the husband obedient and submissive. In some places lamp-black is made on a shoe and put on the husband's eyes as collyrium, which means that all his life he will take beatings with the shoe and never utter a squeak. In some places, at the bath, a *pan* is put under the bride's big toe and then given to the husband to eat, which means that he will always be at her feet. These things show plainly that women are bent on lessening men's rank and authority; but this is very bad teaching, and its result is never free of evil. God has made men lions; if anyone tries to subdue them by pressure and force, it is impossible. The very simplest way to tame them is pleasing them and obedience. And the foolish woman who tries to subdue her husband by putting pressure on him is making a great mistake; from the start she is sowing the seed of strife, and in the end the result will certainly be strife, even if she does not understand that now. Asghari Khanam, my advice is that in your speech, and in how you sit and rise, you always keep your husband's dignity in view.

Why is it that a marriage is made with such eagerness, and after the *chauthi* [TN: the ceremony on the fourth day after the wedding] the mother-in-law and sisters-in-law begin to fall out with the bride? This subject deserves thought. Until the marriage the girl lived with her parents, and her ties were with them alone. The parents brought him up, and all the while hoped that in their old age he would serve them. After the marriage, the moment the bride steps out of the *doli* she begins to plan how to make her husband leave his parents at once. So the quarrel always begins on the bride's side. If the bride makes herself one of the family, and the mother-in-law never feels that she wants to take her son away from her, no strife will ever arise. Everyone knows that after marriage the tie with the parents lasts only a few days; in the end there will be a separate house and husband and wife will live apart; that is how it has always been in the world. But heaven knows where these wretched brides get their impatience from, that whatever is to happen must happen this very minute.

Brides have a fault, telling tales, which causes much strife. That is, they come and tell their mothers every little thing about their husband's home, and the mothers themselves dig and probe and ask; but nothing comes of this telling and asking except fights and quarrels. Some brides are so proud that however good the food and clothes they are given in their husband's home, they always look on them with contempt. Such things wound the husband's heart. Asghari, you must be very careful about this. Everything in your husband's home deserves to be valued, and you should always show pleasure when you have eaten a meal or put on a garment, so that it may be seen that you liked it.

In her husband's home a new bride must also be sure not to live there in low spirits. It is true that, being strange to them, one does not feel at home among unfamiliar people; but one must reason with one's heart, and not be like 'went there crying, stayed there crying; no sooner gone than pestering to come back'. To build up affection little by little, the custom of *chale* [TN: the bride's periodic visits to her parents' home] is a very good one; to show more longing for one's mother's house than that is sure to displease the husband's family.

In speaking, keep to the middle way: neither so much that you chatter on of your own accord, nor so little that it is thought pride. Too much chatter ends in ill-feeling; when there is talk day and night on a thousand subjects, who knows what may slip out of one's mouth in which conversation? Nor should one be so sparing of speech that people must coax and beg one to speak. Obstinacy and insistence on anything are unbecoming; if something goes against your wishes, let it rest for the moment, and it can be settled suitably at another time. One should never ask for anything; asking for things lowers a person in others' eyes and makes his word count for little. You should not think it beneath you to do with your own hands the work your mother-in-law and sisters-in-law do. Kindness to those younger and respect for those older are an excellent way to be loved by all. One should not leave one's own work to others, nor leave one's belongings lying about carelessly for others to pick up. When two people are talking quietly together, one should move away from them; and then do not trouble about what they were saying to each other, and do not imagine for no reason that they were talking about you. From the start, keep your dealings respectful and considerate; the quicker people become very familiar with each other, the quicker ill-feeling begins to arise between them.

I want you to read this letter at least once every day, even when there is no need, so that its meaning may always be before you. With prayers for you. Written by Durandesh Khan."
