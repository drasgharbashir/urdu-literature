# A Match for Mahmuda

After Asghari left, the two sisters praised Mahmuda beyond all measure. Sultana was half won over; but Shah Zamani Begum too had a daughter, Dildar Jahan, and Shah Zamani intended to betroth her daughter to Arjumand. It was fortunate, though, that Shah Zamani had not yet said anything about this to her sister. When Asghari spoke of the match with Mahmuda, Sultana Begum sent to ask Shah Zamani Begum, "What do you think of this proposal?"

Shah Zamani was much alarmed to hear of it, and began to think how Mahmuda's proposal could be suppressed so that she could settle Dildar Jahan's. For the moment she sent word: "I'll think it over and send you an answer."

The next day her ladyship arrived in person, and when the subject came up she said to Sultana, "What a match: you on one side, the Maulvi Sahib on the other! What has the earth to do with the sky? Who brought this proposal here?" Sultana said, "The teacher."

Shah Zamani said, "I'll go to the teacher myself." She took Husn-ara with her and went to Asghari, and said, "Teacher, you are so very sensible, and yet you don't understand that matches are made between equals? A proposal from Ulwi Khan's house was sent back because they would not agree to give a golden canopied bed. Tell me, what will you give with Mahmuda?"

Asghari said, "Begum Sahib, I only put forward a proposal for a girl's marriage; I didn't send an offer to haggle over her price. Although all the customs in the city have gone bad nowadays, I have never heard of a marriage being struck as a bargain over giving and taking. If a man gives his daughter, is he to make a profit out of her? As for equality, it is plain that in point of wealth we cannot compare at all; we haven't a quarter of what Ulwi Khan has. But you are marrying off a son; why should you worry about a dowry? When one gives a daughter, one may well think, 'Let us see how the girl will be provided for'; or if someone is poor and has been counting on the bride's dowry to live on, he may rightly worry about it. But you are receiving a daughter, and you have everything God has given in your house. What you want is a girl, and you have seen the girl; nothing about her is hidden from you. And whatever our family is, good or bad, you know it."

Shah Zamani said, "What of it? All the same, matches are made by looking for an equal."

Asghari said, "Begum Sahib, forgive me, but where has the equality gone now? There was equality in the days when Ali Naqi Khan married his sister into this very house; and is this not the same house, that it is now not equal even to receiving a daughter? Have we come to live in this house on rent now? As for wealth, God does not like such boasting."

Asghari took Shah Zamani to task so sharply that she could find nothing to say, and Shah Zamani said, "Teacher, you are getting angry."

Asghari said, "Begum Sahib, how would I dare? I had hoped that you would help in this matter, not that you yourself would dislike it."

Shah Zamani said, "Teacher, take it well or ill, it is not an equal match."

Asghari said, "In wealth we are not equal; in birth we claim equality; in accomplishments, God willing, they will not be equal to us. What does it matter? In one thing they fall short, in another we do. They may search the world with a lamp for a daughter-in-law like ours and not find one."

Shah Zamani Begum said, "Teacher, why don't you send for a proposal from Iqbalmand Khan's son?"

Asghari said, "I had heard that a match was being discussed in your house, so I did not think of it; and what shortage is there of proposals? There are plenty of boys for girls and plenty of girls for boys. I only thought that accomplishments and wealth go together; such a thing befits the rich, and the rich are graced by it; if the match is settled, it will be good for both. But if it is not acceptable, then betroth Dildar Jahan to him."

Shah Zamani said, "Dildara is still a child; and I mean to give her outside the family; a match within a match is never without its unpleasantness."

With that Shah Zamani took her leave. Husn-ara stayed sitting. Her aunt did say, "Come along, child." Husn-ara said, "You go; I haven't seen the teacher for years; I'll stay and talk." When Shah Zamani had gone, Husn-ara said, "Teacher, Mother is willing; it's this lady who is spoiling things. If she refuses with her mouth, let her; what she really wants is for Dildara's match to be settled."

Asghari said, "Now it is a matter of fate; with her against it, what weight do we have? But, Husn-ara dear, I hadn't thought of anything unsuitable; I had seen that the patch matched the cloth. Yours is such a great house, and one son, the child of many prayers; all the property and wealth is his. So to manage so great an establishment needs great intelligence and great good sense. What if Mahmuda comes from a poor home? God keep her! Her spirit and her good sense are those of the rich. If some girl without sense came into your house bringing cartloads of dowry, what use would they be? She would find it hard to look after and spend her own dowry; how could she manage your household? Mahmuda, God bless her, could manage a kingdom. And then, Husn-ara dear, one should think for what purpose matches are made. In this world, as far as possible, one should widen one's ties and friendships; what is gained by making matches within the same family? When one marries, it should be outside."

Husn-ara said, "Teacher, my sister and I have spoken to Mother very thoroughly, and now I'll tell her all these things as well; I do hope this match will win." So, having coached Husn-ara in all this, Asghari sent her off.

Meanwhile Shah Zamani went to Sultana and said, "My dear, I told the teacher plainly to her face that she is no match for you; one should think before letting words out of one's mouth." But the snag was that Shah Zamani could not speak for her own daughter with her own mouth; she had it in her heart, but she had been thinking that the matter would be settled between the men. Now her great objection to Mahmuda's match was poverty. At last, apart from Shah Zamani, Sultana Begum consulted her two daughters, and Husn-ara said, "Mother, the plain truth is that Aunt is proposing Dildara."

Sultana said, "Well, ask Arjumand too, half in jest."

Jamal-ara called her brother and said, "Well, brother, a marriage is being planned; you say something too: are you willing to have Dildar Jahan?"

Out of respect, Arjumand said nothing in front of his mother; but by a sign he refused to his sisters. His refusal became Jamal-ara's and Husn-ara's strongest argument.

Husn-ara said, "Character, looks, accomplishments, good sense: in these things no girl will come up to even a fraction of Mahmuda; I'll answer for that. Yes, if you want a golden canopied bed, where would these poor people get that?"

Sultana said, "My dear, the real thing is to look at the girl. By God's grace we lack nothing in our house; what do we want with a heavy dowry?"

Jamal-ara said, "Then why hesitate? Begin, in God's name."

Husn-ara said, "Though they are poor, the teacher is a woman of great resource. What if she doesn't say so? When the time comes, she will do more than her means."

Sultana said, "Very well; when your father comes in, let us ask his advice too."

When the younger Hakim Sahib came in, Jamal-ara and Husn-ara presented Mahmuda's case to him just as pleaders present their clients' cases in court. In the end the younger Hakim Sahib too approved of Mahmuda's match. [TN: the head of the family being "the Hakim", his brother, though an old man, is called "the younger Hakim".]

Now the two sisters ran headlong to Asghari. Muhammad Kamil's mother knew nothing at all of these matters. She even asked, "What is it? Begum Sahib, why are you running like this? Hold up your trouser-legs as you go."

Husn-ara said, "Nothing; we're going to the teacher."

The moment they reached Asghari, Husn-ara said, "Here you are, teacher, congratulations! Give us our reward."

Asghari said, "May God bless you all too; and how could I presume to give a reward? My reward is my prayers, and night and day I pray for you."

Husn-ara said, "No, teacher, today we are certainly going to have our mouths sweetened."

Asghari said, "You'll eat sweets without stirring from your seat." She called Diyanat, took out five rupees, put them in her hand, and said, "Go at once and bring the very best *qalaqand* from Ghantewala's shop, pumpkin sweetmeat from the corner of the Dariba, *motiya pak* from Shah Tara's lane, almond sweets from the Chandni Chowk, fried lentils from Nil ka Katra, and *namish* (whipped milk froth) from Khanam's bazaar." [TN: famous sweet shops and quarters of old Delhi.]

Meanwhile she made *gilauris* for them both; and the basket of sweets arrived. Asghari, Akbari, Husn-ara and Jamal-ara all ate heartily together, and what was left was sent to the school. As the sisters were leaving, Asghari said, "Until now I haven't told Mother; now I'll speak to her and, God willing, the usual ceremony can be performed the day after tomorrow, which is a good date and a good day."
