# The Hajjan's Tricks

Some days later Mizajdar asked the *hajjan*, "Tell me, Bi, why haven't you brought any of the begum's things lately?" The *hajjan* saw that she had got the habit badly. She said, "When something comes up that suits you, I'll bring it."

Two or three days later she brought a pair of false pearls and said, "Here, Bi, these are pearls from the begum's own nose-ring. Heaven knows whether the pair is worth a thousand or five hundred. I showed them at the shop of Panna Mal the jeweller; he went mad over them and was ready to tie two hundred rupees into the corner of my scarf there and then. I have brought them from the begum for fifty rupees; you take them, you won't get such goods again."

Mizajdar said, "I haven't fifty rupees in cash."

The *hajjan* said, "What does that matter, my daughter? Sell your wrist-bracelets and buy them; if not, it's up to you, but these pearls will be sold today."

The *hajjan* said it in such a way that Mizajdar at once fetched her jewel box, took out her wrist-bracelets and handed them to the *hajjan*. When the *hajjan* saw Mizajdar's jewellery she said, "Oh dear, how carelessly you have thrown your jewellery in here, like radishes and carrots! My daughter, have new cord put through your locket; your earrings, your leaf-pendants, your crocodile-head bangles, your ear-studs and your armlets have all gone filthy with grime; the dirt is eating away the gold. Have them cleaned."

Mizajdar said, "Who is to have the cord put in? And who is to get them cleaned? If I ask him, he says, 'I have no time.'"

The *hajjan* said, "Why, my daughter, that's no great matter! Look, leave the pearls here; I'll have the cord put in right now, and give me whatever jewellery is dirty and I'll have it cleaned at once."

Mizajdar handed over all the jewellery. The *hajjan* said, "Send Zulfan with me too; she can sit at the goldsmith's while I get the cords put in by the silk-man." Mizajdar said, "Very well," and called Zulfan. When she came, the *hajjan* said, "Girl, come along with me a moment; you sit at the goldsmith's shop."

The *hajjan* took the jewellery and Zulfan went with her. Once out of the lane, the *hajjan* opened the handkerchief and said to Zulfan, "Come, let's put what is to be cleaned on one side and what needs cords on the other."

While she was sorting the jewellery, the *hajjan* said, "Why, where is the nose-stud?" Zulfan said, "It must be in there; it's such a tiny thing, look in this bundle." Then the *hajjan* said, as if to herself, "Oh dear! It's been left on the lid of the *pan*-box. Zulfan, there's a girl, run and fetch it quickly."

Zulfan came running back and shouted from the door, "Bibi, the nose-stud has been left on the lid of the *pan*-box. The *hajjan* wants it; give it to me quickly. The *hajjan* is sitting in front of Dibba the grain-dealer's shop at the corner of the lane."

The moment she said this, Mizajdar Bahu's heart misgave her. She said to Zulfan, "Have you gone mad? What nose-stud? Where did I ever have one? Have you seen one? You wretch, run! See that the *hajjan* doesn't get away!"

Zulfan ran straight back; she looked this way and that for the *hajjan*, but found no trace of her anywhere. She came and told Mizajdar, "Bi, there's no sign of the *hajjan* anywhere; I've looked as far as the bazaar. Heaven knows where she vanished to in so short a time."

Hearing this, Mizajdar began to beat her head: "Alas, I've been robbed! Alas, I've been robbed! Oh, people, for God's sake, run!"

People ran as far as the Momgaron ka Chhatta. There they learned that a woman, a stray from heaven knows where, had come and lived there in a rented room for a month, and had left the house four days before. What could be done now? Muhammad Aqil came and heard it, beat his head and said to his wife, "You woman, you'll bring this house to utter ruin before you're done; I've known what you are from the start."

Mizajdar said, "Go on, get away with you! Now you stand there talking big! When you saw the trouser-cord, didn't you yourself tell me, 'Yes, be sure to look at the begum's things'?"

So husband and wife had a fine quarrel, and the whole neighbourhood gathered. As one thing led to another, it came out that this same *hajjan* had cheated the wife of Ahmad Bakhsh Khan in the Kanchani lane out of all her jewellery, on the pretext that she would get a holy man to double it. In the Cotton Market she had made such friends with the daughter of Miyan Masita that she had made off with her jewellery on the pretext of borrowing it. In short, the jewellery was gone and done with; only a great deal of talk remained.

The dishes had been stolen; the jewellery had been lost in this way; and when people looked at the pair of pearls worth a thousand rupees, they were worth three pice. The police station was informed, and people searched a great deal on their own account, but there was no trace of the *hajjan*; no, none at all.

Now hear what became of the clothes Akbari had received in her dowry. As long as she lived with her mother-in-law, the mother-in-law used to take them out every ten or fifteen days and put them in the sun. They set up on their own in the rainy season. The chest of clothes stayed just as it had been put away in the storeroom; the whole rainy season passed without its ever being looked at, and there it stayed. When the cold weather came and a light quilt was needed, the chest was opened. White ants had eaten up many of the clothes, and rats had gnawed great holes in them; not a garment had escaped whole. Girls who are petted and spoilt in childhood and do not learn skills and good management go through life in sorrow and trouble like Akbari. From all you have read about Akbari, you will have seen what misery her mother's and grandmother's spoiling brought her all her life. In childhood Akbari neither learned any skill nor was her temper corrected at all. When Akbari parted from her mother-in-law and set up a separate house, she had everything: dishes and pots, clothes and jewellery. Because she had no idea of managing a house, in a few days she had brought all her goods and possessions to nothing, and within a single year she was left with bare hands and bare ears. [TN: that is, without a bangle or earring left.] If Muhammad Aqil too had been as foolish and bad-tempered as she was, they might have broken with each other altogether; but Muhammad Aqil always behaved with good sense and decency.

Now hear about Asghari.

In that house this girl was like a rose in a garden, or the eye in a man's body. She had every kind of skill and every kind of good management. Good sense, skill, modesty, consideration: God had granted Asghari every good quality. From childhood she had disliked games and play, laughter and teasing; it was always reading, or the work of the house. No one ever saw her talking nonsense or quarrelling with anyone. All the women of the neighbourhood loved her like a daughter. Truly, happy the fortune of the mother and father whose daughter Asghari was, and lucky the house into which Asghari was to go as a bride.

Now, by the grace and kindness of God, Asghari had reached the age of thirteen. Her match with Muhammad Kamil had long been settled, and now there was talk of fixing the month and the day. Meanwhile Muhammad Kamil's mother had been so frightened by Akbari's ways ("one who has been scalded by milk blows on buttermilk before drinking it", as the proverb says) that the very thought of Akbari made her hair stand on end. In secret, Muhammad Kamil's mother intended to betroth her younger son into another house. Muhammad Aqil somehow came to know of this and said to his mother, "Mother, I hear you want to break off Muhammad Kamil's betrothal."

His mother said, "What can I tell you, son? I am in great perplexity about what to do and what not to do. I can't look you in the face; God has made me guilty towards you. We shall see what Muhammad Kamil's fate will be."

Muhammad Aqil said, "Mother, I tell you truly, Asghari is one girl in a thousand. You could search all your life with a lamp and not find a girl like Asghari. God has made her excellent and worthy both in looks and in character. Don't be anxious at all; in God's name, have the wedding. And if you are thinking of her elder sister, you must have heard the couplet:

'Not every woman is a woman, nor every man a man;
God did not make the five fingers alike.' [TN: Persian in the original: "na har zan zan ast o na har mard mard / khuda panj angusht yaksan na kard".]

Each has his own temper and his own nature. (Verse:)

'Of the thousand flowers in the garden,
see, Zafar, what a show there is!
Each has a colour all its own,
each has a scent all its own.' [TN: lines by Zafar, the pen name of Bahadur Shah, the last Mughal emperor.]

What comparison is there (God preserve us!) between your elder daughter-in-law and Asghari? (Half-verse:)

'What has dust to do with the pure heavens?' [TN: Persian in the original: "che nisbat khak ra ba alam-e pak".]

God grant it all goes well; after the wedding you will believe what I say."

Once Muhammad Aqil had praised Asghari so highly, the match with Muhammad Kamil was made firm. With the agreement of both families it was settled that, all being well, the marriage should take place on the day after Baqr-Eid.

Asghari's father, Durandesh Khan, was in service in the hills. A letter was sent to him. As soon as it arrived, the Khan Sahib's face lit up with joy; of all his children he loved Asghari most. At once he applied for leave, and was refused flatly. He tried hard, but nothing came of it; the cold weather was coming and the tour of inspection was about to begin, so his superior had a reasonable excuse. Durandesh Khan was very sorry not to get leave, but a servant is helpless; what could he do? "A poor man's anger falls on the poor man's own head"; he kept quiet and resigned himself. But his eldest son, Khairandesh Khan, was with him. He gave him five hundred rupees in cash, sent him home and explained everything to him. At home the jewellery, clothes and dishes were all ready beforehand. When Khairandesh Khan reached the house he bought rice, ghee, wheat, spices and salt, all in the quantities needed. The sewing of trimmings onto Asghari's clothes began. Her mother meant Asghari to have a dowry far greater than her elder sister's: her sets of clothes were to be richer, there were to be more pieces of jewellery, and her dishes were to be good heavy ones for daily use. Asghari lived in that very house, after all, and whatever happened she was sure to come to know of it. When Asghari heard that she was to get a bigger dowry than her sister, a foolish girl would have been pleased. Asghari was distressed, and began to think how she could stop her mother. At last, with much embarrassment, she said to Tamasha Khanam, her cousin on her mother's side, "I have heard such and such, and it is worrying me terribly. For several days I have been anxious and wondering, O God, what am I to do? It's good that you have come; as we are the same age I can speak to you without hesitation. Will someone make Mother understand that she must not give me a single thing more than my sister?"

When Tamasha Khanam heard this she said, "Well, my dear, you are a funny one! It's like the proverb: they gave the donkey salt, and he said, 'My eyes are sore.' God is giving it to you; why should you refuse?"

Asghari said, "Have you gone mad? There are several objections to it. You know my sister's temper; she is sure to be hurt. There will be needless ill-feeling with Mother, and my sister will think badly of me too."

Tamasha Khanam said, "My dear, what is there to be hurt about? Each has her own fortune; and there are a hundred ways of looking at it. She had her *bismillah* feast [TN: the ceremony when a child begins to learn the Quran], and her first fast was celebrated; she was betrothed for four years, and which festival or holiday was not kept for her then? Let her count the balance on this side of the account."

Asghari said, "That is true, but what everyone speaks of is the dowry. If the younger gets more, the elder is bound to be hurt. We live in the same neighbourhood and meet every day. Why do something that will come between our hearts?"

Tamasha Khanam said, "Sister, you are doing yourself harm for nothing. Why, in a month or two everyone will have forgotten all about it."

Asghari said, "Oh, Bi, for heaven's sake! What harm and what gain? Does what parents give ever last a lifetime? Does anyone live a whole life on a dowry? Let God give out of His power. Don't press me on this, or I shall find some other way. I will not agree to it on any account."

In the end this reached Asghari's mother, and she, after thinking it over, gave up her plan, saying to herself, "There are a hundred ways of giving; I'll make it up to her some other way."

So on the appointed day, at an auspicious hour, the marriage took place, and the cries of "Congratulations!" and "Blessings!" began. Khairandesh Khan was such a good manager that all on his own he carried out his sister's wedding excellently. The bridegroom's party was entertained handsomely, each according to his rank, and all who had customary dues were very well satisfied. When the time came for Asghari to leave, the house was in an uproar. Her mother was grief-stricken beyond measure. As for the women of the neighbourhood, one after another they came and hugged Asghari and wept, and blessings came from every heart. Asghari entered her husband's home with a great, rich dowry of these blessings.

All the customs of that house were carried out. After the *runumai* [TN: the ceremony of first seeing the bride's face, when gifts are given], Asghari Khanam was given the title of "Tamizdar Bahu" (the Well-Mannered Bride).

As you read on, you will learn how Asghari managed the household, what difficulties she met and how she overcame them by her good sense. One should compare Asghari's situation with Akbari's. Asghari was her mother's second daughter and her mother-in-law's second daughter-in-law; on both sides all the hopes and eagerness had been spent on Akbari's wedding. Akbari was married at sixteen, and Asghari was not even a full thirteen at her wedding. When Akbari was married her bridegroom, Muhammad Aqil, was in service on ten rupees a month, while Asghari's bridegroom, Muhammad Kamil, was still a student. Compared with Muhammad Aqil, Muhammad Kamil had less learning and less sense as well. Akbari was free of the bother of children for two full years, but God made Asghari a mother in the second year of her marriage, at a very young age. Akbari never had occasion to leave the city; Asghari spent years travelling. So in every way Asghari's situation was no better than Akbari's; but Asghari had been well brought up from childhood. Day by day the blessings in her house increased, until now no one even knows Akbari's name, while in the Khanam ka Bazar there stands the lofty mansion of the Tamizdar Bahu, so high that it talks with the sky, and the quarter has become known as the Khanam ka Bazar after Asghari Khanam's name. In the Jauhari Bazar, the high mosque with the tank and the well was built by the Tamizdar Bahu. Beyond the Khas Bazar, beside the Lal Diggi, Tamizganj is hers. In the mosque of Maulvi Muhammad Hayat Sahib, twenty travellers still receive leavened bread and a stew of chickpeas twice a day from her alms-kitchen. At Qutb Sahib [TN: the shrine at Mehrauli, near Delhi], next to the Auliya Mosque, the inn was built by this same Tamizdar Bahu. In Fatehpuri [TN: the Fatehpuri mosque in Delhi] she once gave away five hundred Qurans printed in Bombay in a single day. Every winter to this day a thousand blankets are given to the poor from her house.
