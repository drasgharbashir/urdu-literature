# A House Run on Twenty Rupees

In this way, after much ado, Asghari got rid of her enemy and freed the house from torment. When the matter of Azmat had been settled, Asghari again asked leave to go to her father, and with everyone's good will she went to her mother's house. She stayed there a full week and settled everything on which she wanted her father's advice. Her father asked, "Has Azmat gone?" Asghari said, "Thanks to you, it has all ended well. If my elder brother hadn't gone to Lahore, and Father-in-law hadn't come, these years of accounts would never have been settled and Azmat would never have been turned out."

The Khan Sahib asked, "How will the house be managed now?"

Asghari said, "I came over here the moment the maid left. What is so hard about managing now? All the trouble was this Azmat's doing; now I'll see to everything myself."

The Khan Sahib asked, "And what new arrangements have you made in the house?"

Asghari said, "So far I haven't been able to look into anything; the business with Azmat came up from the very start. But now I mean to think everything over and make proper arrangements; and, God Most High willing, I'll keep you informed by letter."

Since her marriage the Khan Sahib had settled ten rupees a month on Asghari. He asked her, "If you are short of money, I'll give you some rupees before I go."

Asghari said, "Those ten rupees are more than I need; in fact all the money up to today is still saved up with me. What would I do with more? And when I need it, I'll ask you myself."

So Asghari took leave of her father and came back. When she reached her husband's home, she found her mother-in-law blowing up the kitchen fire. Asghari asked in surprise, "What! Haven't you engaged a maid yet?"

Her mother-in-law said, "Several women have come, but when I hear the wages they want, I haven't the heart to engage any of them. Azmat was bad, but she served for twenty-five years at eight annas a month. Every maid who comes now won't take less than two rupees and her food. I was waiting for you to come."

Asghari said, "I have a maid in mind too, but she also asks high wages: Diyanat-un-Nisa, Kifayat-un-Nisa's younger sister. She can cook and sew and do everything; and Kifayat-un-Nisa once told me that if there were a good place, Diyanat-un-Nisa would be ready to take service."

Muhammad Kamil's mother asked, "What wages will she want?"

Asghari said, "She herself asks three rupees and her food; but if we talk to her, perhaps she will agree to two."

Muhammad Kamil's mother said, "If it's to be two rupees and food, the mother of Chuniya, Bhondu the innkeeper's daughter, is begging at the door for the place."

Asghari said, "I wouldn't engage Chuniya's mother even at four annas a month."

Muhammad Kamil's mother asked, "Oh! Why not?"

Asghari said, "A servant who lives near by is a bad thing. The moment your back is turned, she takes whatever she likes and goes and puts it in her own house. And since her house is right next to ours, Chuniya's mother will be going off home every moment, and perhaps spend the night there as well."

Muhammad Kamil's mother said, "Bakhshu's wife has asked me several times for her daughter Zulfan; and Zulfan lives at Sayyid Firoz's bungalow."

Asghari asked, "You mean the Zulfan who always goes about so dressed up?"

Muhammad Kamil's mother said, "Yes; dressed up, what of it? She is newly married and is a little fond of clothes."

Asghari said, "A person like that shouldn't be engaged either."

Muhammad Kamil's mother said, "Zulfan's mother herself is willing to take service."

Asghari said, "She has a little tail of a younger daughter tied to her, who never leaves her mother for a moment. So the name will be one servant, but there will be two to feed."

Muhammad Kamil's mother said, "Well, I can't think of anyone else."

Asghari said, "Look, I'll send for that Diyanat-un-Nisa."

Muhammad Kamil's mother said, "And what about the wages?"

Asghari said, "An honest servant can hardly be had for low wages. With such people it is worth giving three instead of two; but I won't give the likes of Azmat eight annas to have the house plundered. The saying is true: 'Dear for a good reason, cheap for a bad one.'"

That day mother-in-law and daughter-in-law cooked the meal together. After the meal Asghari took Mahmuda with her and went up to the roof. While the Maulvi Sahib was there, Asghari came down from the roof very little; she came down only in the morning and the evening, and she had even told Mahmuda, "Don't keep going downstairs all the time." Mahmuda, being a child, asked, "Dear sister-in-law, why not?"

Asghari said, "One doesn't keep walking about in front of one's elders."

After the meal a quarrel began between the Maulvi Sahib and his wife over the household accounts. His wife complained, "You give far too little for expenses. Here I have to see to weddings and marriages, the give and take of the community, visits and calls, festivals and holidays, all of it." The Maulvi Sahib said, "Twenty rupees a month is not little. You have no idea of management; that is why there is no blessing in this house." Just then the Maulvi Sahib called Mahmuda. When Mahmuda came, he said, "Go and fetch your sister-in-law." When Asghari heard she was sent for, she wondered why she was wanted at that time. She asked Mahmuda, "What's going on?"

Mahmuda said, "There's a quarrel going on."

When Asghari came, the Maulvi Sahib said, "Well, my child, who is to manage things now?"

Asghari said, "Mother will, as she always has."

The Maulvi Sahib said, "We have seen the result of her management. A house into which twenty rupees a month comes, and look at it: not one decent cooking pot, not one thing fit for a respectable family. If a spoonful of sherbet were wanted at any time, I dare say not even the makings of it would be found in the house."

Asghari said, "What fault is it of Mother's? That wretched Azmat ruined the house."

The Maulvi Sahib said, "If she had any sense of management, what power would Azmat have had? Was Azmat the servant or the mistress of the house?"

Asghari said, "When a servant of twenty-five years' standing girds up her loins to plunder, who can see through her tricks? One cannot suspect such an old servant."

The Maulvi Sahib said, "Well, you suspected her in the end, didn't you?"

Asghari said, "What did I suspect? It was her own evil star that made her stir up the business of the suit and wake the sleeping hornets."

Then her mother-in-law spoke up: "Out of fifty you keep thirty rupees for yourself alone, and for the family here, twenty!"

The Maulvi Sahib said, "How can the expenses of a house be compared with the expenses of a man away from home? You think of me as one person alone; but what about a servant, a carriage, a house, clothes and linen?"

His wife said, "The carriage and the house are provided by the household you serve."

The Maulvi Sahib said, "I am given a horse, but I have to feed it grain and grass out of my own pocket. There's a groom at four rupees, and repairs to the house. Then there is keeping up a position fit for the nobleman's court, giving and receiving, a thousand worries; I don't know how I manage."

Asghari turned to her mother-in-law and said, "Mother, what is the use of arguing over twenty rupees? Whatever we get, thank God a thousand times. May God bless Father's earnings; even this much is worth thousands."

Her mother-in-law said, "My daughter, I can't run the house on twenty."

Asghari stopped her mother-in-law with a sign and said to the Maulvi Sahib, "Give two rupees less if you like, but let whatever you give come every month without fail. When there is no money at the proper time, we have to borrow, and borrowing drives away whatever blessing is left in a house."

The Maulvi Sahib said, "In Indian households the custom and practice in paying salaries is very bad: sometimes they are paid every six months, sometimes once a year. That is why expenses cannot be kept regular. But I'll tell Hazari Mal before I go to give you twenty rupees every month."

Asghari asked, "If the moneylender pays it, will he ask you for interest?"

The Maulvi Sahib said, "No, why should he take interest? He has dealings with our nobleman's household too; an order will come from there."

Asghari said, "Then there is no objection."

So the allowance was fixed at twenty rupees; but Muhammad Kamil's mother was displeased, and she took Asghari aside and complained to her.

Asghari said, "God willing, I'll run the house on twenty; please don't worry about that. And really the Maulvi Sahib cannot keep up his position on less than thirty rupees. In an agent's post, to begin with, there is no way of getting anything extra; and even if there were, why would the Maulvi Sahib take it? So it is 'counted pieces of meat and measured gravy'; it would not be right for the Maulvi Sahib to live in hardship just so that a few more rupees come into the house."

Hearing this, her mother-in-law fell silent. Asghari sent for Diyanat-un-Nisa and, after some talk, got her to agree to two rupees and her food, and warned her: "Diyanat-un-Nisa, take care that nothing happens to lessen the trust we place in you. Live here just as your elder sister lives in our house."

Diyanat-un-Nisa said, "Madam, may God strike dead the hour when I cast my eyes on another's goods! If I need anything I'll ask you for it and eat it, but I count it a sin to taste so much as salt without your leave."

The day after Eid the Maulvi Sahib went back to Lahore. Asghari had all the things needed bought in bulk, and from then on she always laid in stores together, buying in season when things were cheap: chillies, onions, coriander, grain, lentils, rice, coarse sugar, firewood, dung-cakes, potatoes, *arvi* [TN: colocasia root], turnips, fenugreek and dill greens; everything was bought at the right time. Counting the maid there were five people. Three-quarters of a *ser* of meat came for the two meals, and Diyanat made two kinds of dish from it: sometimes half with vegetables and half plain, sometimes half as kebabs. At one meal, in the daytime, there were lentils, and on the seventh day the custom was *pulao* and sweet rice. In the house there were two or three kinds of chutney, one sweet-and-sour, one made with mint water, one with vinegar; two or three kinds of preserves were kept ready; besides these she made a bottle each of pomegranate sherbet, lemon *sikanjbin* [TN: a syrup of vinegar or lemon and sugar], violet sherbet, water-lily sherbet and *phalsa* sherbet. Every kind of necessary thing was always in the house. In spite of all these provisions, not more than fifteen rupees was spent. With the five rupees saved, things of this kind were bought: two big cooking pots holding five and ten *sers*, a tray, some small spoons, two water-jugs, and a tea set; two chests were made; two cupboards, one in the kitchen and one in the storeroom. The wooden platforms for sitting on were old; they were repaired, and two bedsteads were made. In short, with these twenty rupees Asghari gave the house such a look that, to all appearances, it seemed very prosperous. She brought economy and order into everything. In Azmat's time, three or four pice worth of things had always been bought from the bazaar every day for Mahmuda, because not a scrap was left over on the cloth after meals. Now two or four loaves were left over on the cloth at both meals. Sometimes she would put aside two pieces of the fried meat for Mahmuda, sometimes give her a pinch of sugar, sometimes a slice of preserve. The daily purchases stopped; only on a rare day, if Mahmuda fancied something, it was sent for. Never in all its days had a beggar received a pinch of flour or half a loaf from that house; now two loaves were given to beggars at every meal. The household things had always lain about in a strange, slovenly way, like greens and radishes; now everything had its place. The bundles of clothes held clothes properly folded and tied up in order. In the storeroom for grain and water, everything was carefully covered. The dishes were clean and shining and kept in their places, the china apart and the copper apart. It was as though the house were a machine whose parts were all in good order, and the key of the machine was in Asghari's hand; when she wound it up, the machine ran as it should.

Gradually two or four rupees began to be saved, and Asghari kept on putting this aside as money held in trust. From the time Asghari took the management of the house into her hands, borrowing was forsworn; never, even by mistake, did anything come from the bazaar on credit, not so much as a farthing's worth. Asghari wrote down all the household accounts in a book. When something was nearly finished and Diyanat-un-Nisa told her, "Madam, there is ghee for two more days," Asghari would take out her book and see on what date how much ghee had come and at what daily rate it had been used; and if it had been used beyond the reckoning, she called Diyanat-un-Nisa to account. No one dared to waste anything or let it go unaccounted for. Even the charges of the woman who ground the flour and the washerwoman's charges were written down in Asghari's book.
