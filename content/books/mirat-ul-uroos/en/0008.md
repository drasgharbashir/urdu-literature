# Mama Azmat

One day in the rainy season, when the sky was overcast with clouds, a fine drizzle was falling and a cool wind was blowing, Muhammad Kamil said, "Today I feel like some *karhai* [TN: sweets fried in a deep pan], but only if the Tamizdar Bahu sees to it." Asghari used to live upstairs on the roof, and she did not know that Muhammad Kamil had asked for *karhai*. Mama Azmat brought the ghee, sugar, gram flour and the rest, and said to Muhammad Kamil, "Here you are, young master, I've brought all the things. Shall I go and fetch the bride?" She went up to the roof, but said not a word to Asghari about the *karhai*; she came straight back down again and said, "The bride says she has a headache."

Mama Azmat could not even cook an ordinary meal; how on earth was she to make *karhai*? She ruined all the ingredients. Muhammad Kamil had asked for it with such longing; he ate the tasteless fried stuff and was very downcast. When he went up to the roof he saw his wife sewing her own trousers. He was displeased in his heart: "What! No headache when it comes to sewing, but the moment I ask for a little *karhai* she makes the excuse of a headache!" This was the first displeasure Muhammad Kamil felt towards Asghari; and it is the way of things that discord between husbands and wives grows from just such little matters. Since marriage usually takes place at a very young age, by the grace of God neither the husband nor the wife has any prudent sense. If the least thing goes against their liking, the husband sits apart, stiff with offence, and the wife lies apart with her face turned away; and when they live together in one place, what wonder is it that little points of disagreement keep arising? These disagreements, happening over and over, do great damage to their unity and harmony; consideration and regard disappear on both sides, and all their lives there is squabbling. [TN: literally "lentils are shared out in shoes", an idiom for constant bickering.] The best course is for husband and wife to keep their dealings with each other clear from the start, and not to let even the slightest ill-feeling arise; otherwise these same little grievances will pile up and at last turn into great strife and a breach. And the way to stop ill-feeling arising is this: whenever even the smallest thing goes against one's liking, not to keep it in one's heart, but to speak of it face to face and clear it up. If Muhammad Kamil had had sense and had known this wisdom, he would surely have asked his wife, by way of complaint, "Well, madam, couldn't you manage such a little thing, that you made the false excuse of a headache?" The matter would have been settled there and then in a few words, and Mama Azmat's mischief would have come out. But Muhammad Kamil set a seal on his lips and wrote out a whole ledger of complaints in his heart.

Asghari was uneasy at Muhammad Kamil's coolness, and she thought, "God help us, it looks like the beginning of a quarrel." When she looked at her mother-in-law she found her troubled too. She was bewildered: "O God, what is the matter?"

Before this had been settled, Mama Azmat struck another blow. Ramzan was near. Muhammad Kamil's mother said to Mama Azmat, "Mama, Ramzan is coming; start getting ready now. All the dishes, big and small, need to be tinned; it is a whole year since the house was whitewashed. Tell Lala Hazari Mal to find us twenty-five rupees somehow from somewhere; the Eid expenses are coming upon us." Mama Azmat said, "The Tamizdar Bahu is going to stay with her mother; and I have heard that the Tahsildar [TN: Asghari's father, Durandesh Khan, a revenue officer] is coming too; he is sure to send for both his daughters. And I have heard somewhere that the Tamizdar Bahu means to go away with her father. If the bride goes, the young master will go too; then, madam, you will be all alone. What will be the use of whitewashing the house, and what will be the use of tinning the dishes? And that wretch Hazari Mal has grown so unobliging that his man stands at the door every day demanding payment; how is he going to lend more?"

When Muhammad Kamil's mother heard this her heart sank, and with reason. From the day her husband had gone to Lahore he had never come back to show his face at home. Every six months or once a year, when he felt like it, he would send something; otherwise he had nothing to do with them. Muhammad Aqil had already separated from his mother. Muhammad Kamil was the only one left at home, and once he had gone the field would be empty. Muhammad Kamil's mother asked the maid, "Tell me the truth, girl, is the Tamizdar Bahu really going?" The maid said, "Madam, God knows whether she is going or not. I told you what I heard." Muhammad Kamil's mother asked, "You wretch, who did you hear it from? How did you come to know?"

The maid said, "If you ask how I heard it, I asked Kifayat-un-Nisa for a loan of two rupees, and she said, 'I would lend it to you, but I am going to the hills.' Then I asked her about it, and from her I learned that everything has been settled; they are only waiting for the Tahsildar to come, and on the morning of Eid they will all set off. And why should it depend on hearing? We have not seen God, but we know Him by reason. Madam, can't you tell from the Tamizdar Bahu's ways? Look, at first the bride used to see to the work of the house, but now she has sworn never to come down from the roof. Letter after letter goes off to her father. What else can it be but going away?"

Hearing this, Muhammad Kamil's mother was struck dumb, and she was sitting lost in thought when Muhammad Kamil came in from outside. She called Muhammad Kamil to her and said, "Kamil, I want to ask you something; will you tell me the truth?"

Muhammad Kamil said, "Why, Mother, what is there that I would hide from you?"

Muhammad Kamil's mother told him, word for word, everything she had heard from the maid.

Muhammad Kamil said, "Mother, I tell you truly, I know nothing whatever about it, and the Tamizdar Bahu has never spoken of it to me."

Muhammad Kamil's mother said, "Go on, you liar! Spinning tales to me? Such a big thing, and you know nothing about it!"

Muhammad Kamil said, "You won't believe me; I swear by your head, I don't know."

Just then the maid came in too. Muhammad Kamil's mother said, "Well, Azmat, Kamil says he doesn't know."

The maid said, "Young master, take it ill or well as you please, but your wife is certainly getting ready to go; perhaps she is hiding it from you. She is not like Mizajdar Bahu, who could not keep anything in her stomach; she is the Tamizdar Bahu, who would never let anyone into her secrets."

Muhammad Kamil's mother asked, "Well, Kamil, if this is true, what do you mean to do?"

Muhammad Kamil said, "Why, how could I go away and leave you alone? And as for the Tamizdar Bahu, how can she just go off without asking anyone? I'll ask the Tamizdar Bahu today, 'Tell me, what is all this?'"

Muhammad Kamil's mother said, "What reliance can be placed on what this wretched maid says? Don't say anything to the bride yet. When we have got to the bottom of it, we shall see."

With talk like this, Mama Azmat was trying to set Asghari's mother-in-law and husband against her. Although no one said anything to Asghari's face, she had understood from all their looks that there was certainly some coolness. Mahmuda was a great spy for Asghari; she told her every little thing, and the maid's wickedness had become quite clear to Asghari. But Asghari was not so foolish as to fly into a temper. She decided that in this matter it was not right for her to say anything on her own part; sooner or later the truth would come out, and then they would see. Asghari said to herself, "Very well, Azmat, just you wait; God Most High willing, I'll straighten you out properly! Your brains have so far gone to your head that you are stirring up strife in the very household. God willing, I'll strike you in a place where there is no water, and ruin you so thoroughly that you will never set foot in this neighbourhood again." [TN: "to strike where there is no water" is a proverbial phrase recalling the death of Husain at Karbala, cut off from water.]

Mama Azmat's evil hour was upon her; she struck a third blow at Asghari. It was Hazari Mal's habit, whenever he saw Mama Azmat passing his shop, to accost her on purpose: "Well, Mama, are you giving any thought to our account?" And every seven or eight days he sent a demand for payment to the house. One day, as usual, Mama Azmat was going to the bazaar to shop, and Hazari Mal stopped her. The maid said, "Oh, Lala, why have you made a rule of teasing me every day? Whenever you see me you dun me. Ask the people you lend to, dun them. I'm a poor woman, worth a couple of pice; what have I to do with the dealings of moneylenders?"

Hazari Mal said, "What do you mean, you have nothing to do with it? It's you who take things from the shop. Hand deals with hand; we know you, and we lend on your credit. What do we know of the family?" The maid said, "Oh, Lala, come to your senses! What a simpleton you are! What means did you ever see in me? I have no property and no money, and you gave me hundreds of rupees with your eyes shut! Well, if you gave it to me, go and get it back from me; sell up the palaces I own, and stop the pension I get from the Fort." [TN: the Red Fort, the palace of the last Mughal emperors, from which many people in Delhi received allowances.]

Hearing such rude talk from the maid, Hazari Mal was quite taken aback, and began to speak to her in a friendly way: "You look as if you have quarrelled with someone today; tell me, what is it? Did the mistress say something, or was the young master angry? Come in here." While he said this to the maid, he gave a pice to the boy who sat in the shop: "Run and get two rolls of *pan* made up, with tobacco in them." When the maid had sat down, Hazari Mal laughed and asked again, "It looks as if you've had a quarrel with someone today."

The maid said, "God forbid! Why should I quarrel? You said something, and I said something back. Why take offence at plain truth?"

Hazari Mal said, "That's right enough; the dealings are with the master, of course, but do they pass through your hands or not? There is no note or letter in our name; whatever you asked for in your master's name, we gave."

The maid said, "Yes, put it like that; when did I ever deny it? Whatever I have taken, I'll say so before thousands, before hundreds of thousands. And our mistress too (blessings come from every hair of my body for her), poor thing, never makes any fuss."

Hazari Mal said, "Mama, the Begum Sahib really is a great lady. What a fine woman!"

Then Hazari Mal asked quietly, "And how is the young bride? What is she like? Does she take after her elder sister, or is she of another temper?"

The maid said, "Lala, don't ask! She comes of a rich family, to be sure, but she is very mean at heart. Even a farthing's worth of shopping doesn't please her until she has sent it back four times. Yes, God keep her, in skill and good management she outdoes all the wives and daughters in the world: her cooking is the very best, and in sewing she beats the tailors and the Mughal seamstresses. But, Lala, she hasn't the ways of a lady. At first she started checking me too; but, Lala, you know how honest my work is; in the end she got tired of it and gave up. The Begum Sahib is a saint, and it is by the blessing of her presence that the house carries on. We poor folk too cling to the hem of her garment. Plenty of people have tried to stir the Begum Sahib up, but God keep her, she has never let it cloud her heart, nor spoken a word of it to anyone's face."

Hazari Mal said, "I hear the young bride got a very rich dowry."

The maid said at once, "Rubbish! It was even less than her sister's."

Hazari Mal said, "That's very strange. At the time of her marriage the Khan Sahib was a Tahsildar; he ought to have given more than to his elder daughter."

The maid said, "Oh dear, it was no fault of the Tahsildar. The poor man had made great preparations. It was this sly little madam herself, so free with her tongue: out of loyalty to her parents, she kept on at them until all the things were cut down."

Hazari Mal said, "If that's how it is, she'll set up a separate house just like her sister."

The maid said, "Set up a separate house? She'll do far worse than that. The elder bride was bad-tempered, but clean-hearted; this one is sweet of tongue and false at heart. However hard anyone works himself to death for her, she is never satisfied. Even when she speaks it is with something underneath: one thing to your face and another in her heart. No, sir, this woman won't get along with them for a single day. Now she is getting ready to go to her father in the hills."

Hazari Mal asked, "Has any letter come from Lahore lately?"

The maid said, "We are waiting every day; heaven knows why, but no letter has come. The mistress is waiting for money. Ramzan is upon us; why, the day before yesterday she was saying to me, 'Go and borrow fifty rupees from Hazari Mal.'"

At the word "loan" Hazari Mal started and said, "Let her first make some arrangement for the money she owes; then why should I refuse in future? My partners won't agree now. Mama, explain it carefully to the Begum Sahib: let her pay the money from wherever she can, or else don't blame me."

The maid said, "Only God will get your money out of her, if it comes out at all. Where is the Begum Sahib to find it? She is in debt up to every hair of her head. The grocer is worrying the life out of her on one side, and the cloth-merchant is making an outcry on the other."

Hazari Mal said, "What have I to do with the other creditors? The Begum Sahib will have to settle our shop's account in full whatever happens. I have great regard for the Begum Sahib's household, but my partner, Chhadami Lal, won't listen. If he got to hear of this, he would bring a suit today."

The maid said, "I'll tell the Begum Sahib all this, that's all I can do. But I know every little thing about the house; sue her or petition against her, there's no money and no means of paying. If there were money, why would she have borrowed?"

After all this talk Mama Azmat took leave of Hazari Mal, did her shopping and came home. Muhammad Kamil's mother asked, "Mama, when you go to the bazaar you become so careless that you forget all about cooking. Look how late in the day it is! When will the meat go on the fire now, when will it be cooked, when shall we get our meal?"

The maid said, "Madam, I was kept all this time quarrelling with that wretched Hazari Lal. The plague-stricken fellow stops me every day as I go past. Today I lost my temper and said, 'Have you made a rule of insulting me every day? What are you dying for? Have a little patience; let the money come from Lahore, and all your accounts, old and new, will be paid in full.' The wretch flew at me and began to disgrace me in the middle of the bazaar."

Muhammad Kamil's mother said, "What has come over Hazari Mal? He was never like that. After all, we have dealt with him for years; sometimes we have paid early and sometimes late, but he never made trouble."

The maid said, "Madam, some other moneylender has gone into partnership in the shop, and that wretch is in a great hurry. He has collected on the spot from everyone who owed money, and sued anyone who didn't pay. Hazari Mal said, 'Tell the Begum Sahib from me, with folded hands, that I can do nothing about it; let her find some way to pay the money in a few days somehow, or Chhadami Lal will certainly sue.'"

Hearing this news, Muhammad Kamil's mother was greatly troubled. Amir Begum, her younger sister, lived in the Khanam ka Bazar and was fairly well off. Muhammad Kamil's mother said to Mama Azmat, "Mama, not even an answer to my letter comes from Lahore; what hope is there of money? If Hazari Mal really does sue, what will happen? I haven't even enough belongings to sell to pay him; and to pay after being sued is a disgrace too, and our name will be blackened throughout the city. Bring a *doli*; I'll go to Amir Begum and see whether some way can be found there."

The maid said, "Madam, the suit is as good as filed; when a man has said it with his own mouth, how long will it take him to do it? And where is poor Chhoti Begum Sahib to get money from? She is in difficulties herself these days."

Muhammad Kamil's mother said, "In the end, something will have to be done."

The maid went close to her and said quietly, "If the Tamizdar Bahu would lend her bangles for a month, our good name would be saved. For the present we could pawn them and pay off a half or a third of Hazari Mal's account. Within a month either the master would send money, or I would get it from some other moneylender."

Muhammad Kamil's mother said, "Girl, are you out of your mind? Take care! Don't even let such a thing pass your lips. I would rather the very house we live in were sold than have to face asking the bride."

The maid said, "Madam, I only said it because, after all, a bride is a daughter; she is not a stranger. And does anyone, God forbid, mean to sell them? It's only for a month. After all, instead of lying in her box they would be lying with the moneylender, so that he would feel secure."

Muhammad Kamil's mother said, "All the same, there is a great difference between a daughter-in-law and a daughter; and to a newly married bride, can anyone say such a thing? Look here, take care never to let that pass your lips again, or Mahmuda may hear it and go and tell the bride."

The maid said, "The young lady was standing here just now listening, but she's a child; she doesn't understand such things yet."

Muhammad Kamil's mother said, "Bring a *doli*; I'll go to my sister at any rate, and then we'll see what advice we get."
