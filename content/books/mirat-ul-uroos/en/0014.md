# Lessons in the School

When Safihan came the next day, Asghari showed her everything: the girls' embroidery, the gold braid the girls had woven, the *gokhru* trimmings they had twisted, the edgings and *champa* lace they had made, and the men's and women's clothes they had cut out and sewn. The sight of them filled Safihan with the greatest wonder. After this she showed her the girls' dolls' houses. In these houses all the requisites of housekeeping, carpets and floor-spreads, bolsters, spittoons, basin and ewer, a betel box, curtains, bamboo blinds, a canopy, a fan, a mosquito net, a bedstead, every kind of vessel and every kind of ornament, were set out each in its proper place; and the dolls were so dressed up that it was exactly as if guests had gathered in a house where there was a wedding. When she had seen the dolls' houses, Asghari said to Safihan, "Of all the girls' games, I like the game of dolls best. Through it the girls learn sewing, the cutting out of clothes, and the management of a house, and come to know the customs of every kind of family occasion: the sixth-day ceremony after a birth, the first feeding of rice pudding, the weaning, the *bismillah*, the first fast, betrothal, the Eid presents, the presents of the rainy season, the *qufliyan* and braid of Muharram, the festivals of the rainy season, the wedding gifts, the bridegroom's procession, the return visit, the wedding itself, the bride's journeys, and the customs of the fourth day after the wedding. Safihan dear, your daughter has been coming only a few days; but of the girls who have been in my school for a long time, like Ummunnabin sitting here, or my sister-in-law Mahmuda, or Husn-ara, I can say, touching wood, that if the management of some large, full household were handed to them now, God willing, they would run it like a woman of great practice and experience. I do not insist only on reading; I make them fit for the work of the world, which will fall on their heads in a few days."

With that Asghari called Husn-ara and said, "My dear, your doll's house is beautifully furnished; there is only one thing lacking: your dolls don't seem to have any coloured clothes. Perhaps you don't know how to dye."

Husn-ara said, "Mahmuda Begum has taught me a great many colours; I just haven't dyed any."

Asghari said, "Well, tell me, then."

Husn-ara said, "Teacher, the colours for the rains: red, orange, pomegranate-flower, peach-blossom, *sardai*, paddy-green, purple; and for winter: marigold, ochre, jujube, moss-green, oily, *kakrezi* (dark purple), black, blue, rose-pink, saffron, *kokai*, *karanjwi*; and for summer: onion-pink, water-blue, *champa*-yellow, cotton-white, almond, camphor-white, milk-white, poppy, *phalsa*-purple, sandalwood, vermilion. There are many more colours, but I have named the ones most often worn."

Asghari asked, "You have reeled off the names of a great many colours; but tell me this: do you know how to dye all of them?"

Husn-ara said, "I named only the colours I know how to dye myself."

Asghari said, "Well, tell me, then: how do they dye *sardai*?"

Husn-ara said, "I sent for half a yard of good deep-coloured green *qand* cloth, boiled the water well, and put in alum and mixed it; the alum makes the colour of the *qand* run; then I dyed the cloth in it."

Asghari said, "Well, and if *qand* is not to be had?"

Husn-ara said, "Then boil *tesu* flowers [TN: the flame-of-the-forest], grind alum and add it, and it will turn out *sardai*, but a pale cotton-like shade; a good *sardai* cannot be dyed without *qand*. And if the colour is drawn from broadcloth instead of *qand*, it gives such a fine colour that you can only say 'Glory be to God!' But these days magenta has become so fashionable that it has beaten every other colour; never mind clothes, even the gold braid on sweetmeats is dyed a lovely colour in magenta. My elder sister sent some sweet rice cooked in the colour of magenta; it was a better colour than saffron."

Asghari Khanam asked in alarm, "Husn-ara, you didn't eat that rice dyed with magenta, did you?"

Husn-ara said, "No, I didn't eat it; but teacher, why? Is there something wrong with it?"

Asghari Khanam said, "Oh dear, there is arsenic in magenta; be careful! Never put anything with magenta in it on your tongue."

Husn-ara said, "But I've eaten a lot of braid dyed with magenta at Muharram."

Asghari Khanam said, "What of it? A great deal of braid is dyed with the merest trace of magenta; that is why it did you no harm. But remember that there is poison in it."

Husn-ara said, "People eat sweets dyed with magenta by the maund."

Asghari Khanam said, "They do very wrong. When the poison reaches its full dose, it will certainly take effect."

When evening came, the girls put away their embroidery and books and sat down as usual to play and to tell and hear stories and riddles. Asghari said to Safihan, "Here there are no stories of the sparrow and his wife. There is a very fine book of stories, with excellent stories in it, and from each story a lesson can be drawn; the language of this book is very polished too. Now these girls will amuse themselves with the stories from that book. Telling stories makes their speech clear and their power of saying what they mean grows; and whenever I have leisure, I take them up in the middle of the stories, and they answer me as far as they understand; if the answer is wrong, I tell them. Guessing riddles develops their minds and sharpens their wits. But you sit among them and watch the fun; Aliya's mother has sent for me today. Her baby is not well, and she has sent many, many entreaties; if I don't go she will be hurt, and my own heart won't let me stay away."

Safihan said, "Yes, I've heard too that her boy hasn't taken milk for several days; the poor woman is very frightened. Oh, God grant the little mite lives! He is a child of many prayers; after ten years of longing God showed her this face. And there was a child after Aliya too. Teacher, she must have sent for you to treat him."

Asghari said, "I know nothing whatever of treatment. Once before this boy had a thirst on him, and I suggested two or four remedies of the kind, *zahr-mohra* (bezoar stone), *banslochan* (bamboo manna), rose-cumin, small cardamom, cumin kernels, cubebs and purslane; by God's doing, the boy got well."

Safihan said, "Teacher, you are perfect in every accomplishment."

Asghari said, "What accomplishment is there in that? In my parents' home great care is taken over medicines; when I was little, whatever medicine came, I was the one who strained it and made it up and kept an eye on it. In that way I remember two or four remedies I have heard of; I tell them to whoever needs them. And women usually see to the treatment of children themselves; when some serious difficulty arises, they take the child to the *hakim* (physician)."

Safihan said, "Teacher, you have kindly shown me all the arrangements of your school; for God's sake, stay just a moment, so that I can see how the girls tell stories and how you teach through the stories."

Asghari said, "My dear, I'm getting late; but very well, for your sake. Now, girls, whose turn is it today?"

Mahmuda said, "It's Amatullah's turn, but have Fazilat tell one."

Asghari said, "Very well, Fazilat, tell some very short story."

Fazilat began the story: "Once there was a king."

Asghari asked, "Whom do we call a king?"

Fazilat said, "Like Bahadur Shah in Delhi."

Asghari: "You have said something that only someone who knows Delhi and Bahadur Shah would understand."

Fazilat said, "A king means a ruler."

Asghari: "Then the police chief and the head constable are kings too."

Fazilat: "No, the police chief and the head constable aren't kings; they are the king's servants."

Asghari: "Why? Isn't the police chief a ruler?"

Fazilat: "He is a ruler, but the king is the greatest ruler of all, and gives orders to everyone."

Asghari: "Who is our king?"

Fazilat: "Ever since the English took Bahadur Shah prisoner to the Black Water [TN: transportation overseas; Bahadur Shah was in fact exiled to Rangoon], there hasn't been any king."

At this all the girls burst out laughing.

Asghari said, "Fazilat, you are very silly. You said yourself that the one who is the greatest ruler and gives orders to everyone is the king; and you also know that the English took Bahadur Shah prisoner to the Black Water; so are the English kings or not?"

Fazilat: "Yes, they are, all right."

Asghari: "Very well, now tell me: who is our king?"

Fazilat: "The English."

Asghari: "What, is 'the English' the name of some particular person?"

Fazilat: "No, there are hundreds and thousands of English."

Asghari: "What, are all the English kings?"

Fazilat: "What else?"

At this the girls laughed again. Asghari signed to Husn-ara: "You answer."

Husn-ara said, "Teacher, our king is Queen Victoria."

Asghari: "Is it a man or a woman?"

Husn-ara: "A woman."

Asghari: "Where does she live?"

Husn-ara: "In London."

Asghari: "Where is London?"

Husn-ara: "It is a very large city in the country of the English."

Asghari: "How far away would it be?"

Husn-ara: "I have seen it written in a book as five thousand *kos*."

Asghari: "How long is a *kos*?"

Husn-ara: "Teacher, they call it three *kos* to Sultan Nizamuddin." [TN: the shrine of the saint Nizamuddin, outside Delhi.]

At this Mahmuda laughed and said, "It is one thousand seven hundred and sixty yards."

Asghari asked Mahmuda, "That time when I went to Qutb Sahib [TN: the shrine of Qutbuddin Bakhtiyar Kaki at Mehrauli, near the Qutb Minar], and you were with me, you saw too that every little way there were stones set up along the road, with writing on them. What stones were those?"

Mahmuda: "I guessed they were *kos* stones; but the carriage was going so fast that I couldn't fix my eyes on them, and I couldn't properly read what was written on them."

Asghari: "Those weren't *kos* stones, they were milestones. A mile is half a *kos*, and at every mile a stone is set up; on it is written how many miles it is from there to Delhi and how many to Qutb Sahib."

After this Asghari turned to Husn-ara again and asked, "Yes, my dear, in which direction is London?"

Husn-ara: "It's in the north."

Asghari: "Is that country hot or cold?"

Husn-ara: "That I don't know."

Mahmuda: "It is very cold; the farther north you go, the less heat there is, and the farther south you go, the hotter it becomes."

Safihan: "Dear teacher, a woman is king?"

Asghari: "What is surprising about that?"

Safihan: "Why isn't it surprising? What can a mere woman do?"

Asghari: "Whatever male kings do, a woman does the same: the government of the country and the care of its subjects."

Safihan: "What can a woman do? The English must be doing everything; they must have made a woman king in name only."

Asghari: "All these English are the Queen's servants; each has his own work, each his own separate authority; they stay ready at their own tasks. And even when men are kings, it is the ministers and officers who do all the work."

Safihan: "My heart just won't accept that a mere woman can rule a kingdom."

Asghari: "Have you heard the name of the Begum of Bhopal?"

Safihan: "Of course! Why wouldn't I have heard it? My own father-in-law is in service at Bhopal."

Asghari: "Then understand it in the same way. Bhopal is a tiny country, and Queen Victoria has a vast empire. Just as the Begum of Bhopal governs her small country, Queen Victoria rules her vast empire. Bhopal is a small government, with few servants and small salaries; Queen Victoria's is a great and exalted government, with great establishments, hundreds of thousands of servants, and handsome salaries."

Safihan: "Dear, has the Queen a husband?"

Asghari: "Yes, but no one has power over death; God has put a spot even on the moon. Some years ago the Queen was widowed."

Safihan: "Has the Queen any children?"

Asghari: "Yes, God keep them! Sons, grandsons, granddaughters, everything."

Safihan: "Dear, why doesn't the Queen come to this country?"

Asghari: "That is a great country too; she has no leisure from the business there. But the Queen's son is coming here soon; great preparations are being made; I saw it in the newspaper." [TN: the Prince of Wales toured India in 1875–76.]

Safihan: "Dear, sitting thousands of *kos* away, what can the Queen know of what happens here?"

Asghari: "Why not? She hears every smallest thing; news comes and goes day and night by post and electric telegraph; thousands of newspapers go to England."

Safihan: "How could one see the Queen?"

Asghari: "How can I tell you? But you can certainly see her picture."

Safihan: "Well, I'd see her picture, then."

Asghari: "My dear, you do say funny things. Haven't you ever seen a rupee?"

Safihan: "Of course I have."

Asghari: "The woman's face on it is the Queen's portrait. There is a picture of the Queen on postage stamps; and I have another very fine picture of the Queen; an Englishman gave it to my father, and he sent it to me. Mahmuda, bring my little box."

Asghari took the Queen's picture out of the little box and showed it, and all the girls looked at the Queen's picture with the greatest eagerness.

Safihan: "What a fine picture! It's the Queen standing there herself."

Asghari: "Certainly this picture is the Queen's very likeness; compare it with the face on the rupee and see how different it is! This picture is not drawn by hand. There is a glass; they put some chemicals on it and set it in front of the person, and the likeness comes out of itself, exactly as it is." [TN: a photograph.]

Safihan: "Husn-ara said London was five thousand *kos* away, so it must take years to go there and back from here."

Asghari: "No, by sea they get there comfortably in a month."

Safihan: "Oh dear, you have to go by sea! What hearts the English have! They aren't afraid of the sea. My hair stands on end just to hear the name of the sea."

Asghari: "What is there to be afraid of in the sea? You sit comfortably on a ship, and it becomes a regular house on the move."

Safihan: "Oh teacher, there is such a great danger of drowning! Look, only last year my husband's maternal aunt went on the *hajj* (pilgrimage to Mecca) with Nawab Qutbuddin Khan; she set out at such an unlucky hour that she was never fated to come back."

Asghari Khanam: "Yes, that is a matter of chance; ships do sink now and then. And if, God forbid, they sank every other day, no one would so much as mention travelling by sea. Nowadays the sea route is busier than the roads on land; thousands and hundreds of thousands of ships come and go day and night. The English, with their wives and children, and all English goods, come here by ship."

Safihan: "What about English women? They are quite another kind of women; how can we compare ourselves with them? They go about out of doors. I hear that they send their tiny children off to England and don't grieve over it; I don't know what kind of mothers they are, or how their hearts find patience. And then, women who go about out of doors and have hearts of iron: what is one sea to them? Even flying through the air wouldn't be hard for them."

Asghari Khanam: "As for what you say about going about out of doors, it is not the custom in their country to keep *purdah* (the seclusion of women). In the days of the Mutiny we fled to a village, and there too purdah was not the custom; everyone's wives and daughters went out of doors. But I stayed there four months, and among those women who went about out of doors I saw such modesty and reserve that I pray God may grant it to all of us who keep purdah! And how did you conclude, from their sending their children to England, that they have no love for their children? Their love, certainly, goes with good sense; it is not a crazy love like that of mothers here, who keep their children from studying and stop them acquiring skills. It is love in name, but in truth they are sowing thorns in their children's path; they bring their children up badly, and give love itself a bad name."

At this point everyone fell silent, and Fazilat began her story again: "And that king had no son, only one daughter. The king, knowing that after him this girl would inherit the kingdom, had her well taught to read and write, and taught her thoroughly all the laws and rules of governing a country, and in his own lifetime handed over the business of the kingdom to her."

Fazilat had got as far as this when Asghari Khanam said, "My dear, you are rattling off your story, and my heart is full of a thousand things to ask; but what can I do? The day is nearly over, and I must go to Aliya's house; and it is not proper to visit the sick at someone's house after dusk. I can't stay any longer now; you girls tell stories among yourselves." And she said to Safihan, "Well, my dear, God be with you! I'm going; if you like, stay on, or come again tomorrow; this is what happens here every day."

So Asghari Khanam set off for Aliya's house, and Safihan was so delighted that she stayed sitting among the girls till a watch into the night. After Asghari Khanam left, Mahmuda and Husn-ara drew many delightful points out of the story as it went along.

From this account the arrangements of Asghari's school and her way of teaching and instructing are clear enough. Asghari certainly loved Husn-ara very much, and her sister-in-law Mahmuda even more. She taught Husn-ara so well that in two years she began reading Persian and could write letters in Urdu. Neither Husn-ara's bad temper remained nor her peevishness; she became a very gentle, educated, accomplished and lovable daughter. Through Asghari, God restored Jamal-ara's home, which had lain desolate for years; but that whole story will be told in another book. In short, the Hakim's entire household, young and old, revered Asghari as if they would drink the water her feet were washed in. Sultana Begum tried a hundred thousand devices to make Asghari accept something, but that servant of God would take nothing. When Husn-ara's wedding was being arranged, the elder Hakim Sahib brought pressure through Maulvi Muhammad Fazil and gave Asghari a pair of jewelled bracelets worth a thousand rupees, saying, "Listen: you are like my granddaughters to me; I am not giving you this as a teacher's fee, but as to my own child." The Maulvi Sahib, for his part, persuaded her, and Asghari accepted the bracelets.
