# Weddings and Their Expenses

The two of them took their leave. Asghari said to her mother-in-law, "Mother, are you giving any thought to Mahmuda?"

Her mother-in-law said, "What thought can I give? Let a proposal come from somewhere first. I have one place in mind: I'll marry Mahmuda to Muhammad Salih."

Asghari said, "Muhammad Salih and Mahmuda, what a pair! Brother Muhammad Salih can't be much younger than my brother-in-law."

Muhammad Kamil's mother said, "Yes, Aqil is six months older than Muhammad Salih; they were both born in the same year."

Asghari said, "Well, that's hardly a small difference!"

Muhammad Kamil's mother said, "And there has been no approach from anywhere else."

Asghari said, "I have thought of something; if you like it, I'll set it going."

Muhammad Kamil's mother asked, "What is it?"

Asghari said, "With Hakim Fathullah Khan's son."

Muhammad Kamil's mother said, "Really, daughter, living in a hut and dreaming of palaces! Where is the Hakim's house? Today they have such wealth that there is no one to equal them in the city; and where are we poor folk, who haven't even a decent hut to live in? Would a proposal from here mean anything to them? We'd only be put to shame for asking."

Asghari said, "If they are wealthy, it is for themselves; are we, God forbid, dependent on them? If they delight in their *pulao* and sweet rice, we are content with our lentils and porridge. In birth we are not beneath them; and the accomplishments that our Mahmuda has, God bless her, even their elders can never have had."

Muhammad Kamil's mother said, "My dear, accomplishments stand with folded hands before wealth. Let me first have a golden canopied bed made, and then go and talk to them. Don't think of it at all, ever. Why, what was wrong with Ulwi Khan? They sent a proposal and then took it back. My dear, the poor find their place among the poor."

Asghari said, "Beauty is the one wealth that is worth a thousand fortunes; and, touch wood, let them search the family for a girl better looking than our Mahmuda."

Muhammad Kamil's mother said, "My dear, how childishly you talk! Even beauty only counts when there is equality of station; and anyway, is it a thing to say aloud, that our girl is beautiful? And I don't understand what this beauty is supposed to be; I have seen great beauties who weren't valued as much as a shoe, and plain women who sit there treasured like rubies."

Asghari said, "Is beauty such a thing that one would not be charmed by it? But very often people who have good looks are bad in character and vile in temper; they are proud of their looks, and so they never get their way anywhere, and their temper brings down the value of their beauty. It is like a horse with a clean coat, good legs, free of bad whorls in the hair, sound in every joint, but vicious in its paces and stubborn; it kicks out at people, rears when ridden, and throws its rider; what is anyone to do with the looks of such a wretched horse? But if, with beauty of form, it is also well trained, sure-footed and gentle, it is a rare thing indeed: like our Mahmuda, looks and character both, God bless her, each a match for the other."

Muhammad Kamil's mother said, "After all, there must be something to give as well. Only a little while ago a girl from your school was reading:

'Either make no friends with elephant-drivers,
Or raise up a door to the height of an elephant.'

That is: either have nothing to do with elephant-drivers, or, if you do, build the door of your house high enough for an elephant to come and go. [TN: Persian, from Sa'di: ya makun ba filbanan dosti / ya dare afraz bar bala-e pil.] Where have poor folk like us anything to give or take fit for their rank? Why make ourselves a laughing-stock for nothing? And suppose the match were made, and the girl were looked down on there: then it would be 'loss of one's capital and the neighbours' gloating'." [TN: a Persian proverb, nuqsan-e maya o shamatat-e hamsaya.]

Asghari said, "Honour and dishonour do not depend on the dowry. Harmony between husband and wife is a different thing altogether. Did Jamal-ara take a small dowry with her? Yet she was not allowed to stay a single day at her in-laws'. Why go so far? My sister was given the same as I was; so why is there a quarrel every day? It is all a matter of one's own temper and one's own good sense."

Muhammad Kamil's mother said, "I grant that the love and sincerity between husband and wife does not depend on the dowry; but when do relations and clansmen ever hold their tongues without being told? And what if the boy pays no attention? The mother-in-law and sisters-in-law themselves, finding an opening, may drop a word now and then, and after all it hurts. In the first place, the girl's family must always bow their heads; on top of that, to give only a bare, necessary dowry is a disaster. No, my dear, I can't see this creeper climbing the trellis."

Asghari said, "What have we to do with relations? Relations don't sit beside one every day. Yes, the taunts of a mother-in-law and sisters-in-law, day and night, would certainly be a terrible thing to face; but Husn-ara and Jamal-ara, let alone taunting and reproaching her, will revere Mahmuda as if they would drink the water her feet were washed in. What would be the sense of it? Will they put potsherds over their eyes the moment the wedding is over? You can see for yourself the love Husn-ara has for Mahmuda; as for Jamal-ara, God knows her heart, but outwardly, whenever she meets her, she can't do enough for her. And after all, I am still alive; if they treat Mahmuda badly, how will they face me? And the one thing worth a hundred, I know, is that mothers-in-law and sisters-in-law watch which way the wind is blowing; if they see the boy delighted with her, nobody will dare so much as look at Mahmuda crossly."

Muhammad Kamil's mother said, "After all, what do you want? Shall I have the marriage performed over a cup of sherbet?"

Asghari said, "That is not what I mean. And when people are so poor that they cannot afford even sherbet, do they not marry off their sons and daughters? Giving presents is a custom of the whole wide world: stretch your feet as far as your sheet will reach. What one can manage within one's means, one gives; what one cannot, one does not. To ruin one's household for the sake of show is not sensible either. There is a girl called Salma studying in my school; after the Mutiny her father was given a reward of ten thousand rupees by the government for saving the life of some English lady. Ten thousand rupees was enough for them to live respectably all their lives. Then came the marriages of a son and a daughter; out of vanity he spent the whole ten thousand the government had given him, and borrowed a thousand or fifteen hundred more on top of it. At the time there were loud cries of 'Bravo!' on every side; now the household is so straitened that they hardly know where their next meal is coming from. I was invited to the wedding too; when I saw the display, I was struck dumb. In fact, though perhaps Salma's mother took it amiss in her heart, I did say to her, 'My dear, giving to a son or a daughter is a joy to the eyes and a comfort to the heart; where did the ghee go? Into the *khichri* [TN: a proverb meaning that money spent on one's own children is not wasted]; but still, one must also take care of one's own cooking pot.' I said that much, but afterwards I was sorry, for Salma's sister must have thought, 'What business is it of the teacher's? She has nothing to take and nothing to give, and she is fretting for nothing.'"

Muhammad Kamil's mother said, "Yes, that's true; but we have to live in this wretched world; what can we do? Where can we go? Whether we have it or not, we must do it. If we don't do as the world does, who wants to be the odd one out? Who wants to be pointed at? I heard in Maulvi Ishaq Sahib's sermon that in olden times the people of Arabia used to kill their daughters as soon as they were born."

Asghari said, "Mother, why go so far? In our own country the Rajputs used to commit the same crime; now it has stopped because the English forbid it; and even so we have heard rumours several times of murders done in secret."

Muhammad Kamil's mother said, "What can good sense do? Pride will not accept it."

Asghari said, "What is there in poverty to be proud about? There are more poor people in the world than rich; if being poor is a matter of pride, then there are a great many people without pride in the world. Wealth and poverty are each one's own lot; how can everyone be alike?"

Muhammad Kamil's mother said, "Oh, if only the English government would forbid spending so much on weddings, the trouble would be at an end."

Asghari: "From the newspapers it seems that the English are going to make some arrangement; all the notables of our city were summoned too; and I hear that a limit has been set on expenses and the size of the dower fixed. But these are things for us ourselves to do: that we all agree together and stop every useless expense."

Muhammad Kamil's mother: "As for what you say about useless expenses, for those whom God has given, nothing is useless; yes, for one who hasn't a cowrie to his name, everything is useless."

Asghari: "Don't say that. At weddings, the necessary expenses are few; it is on useless things that a great deal of money goes. In our family there are no dancing girls, no shows, no bands, no fireworks, no kettledrums; but in the families that have them, hundreds and thousands of rupees are washed away on those alone."

Muhammad Kamil's mother: "Let those who have dancing and shows look to it; but what useless expense is there in our family?"

Asghari: "Why not? The betrothal, the festival gifts, the *sachaq*, the *mehndi* (henna ceremony), the procession, the return visit, the fourth-day ceremony, the bride's journeys, very heavy suits of clothes, jewelled ornaments: all of them are useless."

Muhammad Kamil's mother: "Then why don't you say straight out that marriage itself is useless from start to finish?"

Asghari began to laugh, and said, "Marriage is not useless, but its trappings are certainly empty humbug."

Muhammad Kamil's mother: "Well, never mind the customs; you call even clothes and jewellery useless."

Asghari: "Plain clothes and plain jewellery are useful things; but heavy suits of clothes, judge for yourself, what use are they? My own suits lie rotting; to wear them about the house makes one's heart ache; I have worn them now and then at a wedding, or they come out for a little while at Eid; the other twelve months they lie tied up in a bundle. Airing them in the sun every other day is a needless headache; and if you try to sell them, you don't get their value; they don't fetch even the price of the materials. And the same is true of jewelled ornaments. Have you heard about the wedding of Maulvi Kifayatullah's daughter? Weddings like that are what I like."

Muhammad Kamil's mother: "Which Maulvi Kifayatullah?"

Asghari: "The inspector of girls' schools."

Muhammad Kamil's mother: "I don't think he belongs to this city."

Asghari: "No, he comes from the Agra side; he has brought his wife and children to live with him. He betrothed his daughter in this city. His wife wanted to go to their own city and hold the wedding there, with the procession going from here; the Maulvi Sahib talked her round and persuaded her. One day he sent for two or four friends; when the guests reached the house, they heard that it was his daughter's marriage. After a little while the bridegroom's father arrived with the boy; the marriage was solemnised according to the law of Muhammad; God is God, all is well; and of presents and dowry, there was none at all. After the marriage the Maulvi Sahib brought five hundred rupees in cash, set them before his daughter and son-in-law, and said, 'That is all, my dears; this much of my earnings was your portion. If I had wished, I could have spent it on entertaining guests and had one or two heavy suits made, as the world does; but when I thought about it, it seemed best to give you the money in cash; now use it as you like.'"

Hearing this, Muhammad Kamil's mother said, "Yes, away from home! The Maulvi Sahib could do as he liked; who was there to say anything?"

Asghari: "Why? There was his own wife at home to say something! And why should it depend on being away from home? It takes courage; a man who means to do it will do it in his own city too. Let the talkers chatter; mind your own business."

Muhammad Kamil's mother: "Have you planned the same sort of dull, gloomy marriage for Mahmuda?"

Asghari: "Certainly, I don't care in the least what people say. If I had my way, Mahmuda's marriage would match that of Kifayatullah's daughter. He did invite two or four guests, and in my view even that is unnecessary."

Muhammad Kamil's mother: "No, my dear, for God's sake don't do anything so dreadful! In my old age this is the only child I have left to marry off; am I to come back from the grave to hold someone's wedding and procession?"

Asghari: "No, I don't intend that either; but I have certainly made up my mind to this: not a pice is to be borrowed, and no property is to be mortgaged. Whatever has been put by in her name, and whatever her fate provides at the time, will be enough."

Muhammad Kamil's mother: "Glory be to God! If only it could be so! But that depends on the other side agreeing too."

Asghari said, "And what if they do agree?"

Muhammad Kamil's mother said, "Is their agreeing a joke? An only son, the child of many prayers; who knows what hopes they have in their hearts? They will look for a house of equal standing before they talk, and they will have every wish fulfilled."

Asghari said, "Ever since I came back from Sialkot I have been working on this; on that side everything has been settled. Just now Jamal-ara and Husn-ara came running here; the younger Hakim Sahib has agreed too. Shah Zamani Begum tried many, many schemes for her own daughter; by God's grace none of them succeeded. Now we must not delay; the day after tomorrow is a good day; let the sweets come from them and the match be made firm; then we'll see about the wedding."

Muhammad Aqil's mother was astonished to hear this, and said, "It is a very good match, far beyond our deserts; but it will be hard for us to provide what befits them."

Asghari said, "God is the provider of means; when Mahmuda's fate has been joined to so high a house, God will by His power provide something when the time comes."

Muhammad Kamil's mother said, "Let your father-in-law come; I'll ask him about the sweets."

Shortly afterwards the Maulvi Sahib came, and was delighted to hear of the betrothal, and said, "Let the sweets come the day after tomorrow without hesitation." Asghari sent word to Husn-ara. On the appointed day five maunds of sweets and a hundred rupees arrived; a maund and a quarter of sweets and a hundred and twenty-five rupees went back. On every side there were congratulations and good wishes.
