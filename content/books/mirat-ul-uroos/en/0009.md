# A Letter to Her Brother

So Muhammad Kamil's mother got into the *doli* and set off for the Khanam ka Bazar, and Mahmuda went and told the whole story to the Tamizdar Bahu.

Asghari could think of nothing else to do; she at once wrote this letter to her elder brother, Khairandesh Khan:

"I have not written to you about myself for a long time, because the letters I send to our honoured father must surely pass before your eyes too. Now a particular matter has come up which I think it right to put before you yourself. It is this. Since I came to my husband's home I have had no trouble of any kind; and, thanks to your prayers, the things my elder sister used to complain of do not happen to me. Everyone loves me, and I am happy. Only from Mama Azmat do I suffer such vexation as I would not have from a bad-tempered mother-in-law or a sharp-tongued sister-in-law. This woman is an old servant of the house, and all the work inside and out is in her hands. She has plundered the house and ruined it. Now the debts have grown so large that I see no way of paying them. There is no proper management of any kind in the house. For a few days I took a hand in the ordinary business of the household, and there was embezzlement in everything and fraud in every matter. Because I checked her, the maid has become my enemy, and since that day she stirs up fresh trouble every day. So far, it is true, nothing really bad has come of it, but I find it very hard to bear this maid staying here; yet it is also very hard to get rid of her. All the debts in the bazaar were made through her; if she so much as heard the word dismissal, she would go and stir up the creditors. Then there is no account and no record of the debts; all the borrowing and lending is done by guesswork. What I want is for everyone's account to be gone through and put down in writing, for each creditor to be paid by suitable instalments, for the habit of borrowing to be stopped for the future, and for the maid to be dismissed. I am sure you will come with our father in Ramzan. Please be so kind as to go by way of Lahore and bring Father-in-law back with you, however you can manage it, for two weeks at least. In front of you all this whole matter will be settled properly. I am writing this letter in great anxiety. Some moneylender is about to sue; the maid has advised that my bangles should be pawned; Mother-in-law has gone to her sister just now to arrange for money. That is all."

While Asghari wrote to her brother on the one hand, on the other she sent word to her aunt: "I am alone; please send my cousin Tamasha Khanam to me for two days. I hear she is staying with you." So that very evening Bi Tamasha Khanam arrived. As soon as she got out of the *doli* she cried, "Goodness, Bi Asghari, how could anyone be so unkind! I sent to ask you for my uncle's letter, and you didn't let me have it."

Asghari said, "Oh, who came to ask for it?"

Tamasha Khanam said, "Look, here is Mama Azmat. Tell me, Bi, when you came to our house this Friday, did I tell you or not?"

Azmat said, "Yes, Bi, she did tell me; wretch that I am, I can't remember things. With the work of the house I forgot before I got here."

Asghari said softly, "Yes, you remember well enough how to plunder and make mischief." And she said to Tamasha Khanam, "The letter is here, and a new book has come as well, with very amusing things in it; take that with you too."

Asghari told Tamasha Khanam everything about the maid, down to the last detail. Tamasha Khanam had a very fiery temper; she got up at once, shoe in hand, and started off to beat the maid.

Asghari caught her by the hand and sat her down again, and said, "For God's sake, sister, don't do such a terrible thing. Don't be hasty now, or everything will be spoilt."

Tamasha Khanam said, "You lose your dignity with all this hesitating. My dear, if I were in your place, I swear to God I'd straighten the wretch out with my shoe so that she'd remember it all her life."

Asghari said, "Look, God willing, the wrath of God will fall on this traitor to the salt; it is only a matter of days."

After this Tamasha Khanam asked, "Why has your mother-in-law gone to her sister's?"

Asghari said, "Poor thing, she too is being driven from door to door by this wretched maid. There is a moneylender to whom something is owed; the maid came and told her today that he is going to sue. She has gone to see about the money for him."

Tamasha Khanam asked, "Which moneylender is going to sue?"

Asghari said, "I don't know his name."

Tamasha Khanam asked the maid, "Azmat, which moneylender is it?"

Azmat said, "Madam, Hazari Mal."

Tamasha Khanam said, "You mean the Hazari Mal whose shop is in the Jauhari Bazar?"

Azmat said, "Yes, madam, yes, that same Hazari Mal."

Tamasha Khanam said, "My husband's family deals with him too. Why, how does the wretch dare to sue? When I go home from here I'll tell your cousin [TN: Tamasha Khanam's husband]; just see how he sorts him out."

Tamasha Khanam stayed two days with Asghari. On the third day she took her leave, and as she was going she said, "Asghari dear, I make you swear on my head: when your father-in-law comes and all this business comes up for trial, be sure to send for me, and hand Azmat over to me."

Meanwhile, over there, her sister had kept Muhammad Kamil's mother at her house: "Oh, sister, you come so seldom; do stay a week at least." But every day a servant came over to ask after the Tamizdar Bahu. Mama Azmat, without being provoked, played yet another trick. In those days the Lat Sahib [TN: the Lieutenant-Governor] was expected; the authorities had given strict orders for the city to be cleaned, and notices were posted in every quarter and every lane: "Everyone is to clean his own lane and street, have his doors whitewashed and keep his drains clean; if rubbish is found lying anywhere, the house will be sold by auction." A notice to this effect was posted on the gate of this quarter too. Mama Azmat went at night, tore down the notice from the gate of the quarter, and quietly stuck it on their own door. Then, before daylight, she ran off to the Khanam ka Bazar to tell Muhammad Kamil's mother. The doors of the house were not yet open when she called out.

Muhammad Kamil's mother recognised her voice and said, "Oh, quick, open the doors! Why has Azmat come running at this unearthly hour?" When Azmat came in, she asked, "Mama, is everything all right?"

Azmat said, "Madam, there's an *ishtar* or *shitar* (what do you call it?) stuck up on the house. It looks as though Hazari Mal has sued."

Muhammad Kamil's mother said to her sister, "Well, my dear, I'm going. I'll go and send for Hazari Mal and reason with him; God put pity in his heart!"

Her sister said, "Sister, I am very ashamed that I couldn't arrange the money for you; but I have my necklace; take it with you. If pawning it will serve, well and good; otherwise sell it."

Muhammad Kamil's mother said, "Very well, I'll take the necklace; but the debt has grown very large. What use will one necklace be?"

Her sister said, "After all, my husband has said that he will borrow from another moneylender. You go, in God's name; when he comes, I'll send him after you."

So Muhammad Kamil's mother reached home. When she got down at the door she saw the notice stuck there. She came in and sat down in silent distress. Hearing that her mother-in-law had come, Asghari came down from the roof and greeted her. Seeing her mother-in-law so sad, she asked, "Mother dear, your face is very sad today."

Her mother-in-law said, "Yes, the moneylender has sued. There is no way of getting the money from anywhere. Amir Begum couldn't help either, and the notice has been put up on the house. Heaven knows what will happen."

Asghari said, "Please don't worry about it at all. If Hazari Mal has sued, there's no harm done. He does business with Tamasha Khanam's husband's family; Tamasha Khanam has promised me she will make Hazari Mal see reason, and if he won't listen, some way will be found to pay his money. What good does it do to grieve?"

Her mother-in-law said, "If Kamil were here, I'd send him to Hazari Mal."

Asghari said, "Of course, that is for you to decide; but to my mind it is not at all wise to be afraid of a moneylender. Otherwise he will grow bold in future and keep threatening us with suits. The best thing is that no hint should come from this side, and that some pressure should be brought on him from outside, so that he gives up pressing the suit."

Muhammad Kamil's mother said, "Tamasha Khanam is only a girl; what does she know of courts and officials? I'm afraid that relying on her the business will go wrong and slip out of our hands."

Asghari said, "Tamasha Khanam is certainly a girl, but I have made the matter quite sure, and I am easy about it."

They were still talking when Miyan Muslim called at the door. Asghari said, "Look, Muslim has come; he must have brought some news about this business." Asghari signed to Mahmuda, and Mahmuda went into the side room. They called Muslim in and asked, "Muslim, what news have you brought?"

Muslim said, "My sister sends you her greetings and asks how you are, and says she sent for Hazari Mal and frightened and threatened him thoroughly, and he has promised that there will be no suit."

Hearing this, Muhammad Kamil's mother was somewhat comforted. But Asghari was puzzled: "Tamasha Khanam has sent me word of this, yet Hazari Mal has gone and sued! What can it mean? And the notice is strange too; I was sitting at home and knew nothing about it. If it were an official notice, some messenger or constable would have called out and announced it."

When Muslim had gone, Asghari said to Mahmuda, "Go and quietly tear down the paper that is stuck on the door and bring it here."

Mahmuda tore down the paper and brought it. When Asghari read it, it was an order about cleaning; there was no mention of any suit. She understood that this too was a trick of this Azmat's. She did not reveal this to her mother-in-law, but she reassured her thoroughly: "You may rest easy; there is no fear of a suit."

Her mother-in-law said, "Since you say so, my mind is at rest about the suit; but Shab-e-Barat [TN: the festival on the night of the fifteenth of Sha'ban] and Ramzan are coming upon us, and both festivals mean nothing but expense. And letters from Lahore have stopped coming. The worry of it is drying up my very blood."

Asghari said, "There are many days yet before Ramzan; God is the Causer of causes, and by then some means will appear from the unseen. It's true there are only four days left before Shab-e-Barat, but Shab-e-Barat is not the kind of festival that needs a great deal of money."

Her mother-in-law said, "In my house, year after year, twenty rupees are spent at Shab-e-Barat. Ask Azmat here; she is the one who spends it."

Asghari said, "Spending is easy enough; but there is spending for a need and spending without need. At Shab-e-Barat there is no need that calls for so much money."

Her mother-in-law said, "My dear, the *fatiha* for the saints and the Prophet and our great elders comes first [TN: food is consecrated by reciting the opening chapter of the Quran and then shared out]; then it is necessary to send portions to people's houses. And then, though it seems a little thing, if there are five rupees, all being well, there will be fireworks and crackers for your husband and Bi Mahmuda. What if Muhammad Kamil is married? God keep him, he still has childish ways; until he has had a hundred fireworks and twenty packets of crackers, he'll worry the life out of me. And Mahmuda will cry herself into a state too."

Asghari said, "Five *sers* of sweets will be plenty for the *fatiha*. As for sending portions, what comes in from one side goes out on the other. And Mahmuda won't insist on crackers now; I'll talk to her. In short, I'll arrange for Shab-e-Barat as best I can. I take it on myself; please don't think of borrowing for it."

That was what she said to her mother-in-law; but Asghari was wondering how to keep her husband from the fireworks and crackers. In the end she made her husband understand it so cleverly that the thing was said, yet her husband was not offended.

In front of Muhammad Kamil, she teasingly asked Mahmuda, "Well, my dear, what plans have you made for Shab-e-Barat?"

Mahmuda said, "When my brother brings fireworks and crackers, he'll give me some too."

Before Muhammad Kamil could say anything, Asghari said, "Why should your brother bring such silly things for you? Mahmuda, what fun is there in fireworks and crackers?"

Mahmuda said, "Sister-in-law, when the fireworks and crackers go off, how lovely it is!"

Asghari said, "Hundreds of fireworks will go off in the neighbourhood; you can watch them too from the roof."

Mahmuda said, "What! And not let off any ourselves?"

Asghari said, "Aren't you afraid?"

Mahmuda said, "Do I let them off with my own hands?"

Asghari said, "Then the neighbourhood's fireworks are just like watching your own go off. And listen, Mahmuda: it's a very bad game; there is a danger of being burnt. Once in our neighbourhood a firework burst in a boy's hand, and both his eyes were put out and ruined. Even if you want to watch it, watch from a distance. And Mahmuda, you see how Mother is; is she sad or not?"

Mahmuda said, "Yes, she is sad."

Asghari asked, "Have you ever thought why she is sad?"

Mahmuda said, "I don't know."

Asghari said, "Well! And yet you say you love Mother very much?"

Mahmuda asked, "Dear sister-in-law, why is Mother sad?"

Asghari said, "Money is short; the moneylender won't lend; she is worrying where she will get the money from if Mahmuda insists on having fireworks."

Mahmuda said, "Then I won't ask for fireworks."

Asghari said, "Well done, well done, you are a very dear sister." And she hugged Mahmuda and kissed her.

Muhammad Kamil sat quietly listening to all this. Since it was sensible talk, his heart accepted it, and he went straight downstairs to his mother and said, "Mother, I hear you are sitting worrying about Shab-e-Barat. Well, don't worry about me; I don't need fireworks and crackers. And Mahmuda says she won't ask for any either." So one item of expense was cut in this way.

For the *fatiha*, a good quantity of sweets was made for two rupees. Asghari saw to the sending of portions herself. When a portion came in from outside, she did not let it stay in the house; the servant who had brought it was sent out again with it and told, "Take this portion to such-and-such a place." Everyone who was to be given something received it by name, and a very pleasant Shab-e-Barat was had for two rupees.

Azmat was furious to see this arrangement, because she lost a large sum by it. She used to take all that came in from outside, and keep back half of whatever went out from the house; and the *halwa* of Shab-e-Barat, which she used to dry and store, she would eat by the handful for months like *panjiri*. [TN: a dry sweet of fried flour, sugar and nuts.]
