# A Post in Sialkot

But we were beginning to write another book within this one. While Asghari was busy with her school, Muhammad Kamil was growing restless at being without employment. One day he said to Asghari, "I'm very restless now. If you agree, I'll go to the Tahsildar Sahib in the hills and look for employment through him."

Asghari thought a little and said, "Employment is certainly very necessary, for you can see how straitened we are at home. Father has grown old now; it is right that he should sit at home and that you should earn and look after him. Besides, Mahmuda is growing up; I am thinking about her betrothal, and I intend that she should be married into a very high family. I am working on it, and, God willing, her match will be settled this very year; but for that a great deal of provision will be needed, and at present there is nothing of any kind ready. Your elder brother, in the first place, lives separately, and then on such a small salary he cannot even manage for himself; how could he give to anyone else? So there is no other way except for you to take employment. But I do not advise going to the hills. Father will certainly try for you, and very likely you would soon find a good post too; but taking employment by leaning on someone's support is not quite right. Never mind if it is small, so long as it comes by the strength of your own arm. Although Father is no stranger, and by relationship his hand is above yours, so that to ask of him, let alone to take, is no disgrace, still, may God never put anyone under obligation to another! It keeps one's eyes lowered for ever. Even if he never threw it in your face, there are, God keep them, a hundred people in the family; if they don't say it to your face, they will certainly say behind your back, 'Look, he got his post on his father-in-law's back.'"

Muhammad Kamil said, "Then what should I do? Go to Lahore?"

Asghari said, "What is there in Lahore? The nobleman's household is itself ruined. I don't know how, out of regard for old times, he still gives even Father fifty rupees; where is there room for a new man in his household?"

Muhammad Kamil said, "There are plenty of other households."

Asghari said, "Since the English rule began, all the noblemen have been ruined in the same way. They all keep up their former name and show, and so five or ten familiar faces still cling to them; and even then, what does it amount to? Salaries go unpaid for years."

Muhammad Kamil said, "Then what is the remedy?"

Asghari said, "Look for employment under the English."

Muhammad Kamil said, "But employment under the English can't be had without influence and recommendation; thousands and hundreds of thousands of men far better than I am go about wretchedly and nobody asks after them."

Asghari said, "Yes, that's true; but when a man makes up his mind to something, he should trust in God and not let the thought of despair enter his head. Granted that thousands go about searching for employment in vain, those who are employed are men just like you; and the one thing worth a hundred is that employment comes by fate. The most able are left looking on, and if God means to give, then, without influence or merit, He gives through the very roof; people send for a man from his house and employ him."

Muhammad Kamil said, "So what you mean is that I should sit at home?"

Asghari said, "That is not what I mean at all. One should certainly make every effort one can."

Muhammad Kamil said, "That's just the difficulty: what effort should I make?"

Asghari said, "Get to know people who are in employment and make friends with them; through them you will keep hearing of posts, and through them you will reach some official."

Muhammad Kamil did just this: he began to make the acquaintance of men in employment, until he was going in and out among people such as the head clerk and the *tahsildar*. Through his coming and going every day, they all learned that he too was looking for a post, until Banda Ali Beg, who was a statement-writer in the court, said to Muhammad Kamil, "Sir, if you are looking for employment, come along to the court with me. Be a candidate for a while, learn the work of the office, and show your face to the officials; in this way, sooner or later, something will turn up."

Muhammad Kamil began going to the court, and worked alongside Banda Ali Beg, until he was even getting the magistrate's signature on papers. The officials began to know him. In the meantime he was given two or four acting posts for minor officials as well: when some clerk needed leave, he would leave Muhammad Kamil as his substitute at a half or a third of his pay. At last it happened that a diarist on ten rupees went on three months' leave; after three months he sent in his resignation, and Maulvi Muhammad Kamil Sahib was confirmed in his place.

Sometimes, when the subject of his employment came up with Asghari, Muhammad Kamil would say contemptuously, "What a wretched job it is! Sweat all day, and ten rupees; nothing to be made on the side, and no hope of promotion in future! I'm going to give it up."

Asghari always reproved such thoughts: "You are being grossly ungrateful. Have you forgotten the days when you could not even get taken on as a candidate? Now that you are in work, you don't value it! Ten rupees while living at home: is that so little? Look at your elder brother, who worked for years for the merchant at ten rupees. And when you are so out of heart with your employment, what sort of work can you be doing? In the end the job will drop away by itself. And this is how little grows into much: my father was once a copyist on eight rupees a month, and now by God's grace he is a *tahsildar*; and if God wills, he will rise still higher. Never, even by mistake, set your eyes on income from the side; there is never any blessing in ill-gotten money. No one can get more than is fated; then why should a man let his integrity waver? If more is coming to you, God can give it honestly as well."

In short, Asghari was always advising Muhammad Kamil, until the official under whom Muhammad Kamil was employed was transferred to Sialkot. This official was very kind to Muhammad Kamil. The news was known in the court during the day, and when Muhammad Kamil came home in the evening he was very downcast. Asghari asked, "Is everything all right? Why are you so gloomy today?"

Muhammad Kamil said, "What can I tell you? James Sahib has been transferred to Sialkot; he was the one person who was kind to me; now there's no pleasure in staying at the court."

Asghari was silent for a long time; then she said, "Certainly James Sahib's transfer is a pity, but not as much as you feel it is. Whoever comes in his place, God will put kindness in his heart too. One man should not put his trust in another."

Then Asghari asked, "When will James Sahib go?"

Muhammad Kamil said, "He'll take the mail coach tomorrow evening."

Asghari said, "Haven't you been to his bungalow?"

Muhammad Kamil said, "What's the use of going now?"

Asghari said, "Why, this is just the time to call on him; if nothing else, he will leave you some letter or certificate."

Muhammad Kamil said, "Very well, I'll go in the morning."

Very early, Muhammad Kamil put on his clothes and went to James Sahib's bungalow. James Sahib said, "Muhammad Kamil, we go to Sialkot now, and we was very pleased with you; now if you want, come with us to Sialkot; we give you a job there; if not, we give fifteen rupees out of our own pocket." [TN: James Sahib speaks the broken Hindustani of Europeans.]

Muhammad Kamil thought and said, "I shall come again and give Your Honour an answer; let me ask my mother."

So Muhammad Kamil came back home and told them, "James Sahib wants to take me with him." The moment his mother heard it she raised an outcry; Asghari too was struck silent. At last Muhammad Kamil asked, "Good people, tell me what answer I should go and give."

Muhammad Kamil's mother said, "What answer is there to give? Is he going to sit waiting for you now, or send soldiers to fetch you?"

Muhammad Kamil said, "No, Mother, I promised him; he will say to himself, 'How selfish Hindustanis are! He told us a lie when we were leaving.'"

Muhammad Kamil's mother said, "Very well, go and tell him, 'Sir, I cannot go.'"

Muhammad Kamil asked Asghari, "Well, madam, what do you advise?"

Asghari said, "Advice is one thing and the heart's wish is another. My heart's wish was that you should stay here; the management of the house depends on you alone; after all, there must be some man in the house. But if you ask my advice, it is right to go; when an official takes you with him of his own accord, without being asked, he is sure to treat you very well once he reaches his post."

Muhammad Kamil said, "A journey of two or three hundred *kos* for the sake of five rupees! My heart isn't set on going. As the saying goes, 'Better half a loaf at home than a whole one abroad.'"

Asghari said, "As for that, you are free to choose; but such a chance comes by fate, and it won't come your way again. And who does not travel? Your father, my father: look, these men have spent their lives travelling. And for the present you have heard of five; later you will see how many fives there are. But if you don't go, then don't complain about the ten rupees any more."

Muhammad Kamil said, "So am I to resign my post here before I go? And suppose nothing comes of it there; then I've lost both this and that."

Asghari said, "In the first place, to suppose that nothing will come of it there is against all reason. James Sahib is so high an official; if he wants to give you work and nothing comes of it, that I cannot understand. And then, why resign? Take a month or two's leave."

Muhammad Kamil said, "Oh yes, leave is just lying there granted!"

Asghari said, "What is there in getting it granted? Ask the same James Sahib, and he will write a note."

So Asghari, by pushing him hard, got Muhammad Kamil to agree to go. She gave him twenty-five rupees in cash of her own, had six new suits of clothes made for him, and sent Rafiq, Diyanat's son, with him. Maulvi Muhammad Kamil graced Sialkot with his presence.

Meanwhile Asghari wrote all this to Maulvi Muhammad Fazil Sahib in a letter, and added: "On his way to Sialkot, James Sahib will certainly pass through Lahore; if you could manage to meet him there and get the nobleman to put in a word for him, it would be very useful."

The Maulvi Sahib sought out James Sahib; and as the nobleman also owned some villages in the Sialkot district, the Maulvi Sahib invited the Sahib on the nobleman's behalf and lodged him in the nobleman's garden house. After dinner, as the Sahib and the nobleman sat talking together, the Maulvi Sahib said to James Sahib, "The people of Delhi are deeply grieved at your departure; although you were an official in Delhi for only two years, the people there were very pleased with your justice and your kindness to respectable families. A son of your humble servant also served in your court; I kept hearing all the news from his letters."

The Sahib asked, "What, was a son of yours in my court too?"

The Maulvi Sahib said, "Muhammad Kamil."

The Sahib said, "Why, he is coming with me; he is your son?"

The Maulvi Sahib said, "He is your servant." On this occasion the nobleman said to the Sahib, "The Maulvi Sahib is an old servant of our state, and his welfare is always close to our heart in every way; but as you know, there is no longer any room. So if you will be the patron of his son, we shall be obliged to you."

James Sahib had already been well disposed towards Muhammad Kamil, and the occasion arose at such a fitting moment that James Sahib took a great interest in him. First, he was young; second, of good family; third, recommended by the nobleman; fourth, the Sahib's own companion on the road; fifth, capable: Muhammad Kamil had all these claims. On the very first day he held court, the Sahib made Muhammad Kamil deputy head clerk on twenty-five rupees, and wrote to Maulvi Muhammad Fazil Sahib: "For the present we have given your son a post at twenty-five, and we shall promote him soon; please inform the nobleman."

The Maulvi Sahib thanked the Sahib in a fitting manner. And that Muhammad Kamil who had once been reduced to seeking candidacy, who had acted as a substitute for minor officials, and who had been a mere diarist on ten rupees, and had come to Sialkot with James Sahib on the promise of fifteen, only because Asghari pushed him, now at a stroke became an official on twenty-five. Muhammad Kamil's mother, although she had been displeased when he left, broke into a broad smile at the sound of twenty-five. Now there was four times the blessing in the house: Asghari's management, and instead of twenty, sixty rupees a month now coming into the house; what more could be asked?

Within a single year Muhammad Kamil became head clerk. Until he became head clerk he kept himself steady: the money for expenses came regularly, and letters kept coming one after another. But after all he was a young man, living as his own master; he fell into bad company and began to go astray. His letters began to grow fewer. Asghari was very shrewd; she understood that there was something amiss. For a long time Asghari was anxious, wondering what she should do; at last nothing occurred to her except that she must go herself.
