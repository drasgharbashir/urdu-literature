# Asghari's School

The next day Shah Zamani Begum and Sultana Begum, the two sisters, brought Husn-ara to Asghari's house. Although Asghari's household had only the means of poor people, thanks to her management and good sense the begums were entertained in such a way that everything appeared without anyone leaving her seat. Two or four kinds of perfume, *chaugharha* (a four-chambered box of spices), cardamoms, glossy areca nut and tea all appeared in a moment, and delicious *gilauris* (folded betel leaves) were made ready. Both sisters said to Asghari, "Please be so kind as to teach her with all your heart." Asghari said, "In the first place, what do I know myself? But I know a few letters by the kindness of my elders; God willing, I shall spare no effort within my power in teaching them to her."

As they were leaving, Sultana Begum began to give Asghari a gold *ashrafi* (gold coin). Asghari said, "There is no need for this. How could I possibly take a teaching fee from you?"

Sultana said, "God forgive us! How could we dare offer a teaching fee? These are the sweets for her *bismillah* (the ceremony of beginning a child's lessons)."

Asghari said, "At the start, for a blessing, people hand round sweets; so what is the gold coin for? Half a *ser* or a *ser* of sweets is enough to sweeten the children's mouths."

With that she made a sign to Diyanat, who brought out a dish filled with *nuktiyan* (small sweet drops of gram flour) from the storeroom. Asghari herself said the *fatiha* (the opening prayer of the Qur'an) over them and gave some first to Husn-ara, and handed the full dish to Diyanat: "Share them out among all the children."

Sultana said, "Well, you have put me to shame."

Asghari said, "What are poor folk like us worth? But whatever is here is yours as well. My gift is simply to teach Husn-ara Begum; and may God bring the day when I can face you with honour."

So, after the usual civilities, Shah Zamani Begum and Sultana Begum went away and left Husn-ara in Asghari's charge. A separate book will be written on the method by which Asghari taught Husn-ara; if all of that were told here, this book would grow very long. [TN: Nazir Ahmad later wrote it as *Banat-un-Na'sh*, "The Daughters of the Bier", the Arabic name for the Great Bear.] What matters at this point is that as soon as Husn-ara began, the whole neighbourhood descended on Asghari; everyone you saw came bringing her daughter. But Asghari chose the girls of good family and put the rest off tactfully: "I am always going to my mother's house; teaching and learning are no use unless they are done steadily." Even so, twenty girls attended; but Asghari had sworn never to take anything from any girl; on the contrary, a rupee or two of her own was spent on the girls. Reading went on from morning till midday, then there was an hour and a half's break for the meal, after that writing, and from the last quarter of the day, sewing.

The sewing was an extensive affair, for not only plain sewing was taught but every kind of openwork embroidery, every kind of stitching, every kind of cutting out, and the making and sewing on of trimmings. At first Asghari spent ten rupees collecting the materials; but after that the work itself began to bring in a profit. Whatever the girls made, Diyanat quietly sold in the bazaar, and in this way, little by little, a large sum belonging to the school was built up. If a girl was poor, her clothes were made and her books bought out of this sum; there was a woman servant especially to give the girls water and fan them, and she was paid from the school's money.

The girls were in such a state that going to other teachers frightened the life out of them, but Asghari's pupils adored her. She would hardly have got up from sleep when the girls began arriving of their own accord, and they stayed together till a watch into the night, and could hardly be made to go. For Asghari loved them all with her whole heart, and she had a way of teaching so pleasant that the lessons came in the course of talk; it was not that a droning spinning wheel started up in the morning and never stopped till dusk. Asghari taught her pupils just as her father had taught her. So these girls were at once her pupils and her friends. Whenever one of the girls was married, a little jewellery was given her out of the school's money. If Asghari had wanted to enlarge her school, every school in the city would have been emptied. Hundreds of women begged her on behalf of their daughters, and the girls themselves came running; for in the other schools there was the day-long confinement and the harshness of the teachers: little reading, and a great deal of beating and work. In a whole day they read perhaps two letters; morning and evening the regular beating; and the moment a girl fell silent and the teacher's eye fell on her, trouble came. And as for work: the moment they arrived in the morning they swept the house; they folded the bedding of the teacher, of "Master" [TN: said in jest of the teacher's husband] and of ten or twelve "monitors" [TN: the teacher's own children], and even of the neighbours; and four or five of them together lifted the wretched heavy, cumbersome beds. Then two or four whose bad luck had come sat down with their *sipara* (a part of the Qur'an); the moment a sound came out of their mouths, the teacher began to flourish her cane. And two or four who had seen a lucky face on waking [TN: it was believed that the first face one sees in the morning decides the day's luck] were put to the household chores. One took the teacher's little boy on her hip; her hip is breaking under his weight, but the dread of a beating rides on her neck, and she walks up and down, passing the time. The cries of the girls being beaten come to her ears; her heart shrinks inside her; beside that torment this hardship seems a blessing. Another began scouring the night's dirty pots; her finger joints are calloused and her shoulders keep giving way; but her little sister is being beaten and screaming, "Mercy, teacher, I'm dying! Mercy, I'd give my life for you! Mercy, for God's sake! Mercy, for the Prophet's sake! Mercy, I'll be the monitor's slave! Oh! Oh! Oh! Oh Mother! Oh Sister!" and the sister is scrubbing the pots as fast as she can, scrape, scrape. When these tasks were done, it was time to grind the spices, knead the dough, light the fire and fry the meat. Then at midday the teacher lies asleep, and the innocent children fan her and pray in their hearts, "O God! May she sleep so that she never wakes!"

In short, in the other schools there is this misery; at Asghari's there was neither beating nor shouting. The great threat was: "Listen, my dear, you don't learn your lessons, and because of you our school gets a bad name. I'll send for your mother and tell her, 'Lady, your daughter doesn't study here; put her with some other teacher.'" No sooner was that said than the girl was terrified, and then the lesson was on the tip of her tongue. Or a girl who had not learned her lesson was told, "My dear, you haven't learned your lesson today; the other girls will sew after midday, and you will study." No sooner was that said than she quickly learned her lesson by heart.

In the school Mahmuda and Husn-ara were the two monitors. Here there was no sweeping to be done, no bedding to lift, no beds to carry, no pots to scour, no monitors to be carried about on one's hip; on the contrary, there was a woman servant to wait on the girls themselves. Love and comfort; reading, writing and sewing, three tasks: the girls learned with great eagerness.

Here a story of the school is told, from which Asghari's way of teaching will be briefly understood.

There was a woman called Safihan, and her daughter Fazilat was about ten years old. This Fazilat had a natural liking for reading and writing and for sewing. Safihan wanted Fazilat to sweep the whole house, plaster the floors, and scour the pots; Fazilat's heart was not in such work; she did it when her mother told her to, but even then half-heartedly. One day, when Safihan was displeased with Fazilat, she took her along and left her at Asghari's school, saying, "Teacher, this girl is very useless; whatever work I tell her to do, she gives me a flat refusal. Teach her such manners that she takes to the housework."

When Asghari looked at Fazilat, she found her a girl after her own heart. Fazilat, for her part, had found a teacher to her liking. She came at first light; at midday she went home for her meal, ate, and ran back to drink her water at school [TN: proverbial for great haste: "eat here, drink there"]; and once she had come back in the afternoon, she would leave only an hour and a half after nightfall.

Now and then Safihan came to the school to see how she was getting on, and several times she saw her playing dolls with the girls, and two or four times cooking in toy pots. One day, when it must have been an hour and a half after nightfall, Fazilat was late in going home, and Safihan came to fetch her. What does she see but Mahmuda telling stories, all the girls of the school sitting round her, and the teacher herself sitting among the girls listening to the stories. At that Safihan burned with anger and said, "Well done, teacher! A fine way you have ruined the girls. Every time I have come to see Fazilat, I have never once found her studying. What sort of school is this? A fine playhouse; no wonder the girls come running."

Asghari said, "My dear, if your daughter is not being taught as you wish, you are free to take her away; but don't lay an unjust charge against the school. Now let me ask you: how long did Fazilat study at Maiji's school?"

Safihan said, "I put her there at the new moon of Miranji; she studied the whole of Madar, and went on through Khwaja Muinuddin; she has been with you since the month of Rajab." [TN: Miranji, Madar and Khwaja Muinuddin are popular names for the fourth, fifth and sixth months of the Muslim year, from the saints' festivals held in them.]

Asghari asked, "What did Fazilat learn at Maiji's?"

Safihan said, "In three months, the *sipara* beginning 'Wal-muhsanat' and half of 'La yuhibbu-llah'." [TN: the fifth and sixth parts of the Qur'an, named by their opening words.]

Asghari said, "A *sipara* and a half in three months makes half a *sipara* a month. Here your Fazilat has been since Rajab, and now the moon of Khali is rising: that is four months [TN: Khali is the popular name for the eleventh month]. The *sipara* beginning 'Wa ma ubarri'u nafsi' was finished yesterday [TN: the thirteenth part]; that is, she has read seven and a half *siparas*, which by reckoning comes to nearly one *sipara* a month, double what she did at Maiji's school. And when Fazilat came here, she could not even draw a black line; now she writes her name, and for her age her letters are not bad. She could not count properly even up to twenty; now she is learning the fifteen times table. In sewing she could not even do a straight running stitch; now look at the backstitch she does. Bring it, Aqila, just bring the little bundle; show her the backstitching Fazilat did on the shirt; and if there is any of Fazilat's *kikari*, *murmura*, sprigs, waves, stripes, *khana-tor*, *dekhat-bhuli*, outline work, thread-counted work, jasmine net, *tirpan* creeper or *kamdani* [TN: names of embroidery stitches and patterns], bring that too."

Fazilat said, "Teacher, I'll go and fetch it."

Fazilat ran off and brought her embroidery. Safihan, hearing ten answers to her one remark, was left dumbfounded.

Asghari said, "Tell me, my dear, is there any fairness in it? What more could your daughter have learned in four months?"

Safihan was so ashamed that it was as if pitchers of water had been poured over her; now she could not look the teacher in the face.

Because of the wretched Safihan's coming, Mahmuda's lovely story was left unfinished; all the girls began glaring at her. Safihan said, "Teacher, how was I to know any of this? Fazilat stays here all day; at night she goes home so late that she eats and falls asleep; I never get a chance to ask her anything. The two or four times I happened to come this way, I found her once playing dolls, once cooking in toy pots, once listening to stories; that made me think she was wasting her time in play. Now the words have slipped out of my mouth; please forgive me."

Asghari said, "Certainly your suspicion was not unreasonable; but it is through these very games that I teach them useful things. In the toy-pot cooking the girls learn how to make every kind of dish: the right amount of spice, the guess for salt, how to judge a flavour, how to recognise a smell, they know it all. Tell me, Fazilat, the day before yesterday was Friday; how much sweet saffron rice did you girls cook together? Tell us how you made it and all the accounts."

Fazilat said, "Mahmuda Begum has written the accounts in her book, but as you told us, I watched the method with great attention and understood it well. There was a *ser* of rice; first we soaked it in a basin. I think we sent for half a pice worth of *harsinghar* stalks [TN: the coral-jasmine, whose stalks give an orange dye], and got a pice worth; we boiled them in about a *ser* and a half of water; when it had come to the boil and the colour had come out, we strained it, squeezed out the rice and put it into the liquid. When the rice was half cooked and only the hard core of the grain remained, we spread it on a cloth so that all the water drained off. Then we heated half a quarter-*ser* of ghee in the pot with cloves till it crackled, and put in the rice; on top we put raw sugar equal in weight to the rice, and by guesswork as much water as would soften the core of the grain that was left. Then we fried a *chhatank* of raisins in ghee, and when they had swelled, we added them to the rice, put live coals above and below, and left it to steam."

Asghari said, "The method is right; but when I saw the rice it had gone mushy; it seems you did not rinse it in cold water after spreading it on the cloth."

Then Asghari turned to Safihan and said, "Well, my dear, didn't your daughter cook the sweet rice properly? All this is thanks to the toy-pot cooking. Mahmuda dear, read out the account of your sweet rice."

Mahmuda went and brought the account book, and said, "Teacher: rice at six *sers* to the rupee, one *ser*, two and three-quarter annas; a pice worth of stalks and cloves; ghee at two *sers* to the rupee, three-quarters of a quarter-*ser* bought, half a quarter-*ser* put in for the frying and a *chhatank* for crisping the raisins at the steaming, that was one and a half annas of ghee; sugar at four *sers* to the rupee, one *ser*, four annas; a pice worth of raisins: in all, ten and three-quarter annas spent. Ten girls shared in it; one and three-quarter annas were mine; and Fazilat one, Aqila two, Husn-ara three, Amatullah four, Aliya five, Salma six, Ummunnabin seven, Shakila and Jamila, the two sisters, nine: an anna each." [TN: the items as listed actually add up to eight annas and nine pies, not ten annas and nine pies.]

Asghari said, "Mahmuda, you have been cheated."

Mahmuda thought, and said, "Yes, teacher, there are cowries owing on the rice; that wretched grain-seller swallowed them. Oh dear, if the stalks and cloves had been included in it, a pice would have been saved. Diyanat, go and get the cowries from the grain-seller." [TN: cowrie shells were used as the smallest change.]

Asghari said, "What! What are you doing? A matter of cowries, from the day before yesterday! Don't say anything now; this loss is the penalty for your mistake."

Asghari turned to Husn-ara and said, "Now we know how the sweet rice was made and what it cost; but tell me, what did you all do with the potful, a *ser* of sweet rice?"

Husn-ara answered, "Two middling plates, heaped high, we sent to the mosque in the name of God; the rest filled thirteen saucers. There are twenty-five of us girls in the school; each two shared a saucer, and in the thirteenth saucer I was alone."

Asghari asked, "What, did you take a double share?"

Husn-ara said, "No, my saucer was half; ask anyone."

Asghari said, "Then why did you keep apart from the company?"

Husn-ara was silent. Amatullah said, "Teacher, she finds it disgusting to eat with everyone."

Husn-ara said, "No, teacher, it's not a matter of disgust; I came to the cloth after all the other girls, and so I was left alone; ask Mahmuda Begum."

Amatullah said, "Why! Weren't you quarrelling just a little while ago about drinking my leftover water?"

Husn-ara said, "Did I quarrel, or did I only say that you should take as much water as you are thirsty for? Isn't it bad manners to leave water you've drunk from in the glass?"

Then Asghari asked Mahmuda, "That booklet, *Alwan-e-Ni'mat* (Varieties of Delicacies), which I gave you: have you all tried cooking every dish in it, or not yet?"

Mahmuda thought a little and said, "As far as I know, I have had them all cooked, and some of them several times. All the bigger girls know how to make the ordinary everyday dishes. Besides these, every kind of kebab, skewered, of fillets, *shami* and meatball; *koftas*, *pulao*, sweet saffron rice, *mutanjan*, raw-meat *biryani*, *nur-mahali*, *qorma*, sweet and savoury *samosas*, *qalami* fritters, curd fritters, *suhal*, vermicelli, lentils fried in ghee, *kachauris*, *papads*, *burani*, *firni*, *sohan* halwa, *peras*, rice pudding and *andarsa* balls [TN: names of dishes, sweets and snacks]: all have been cooked again and again; and all the girls have watched them cooked, and indeed cooked them with their own hands. And you know that in our school it is toy-pot cooking only in name; whatever is cooked is cooked in quantity enough for a whole family. And Husn-ara is very fond of chutneys and preserves; apart from her, the other girls know little about these."

After this Asghari said to Safihan, "My dear, now you will have seen the use of the toy-pot cooking here. It is late in the night, and some of the girls live far away; if you come tomorrow, we'll show you the dolls, and if you stay till evening, we'll let you hear the stories too."

Everyone took leave. As she was going, Safihan joined her hands before Asghari and said, "Teacher, for God's sake, forgive my fault."
